Attribution allocates credit; experiments identify causation

Attribution is a bookkeeping rule that assigns each conversion to an ad touchpoint (a click or an impression), usually because billing or optimization needs a single owner; in mobile app advertising the winner takes all, while multi-touch rules split credit fractionally but answer the same bookkeeping question. The common rule is last-touch: credit goes to the last qualifying click within a lookback window before the conversion. A click-through window bounds the gap between click and conversion; a view-through window, usually much shorter, lets an impression with no click claim the conversion when no qualifying click exists. Typical defaults for app installs are 7 days for clicks and 24 hours for views, but contracts and measurement settings vary. For mobile apps the matching is done by an MMP (Mobile Measurement Partner; see Chapter 2): the advertiser's app embeds the MMP's SDK, which reports installs and in-app events; ad networks send the MMP their click and impression claims; the MMP picks the winner under the configured rules and sends postbacks (server-to-server notifications of the attributed event) to the winning network.

  1. t = 0
    Impression. The ad renders; the network sends the MMP an impression claim (device identifier where permitted, otherwise coarser signals).
  2. ≤ 24 h
    View-through window. If no qualifying click exists, an install within 24 hours of the impression can be credited to it.
  3. click
    Click. The user taps; the network sends a click claim and opens the app store.
  4. ≤ 7 d
    Install = first open. The MMP SDK reports the first launch (not the download). The MMP looks back 7 days for clicks and credits the last one.
  5. minutes
    Install postback to the winning network; losing networks typically receive nothing about this install.
  6. later
    In-app events. Purchases inherit the install's attribution and trigger further postbacks; the cohort cutoff (e.g. day-7 revenue) is a reporting choice.

Incrementality is a different question: how do business outcomes with the advertising intervention compare with outcomes without it? The two can disagree in both directions. A real conversion can be credited to the wrong touchpoint, and a correctly credited conversion may have happened anyway. Last-touch and view-through rules systematically reward ads placed close to purchases users were already going to make: retargeting existing customers, or spraying cheap impressions to collect view-through credit.

Attribution answers

Which touchpoint gets credit for this conversion? A rule can assign credit per conversion. Availability and revisions depend on the measurement window, reporting delays and the attribution method. It drives CPA and CPI (cost per install) billing and daily optimization signals. It is silent on whether the conversion would have happened anyway.

Incrementality answers

How many conversions exist because the ad ran? Defined only for a population against a counterfactual, needs randomization or strong assumptions, arrives after the window matures, can be negative, and comes with a confidence interval. It decides whether spend is profitable.

Field evidence shows that rich observational data does not reliably recover the effects that randomized experiments identify. Observational models are not useless, but predictive accuracy does not supply the identifying assumptions a causal claim needs. For a causal comparison based on observed exposures, you would need comparable exposed and unexposed users at each combination of logged features. You would also need those features to capture every factor that affects both exposure and the outcome. Ad targeting often uses relevant signals the analyst cannot observe. Gordon et al., observational methods versus large Facebook experiments

The next example is self-contained; it is not the network campaign from Chapter 1, so that network's media budget does not enter the advertiser's return calculation. Randomly split one eligible population into a treatment group, where the campaign may deliver, and a control group (a holdout), where it may not. Net revenue per purchase means sales revenue after refunds and other adjustments but before cost of goods, fulfillment and other variable costs. The pre-advertising contribution rate is the share of net revenue left after those non-advertising variable costs. ROAS (return on ad spend) is revenue divided by ad spend; attributed ROAS uses attributed revenue, and iROAS (incremental ROAS) uses the revenue the campaign actually caused. Assume randomization and event definitions are correct, the window has matured, and there is no interference: one group's treatment does not change the other group's purchases.

Worked example

Holdout experiment: from attributed ROAS to a decision

Given: 100,000 users per group. Over the same matured window, treatment makes 1,200 purchases and control 1,000. Ad spend is $5,000, all in treatment. Attribution credits 500 purchases to the campaign. Net revenue is $50 per purchase and the contribution rate 40%, so each purchase contributes \$50\times0.40=\$20 before ad spend. For the standard error: each user buys at most once and users are independent.

  1. Attributed ROAS: 500\times\$50\div\$5{,}000=5.
  2. Lift (equal group sizes, so counts compare directly): 1{,}200-1{,}000=200 incremental purchases per 100,000 users assigned to treatment.
  3. iROAS: 200\times\$50\div\$5{,}000=2. Each incremental revenue dollar yields $0.40 of contribution, so break-even iROAS is 1\div0.40=2.5.
  4. Incremental contribution after ad spend: 200\times\$20-\$5{,}000=-\$1{,}000. Break-even lift: \$5{,}000\div\$20=250 purchases.
  5. Credit that was not incremental: at the point estimate, at least 500-200=300 credited purchases would have happened without the campaign (assuming the ad never suppresses a purchase).
  6. Standard error of the lift. Under the stated independent-user model, each count is binomial. Estimate its variance with n\hat p(1-\hat p): 100{,}000\times0.012\times0.988=1{,}185.6 for treatment and 100{,}000\times0.010\times0.990=990 for control, so \text{SE}=\sqrt{1{,}185.6+990}=\sqrt{2{,}175.6}=46.6. The lift is 200/46.6=4.3 standard errors from zero.
  7. 95% interval: 200\pm1.96\times46.6=200\pm91.4, i.e. [108.6,\ 291.4]\approx[109,\ 291] purchases.
  8. Map through the linear formulas: iROAS =\text{lift}\times\$50/\$5{,}000 gives [1.09,\ 2.91]; contribution =\$20\times\text{lift}-\$5{,}000 gives [-\$2{,}828,\ +\$828]\approx[-\$2{,}830,\ +\$830].
  9. Decision. Break-even requires 250 additional purchases. That threshold lies within the interval, so this sample leaves the sign of profit unresolved.

Result: the campaign causes purchases (lift 200, 95% CI [109, 291]); its profit is −$1,000 at the point estimate with 95% CI ≈ [−$2,830, +$830]. Attributed ROAS is 5. The experiment estimates a loss, with an interval that also allows a gain.

Check: both ROAS figures equal purchases × $50/$5,000 = purchases/100, so their ratio is 500/200 = 2.5. Contribution also equals \$5{,}000\times(0.40\times\text{iROAS}-1): at iROAS 1.086 that is −$2,828, matching step 8.

Repeat purchases, heavy-tailed order values, or cluster randomization widen these intervals. If the two groups differ in size, compare per-person outcomes first, then state the delivery scale you are extrapolating to.

Assignment to "eligible to see the ad" is not exposure: many treatment users never win an auction slot, never open the app, or scroll past. Use potential-outcome notation. For user i, Z_i\in\{0,1\} is the randomized assignment (in this section Z is not the competing bid of Chapter 3), D_i(z)\in\{0,1\} is whether the user would actually be exposed under assignment z, and Y_i(z) is the outcome under assignment z. Under the exclusion restriction below, write Y_i(d) for the outcome under exposure d. Because the control group cannot be served the campaign, D_i(0)=0 for everyone (one-sided noncompliance). Let c=P(D=1\mid Z=1) be the exposure rate in the treatment group.

Estimand Definition Estimator Requires
ITT (intent-to-treat effect) \tau_{\text{ITT}}=\mathbb{E}[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)], effect of assignment over everyone randomized \bar Y_{Z=1}-\bar Y_{Z=0} Randomization; no interference between users (SUTVA)
TOT (treatment on the treated) = CACE (complier average causal effect) \tau_{\text{TOT}}=\mathbb{E}[Y_i(d{=}1)-Y_i(d{=}0)\mid D_i(1)=1], effect of exposure on users who would be exposed if assigned (\bar Y_{Z=1}-\bar Y_{Z=0})/\hat c (Wald / Bloom ratio) ITT assumptions, plus exclusion restriction (assignment affects outcomes only through exposure) and c>0; one-sided noncompliance makes TOT and CACE coincide and makes monotonicity automatic
Naive exposed-vs-control \mathbb{E}[Y\mid D=1,Z=1]-\mathbb{E}[Y\mid Z=0] Mean of exposed treatment users minus mean of all control users Nothing valid: it equals \tau_{\text{TOT}}+\big(\mathbb{E}[Y_i(0)\mid D_i(1)=1]-\mathbb{E}[Y_i(0)]\big), and the selection term is positive when delivery targets likely buyers

Analyze ITT first. Filtering the treatment group down to exposed users and comparing them with all of control reintroduces exactly the selection that randomization removed. Switching the denominator alone identifies nothing; the TOT rescaling is valid only under the stated assumptions. The Wald ratio also adds no precision: it divides the estimate and its standard error by the same \hat c, so the z-statistic is unchanged. TOT changes the unit (per exposed user instead of per assigned user), not the total effect, the evidence or the profit. Precision needs extra information about who would have been exposed, which is what ghost ads supply.

Worked example

ITT, TOT and the naive comparison with exposure logs

Given: the holdout experiment above, plus hypothetical exposure data. In treatment, 40,000 users were exposed and bought 800 times; the other 60,000 bought 400 times. Ghost-ad logs (described below) flag the 40,000 control users who would have been exposed; they bought 600 times, and the other 60,000 control users bought 400 times.

  1. ITT per assigned user: (1{,}200-1{,}000)/100{,}000=0.002 purchases, SE 46.64/100{,}000=0.0004664.
  2. Exposure rate \hat c=40{,}000/100{,}000=0.40. Wald: \tau_{\text{TOT}}=0.002/0.40=0.005 purchases per exposed user, SE \approx0.0004664/0.40=0.001166 (accounting for the sampling error of \hat c changes this variance by about 0.1%). z=0.005/0.001166=4.3, the same as ITT.
  3. Totals agree: 100{,}000\times0.002=40{,}000\times0.005=200 purchases, so iROAS is still 2.
  4. Naive comparison: 800/40{,}000-1{,}000/100{,}000=0.020-0.010=0.010 per exposed user, i.e. 400 "incremental" purchases, twice the truth. The selection term is visible in the ghost logs: would-be-exposed control users buy at 600/40{,}000=0.015 against 0.010 overall, a gap of 0.005, exactly the bias.
  5. Ghost-ad TOT: exposed treatment vs ghost-exposed control, 0.020-0.015=0.005. Its standard error is \sqrt{800(0.98)+600(0.985)}/40{,}000 =\sqrt{1{,}375}/40{,}000=0.000927, so z=0.005/0.000927=5.4. The variance ratio (0.001166/0.000927)^2=1.58 is worth 58% more users, because the 120,000 never-exposed users only added noise.
  6. Exclusion check: never-exposed users buy 400/60,000 in both groups, consistent with assignment having no effect except through exposure. Without ghost logs this is untestable.

Result: TOT = 0.005 purchases per exposed user by both methods; the naive estimate (0.010) is 2× too high; ghost ads cut the standard error by 20.5%.

Check: \hat c\times\tau_{\text{TOT}}=0.40\times0.005=0.002=\tau_{\text{ITT}}, and naive − TOT = 0.010 − 0.005 = 0.005 = selection term.

The exclusion restriction needs care in an auction. Withholding the campaign from a control user does not leave the slot empty; the next-best ad wins. The estimand is therefore the effect relative to the ad environment the user would otherwise see, not relative to no ad at all. Ghost ads (Johnson, Lewis and Nubbemeyer, 2017) estimate TOT more precisely. For control users the ad server runs the auction with the campaign included, logs a "ghost impression" whenever the campaign would have won and rendered, then serves the runner-up instead. This observes D_i(1) in both groups, so you can compare exposed treatment users with ghost-exposed control users directly. It requires the ghost logic to replicate the real delivery decision exactly, with identical code paths in both groups. The older placebo design, which shows control users a public-service ad (PSA), fails under optimized delivery: the optimizer delivers the PSA to different users than it would deliver the real ad, so the two exposed groups are not comparable.

Model experiments and channel incrementality experiments are not the same question

A model A/B experiment randomizes traffic between two delivery policies and compares them. A channel incrementality experiment compares advertising with not advertising, with reduced spend, or with different budget levels. The first can show that B beats A; it cannot by itself show that the channel generates positive incremental profit relative to not spending at all. Geo experiments, which randomize geographic regions instead of users, also come in several designs (go dark, heavy-up, budget levels), so choose the question first and the design second. Google GeoX experiment types

Before launch, fix the primary metric, the minimum detectable effect (MDE: the smallest true effect the design detects with a prespecified power at a prespecified false-positive rate), the duration, the label-maturity period, guardrail metrics (metrics that must not degrade, such as latency, user retention or publisher revenue) and the stopping rule. Several mechanisms couple the arms. A shared budget makes them compete for one quota. Bids from one arm move the prices the other arm pays. Cross-device and cross-region behavior leaks users across assignment boundaries. Whether you randomize by user, region or time block, document how each of these interferences is handled. Deciding to stop whenever a peek shows significance inflates the actual false-positive rate unless the test is designed for sequential monitoring.

In the running example the network optimizes its gross spread (collections minus media procurement cost; see Chapter 3), while the advertiser cares about incremental contribution. Report both. Otherwise a policy can raise the network's short-term revenue by chasing conversions that are easy to attribute but not incremental, and leave the resulting customer churn to the sales team.

Privacy and anti-fraud change observability

On iOS, two separate Apple mechanisms matter. ATT (App Tracking Transparency, iOS 14.5, 2021) requires an app to obtain user permission through a system prompt before it tracks the user across other companies' apps and websites or reads the IDFA (Identifier for Advertisers, the per-device ad identifier that previously enabled deterministic user-level attribution). SKAdNetwork (SKAN), and its successor AdAttributionKit (iOS 17.4), are Apple's privacy-preserving attribution APIs: the device itself decides which ad gets credit for an install and sends the ad network a delayed, aggregated postback with no user identifier. ATT and these attribution APIs are not synonyms. Apple's documentation states that using AdAttributionKit alone does not require the ATT prompt, while tracking as Apple defines it, or reading the IDFA, does. Crowd-anonymity thresholds control which postback fields are populated: with too few installs from a campaign, fields such as the fine-grained conversion value (an app-set integer from 0 to 63 encoding post-install activity) or parts of the source identifier come back coarse or empty. Postbacks are cryptographically signed and carry transaction identifiers so receivers can reject forgeries and replays. Apple ad attribution

The timing alone reshapes the training pipeline. In SKAdNetwork 4 (AdAttributionKit keeps the same three-window structure), a postback arrives only after its conversion window closes plus a random delay:

  1. day 0
    Engagement and install. The install must follow a StoreKit-rendered ad tap within 30 days or a view-through impression within 24 hours.
  2. days 0–2
    Window 1. The app may raise its conversion value; its postback can carry the fine value (0–63) if the crowd is large enough, otherwise a coarse value (low, medium, high). Postback 1 is sent 24–48 hours after the window closes.
  3. days 3–7
    Window 2. Coarse value only. Postback 24–144 hours after close.
  4. days 8–35
    Window 3. Coarse value only. The last postback can arrive as late as day 41 (35 days + 144 hours).

The system consequences follow directly. A missing user-level postback cannot be labeled "not converted." Aggregated postbacks cannot be split back into individual users without additional assumptions. A signed event proves authenticity, not incrementality. And missingness is not random, because it depends on consent rates and campaign size. A new platform that subsidizes impression purchases has bought delivery opportunities only. It does not automatically obtain training labels that can be joined across customers and used under long-term consent.

Examine fraud layer by layer, because each layer is a separate attack surface. Is the supply identity real? Was the impression actually rendered? Was the click made by a human? Is the conversion valid? Was attribution hijacked, for example by click injection, where malware on an Android device detects that an app is being installed and fires a fake click before its first open, winning last-touch credit for an install that was already happening? Was the purchase later refunded? Supply-chain transparency standards address only the first layer. ads.txt (app-ads.txt for apps) is a file the publisher hosts on its own domain listing which ad systems are authorized to sell its inventory. sellers.json is published by each SSP (supply-side platform; see Chapter 2) or exchange and lists the sellers it pays. The SupplyChain object in an OpenRTB bid request (OpenRTB is the IAB's standard bid-request protocol) lists every hop the request passed through. None of these guarantees traffic quality or economic incrementality. IAB ads.txt, IAB sellers.json and SupplyChain FAQ

The trading analogy applies: positive expected value per trade does not guarantee survival. Correlated fake events, retroactive changes to attribution windows, chargebacks, and a payment-timing mismatch, where the network pays publishers before advertisers pay it, all concentrate risk. A fraud model is one layer. You also need per-source exposure limits, settlement reconciliation, contractual clawback rights, and gradual ramp-up of new sources.
