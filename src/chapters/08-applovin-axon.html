What public sources confirm about the products

Vocabulary first. AppLovin is the ad-tech company under discussion (Chapter 1). A publisher is an app developer that sells ad slots inside its app; an advertiser buys them; an impression is one ad display. An SDK (software development kit) is a library compiled into the app; ad networks reach in-app inventory through SDKs. Mediation software sits in the publisher's app and decides, for each ad request, which of many demand sources fills it (Chapter 2). Under a waterfall, mediation calls ad networks one after another in a configured priority order; under in-app bidding, networks submit real-time bids for the same request and the highest bid wins. A DSP (demand-side platform) is a buyer's system that bids on behalf of advertisers. MAX is AppLovin's mediation product, which the company calls its in-app bidding solution. Axon is AppLovin's ad prediction and bidding engine (the filings call it a "recommendation engine" or "recommendation system"). The advertiser-facing product it powers is called Axon Ads Manager in the 2025 Form 10-K and AppLovin Ads in the Q3 2026 Form 10-Q; this chapter says AppLovin Ads.

Public fact. AppLovin describes Axon as the AI advertising engine that powers AppLovin Ads. AppLovin bids on each impression according to the value Axon assigns to it for a given advertiser, aiming at that advertiser's return-on-ad-spend (ROAS) goal. The 2025 10-K and the Q3 2026 10-Q both say this product generates substantially all of AppLovin's revenue; the 10-K adds that revenue from other services "was not material". Axon official description, AppLovin 2025 10-K, AppLovin Q3 2026 10-Q

Public fact. MAX's partner documentation describes a "unified first-price auction" in which the highest bidder wins, and its OpenRTB specification says bidders are expected "to pay the CPM amount they bid in full". DSPs (demand-side platforms) bidding through AppLovin Exchange integrate via OpenRTB 2.5, the IAB Tech Lab's real-time bidding protocol; ad networks bid from inside the app through bid tokens generated by their own SDKs. Non-bidding networks enter the same auction at a CPM price that the publisher types in or that auto-CPM sets daily from the network's reported CPM over the last seven days, weighting recent days more. The documented sequence for one ad request: MAX auction flow, AppLovin OpenRTB specification, bid responses and notices, auto-CPM

  1. Launch
    SDK start-up. When the user opens the app, MAX initializes every eligible network SDK.
  2. Request
    Ad request. The app asks for an ad. MAX collects bid tokens from the SDK bidders and sends the request to the MAX ad server.
  3. ≤ tmax
    Bids. The auction server sends bid requests to SDK bidders and to DSPs in AppLovin Exchange. Only bids returned within the timeout tmax count; the page gives no value.
  4. Resolve
    Unified auction. Bids are merged with the publisher's waterfall networks at their CPM prices; the highest CPM wins. Bidders whose bids are invalid (below the floor, missing or zero) get a loss notice now.
  5. Load
    Win and loss notices. The winner's ad is loaded. If the load fails, the winner gets a loss notice. Other bidders get their loss notice after a different winning ad loads.
  6. Render
    Billing. When the ad renders (video starts, or a graphic is displayed), MAX calls the winner's billing URL. The spec tells DSPs to track impressions and spend on this call.

The two pages disagree slightly on when the win notice fires: the auction-flow page says after a successful load, the OpenRTB page says when the auction completes and the ad is sent to the device. Both put billing at render.

Public fact. The notices carry prices. ${AUCTION_PRICE}, the final price, is substituted into both the win URL and the loss URL, "so every bidder (win or loss) sees the final paid price". The winner also receives ${AUCTION_MINIMUM_BID_TO_WIN}, defined as the second-highest bid. A loss notice carries an OpenRTB loss-reason code. MAX notice macros

Worked example · hypothetical numbers

One MAX auction: who earns what

Given, for one ad request (prices in dollars CPM unless marked per impression): publisher floor $3.00; waterfall network W entered at $5.50. Bids: AppLovin Ads $7.50, SDK bidder X $6.80, DSP Y $6.00, DSP Z $2.00. AppLovin Ads bids for an advertiser who pays a=\$30 per conversion, with conversion probability q=0.0005 per impression, so value v=qa=\$0.015 per impression; $7.50 CPM is the running example's first-price bid v/2 (Chapter 3). Let \varphi=5\% be the MAX fee on winning auction spend; MAX's real fee rate is not disclosed.

  1. Filter. Z's $2.00 is below the $3.00 floor, so Z gets a loss notice during the auction.
  2. Rank by CPM: $7.50 (AppLovin Ads) > $6.80 (X) > $6.00 (Y) > $5.50 (W). AppLovin Ads wins and, under first price, pays its bid: b=\$7.50\ \text{CPM}=\$7.50/1000=\$0.0075\ \text{per impression}.
  3. Notices. The winner learns the second-highest bid, $6.80, so it paid $7.50 − $6.80 = $0.70 CPM more than the minimum needed to win: the cost of bidding without seeing rivals. X and Y each receive AUCTION_PRICE = $7.50 in their loss notices, so each learns exactly the price it would have had to beat.
  4. Render and bill. The billing URL fires at render; the publisher is credited $0.0075 for this impression. Had the load failed, the winner would have received a loss notice and no billing call.
  5. AppLovin's revenue when its own bidder wins. AppLovin is an agent, so it books the advertiser charge minus the publisher payment (see below). Per impression, in expectation over the conversion: v-b=\$0.015-\$0.0075=\$0.0075.
  6. Counterfactual: X wins at the same $7.50. AppLovin earns only the MAX fee: \varphi\,b=0.05\times\$0.0075=\$0.000375\ \text{per impression}.

Result: the publisher earns $7.50 per 1,000 such impressions whoever wins; AppLovin expects $7.50 per 1,000 when its own bidder wins and $0.375 when a third party wins at the same price, a factor of 20.

Check: (v-b)/(\varphi b)=0.0075/0.000375=20. The winning price ($7.50) is at least the second bid ($6.80), as a highest-bid-wins auction requires; a loss notice showing AUCTION_PRICE below the loser's own valid bid would break that rule.

In this hypothetical comparison the auctioneer receives more when its own bidder wins. The direction depends on both margins. At a fixed publisher payment b, the own-bidder contribution exceeds the third-party fee only if v-b>\varphi b. The example isolates a possible incentive. Auction behavior requires separate evidence.

Do not generalize this sequence. It is MAX's interface semantics. OpenRTB has analogous win, loss and billing callbacks (nurl, lurl, burl), but each exchange decides when they fire and what they commit the buyer to (Chapter 5). MAX's choices do not redefine those terms for other OpenRTB integrations.

Public fact. The 10-K states that AppLovin is an agent, not a principal, in its advertising arrangements: it does not control the inventory before it passes to the advertiser and is not primarily responsible for delivering it. Under US GAAP an agent reports revenue net, so reported revenue is advertiser payments minus amounts paid or payable to publishers. Revenue is recognized when an impression is delivered (impression-based arrangements) or when a specified action such as a click or install occurs (action-based arrangements). The price is set dynamically from the advertiser's campaign goals, not as a fixed price per impression or per action. Reported revenue was $5,480.7 million in 2025 (up 70.0% from $3,224.1 million in 2024) and $2,184.8 million in Q3 2026 (up 55.5% from $1,405.0 million in Q3 2025); both exclude the divested Apps business. Inference. Because revenue is net, you cannot recover total advertiser spend, and therefore cannot compute the platform's take rate (amount retained as a share of advertiser spend, Chapter 2), from reported revenue alone. In the worked example the advertiser's expected charge is $15 per 1,000 impressions and AppLovin's revenue is $7.50; a filing shows only the second kind of number. Likewise, "MAX mediation is free for publishers" on the MAX website does not mean MAX is free to everyone in the chain. The same FAQ says bidding networks pay AppLovin a fee on the ad revenue they generate; the 10-K says MAX revenue is "a percentage of client spend", and the Q3 2026 10-Q says "a percentage of winning auction spend". Since revenue outside AppLovin Ads is immaterial, MAX's value to AppLovin lies mainly in the supply and the auction position it provides, not in its fees. What every MAX bidder learns (public fact)

The bid request for each auction it is invited to. For each of its own bids: win or loss, a loss-reason code, and the final price AUCTION_PRICE. If it wins, the second-highest bid. A billing call when its ad renders.

What only the auctioneer sees

The bids submitted to MAX for an auction, along with which invited bidders did not submit a bid. The auctioneer also sees waterfall CPM prices, floors and other publisher settings; load and render outcomes for all networks. AppLovin says Axon does not ingest other bidders' bids or other bidders' user data; whether the pipeline complies is unknown.

The final auction price and the competitive threshold are different fields in a first-price auction. On a win, the final price is the bidder's own bid. The documented minimum-to-win field is the relevant signal for the winning bidder. A price in a valid outbid loss notice can help estimate competition. Its interpretation depends on the loss reason and the auction's eligibility, floor and waterfall rules.

How a prediction advantage becomes retained profit

Start from a standard result: for a single decision-maker, the optimal value of extra information is nonnegative. Let \Pi(\mathcal I) be the set of feasible policies (same objective, actions and budget constraints) that are measurable with respect to information \mathcal I. Then

\mathcal I_{\text{old}}\subseteq\mathcal I_{\text{new}}\;\Rightarrow\;\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{old}})\subseteq\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{new}})\;\Rightarrow\;\max_{\pi\in\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{new}})}\mathbb E[u(\pi)]\ \ge\ \max_{\pi\in\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{old}})}\mathbb E[u(\pi)].

The new decision-maker can always ignore the extra information and replay the old policy. The result needs every one of these premises:

  • Competitors' strategies and the distribution of clearing prices stay fixed.
  • The objective, the action set and constraints such as budget are unchanged.
  • Acquiring and using the information costs nothing.
  • The new information is available at decision time and refines the old (the old signal can be recovered from the new one).
  • Conditional valuations are accurate, and the policy actually optimizes on them.

A higher offline AUC (ranking quality on held-out logs) establishes none of this. It does not show that the new information set contains the old one, that probabilities are calibrated, or that decisions are optimal. Still less does it show that the gain survives once the market reacts. The next two examples hold the market fixed and isolate two properties: refinement (telling opportunities apart) and calibration (getting the probability level right).

Worked example · hypothetical numbers

A sharper model: same win count, more profit

Given: N=1{,}000{,}000 requests; highest competing bid Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) with L=\$0.02 per impression, unaffected by our bids; a=\$30 per conversion; first price; no budget cap. Half the requests have q_H=0.0008, half q_L=0.0002 (mean 0.0005). The old model cannot tell them apart and predicts 0.0005 everywhere; the new model sees the segment. Both bid b=\hat v/2, the uniform-threshold optimum (Chapter 3), where \hat v=\hat q\,a is the model's value estimate per impression.

  1. Values per impression: v_H=0.0008\times\$30=\$0.024, v_L=0.0002\times\$30=\$0.006; pooled \bar v=\$0.015.
  2. Old model: bid $0.0075 ($7.50 CPM) everywhere; win rate F=0.0075/0.02=37.5\%; 375,000 wins, 187,500 per segment. Spend 375{,}000\times\$0.0075=\$2{,}812.50. Conversions 187{,}500\times0.0008+187{,}500\times0.0002=150+37.5=187.5, worth 187.5\times\$30=\$5{,}625. Profit $2,812.50.
  3. New model, high segment: bid v_H/2=\$0.012 ($12 CPM), win rate 60%, 300,000 wins, spend $3,600, 240 conversions worth $7,200.
  4. New model, low segment: bid v_L/2=\$0.003 ($3 CPM), win rate 15%, 75,000 wins, spend $225, 15 conversions worth $450.
  5. New totals: 375,000 wins, spend $3,825, 255 conversions worth $7,650, profit $3,825.
  6. In general, profit per request at b=v/2 is v^2/(4L), so the gain from refinement is N\,\frac{\mathbb E[v^2]-\bar v^2}{4L}=N\,\frac{\operatorname{Var}(v)}{4L}=10^6\times\frac{0.009^2}{0.08}=\$1{,}012.50.

Result: win count unchanged at 375,000; profit +36% ($2,812.50 → $3,825); spend, which is publisher revenue, also +36%; average clearing price $7.50 → $10.20 CPM.

Check: $3,825 − $2,812.50 = $1,012.50, matching step 6. Cost per conversion is b/q=a/2=\$15 in every segment under both models: $2,812.50 / 187.5 = $3,825 / 255 = $15.

Win rate did not move, so it cannot be the metric that detects a better model. The gain came from moving wins out of the low segment into the high one.

Worked example · hypothetical numbers

A miscalibrated model: more volume, less margin

Given: the same market with a single segment, true q=0.0005, so v=\$0.015. The model predicts \hat q=r\,q with r=1.2: every probability is 20% too high. Multiplying all scores by a constant leaves ranking and AUC unchanged.

  1. Bid: b=r\,v/2=1.2\times\$0.015/2=\$0.009 ($9 CPM).
  2. Win rate: F=0.009/0.02=45\%, so 450,000 wins.
  3. Spend 450{,}000\times\$0.009=\$4{,}050; true value 450{,}000\times0.0005\times\$30=\$6{,}750; profit $2,700.
  4. In general, with r=1+\varepsilon: \begin{aligned}\text{profit}&=N\,\frac{v^2}{4L}\,r(2-r)=N\,\frac{v^2}{4L}\,(1-\varepsilon^2),\\ \text{spend}&=N\,\frac{v^2}{4L}\,r^2=N\,\frac{v^2}{4L}\,(1+\varepsilon)^2,\end{aligned} with Nv^2/(4L)=\$2{,}812.50.

Result: a 20% overprediction raises wins by 20% and spend (publisher revenue, and MAX's fee base) by 44%, while the bidder's media spread falls 4%.

Check: \$2{,}812.50\times(1-0.2^2)=\$2{,}700 and \$2{,}812.50\times1.2^2=\$4{,}050. Underpredicting by the same amount (r=0.8) costs the same $112.50 of profit but cuts spend to $1,800.

Inference. Suppose a new model finds more valuable opportunities. It can stop buying low-value impressions, and it can bid more for high-value ones. Bidding more raises publisher revenue. Competitors and publishers' floor prices (the minimum price a seller accepts, Chapter 3) keep adjusting, and advertisers may push for better pricing. How the resulting rent is split depends on whether the information is exclusive, whether access to it is stable, whether the model generalizes, whether customers have alternatives, and how the platform charges. Both "a model lead turns entirely into profit" and "auctions compete away every advantage" overstate the case.

To test for a moat, do not ask whether a competitor can build a similar model. Ask whether, on the same supply surface (the inventory one can bid on), with comparable data permissions, label maturity windows (how long one waits before treating a conversion label as final) and budget constraints, new advertisers and additional budget keep producing incremental contribution. Model, measurement, supply access, creatives, execution cost and customer relationships all have to enter the explanation together. Correct for survivorship bias too: a showcase of the surviving high-return customers proves nothing about the rest.

Model quality, access to data and auction treatment can all affect growth. To distinguish them, compare performance on matched supply and at the same budget, check which signals each bidder was entitled to use, and inspect valid outbid notices for inconsistencies with the documented auction rules. An advertiser holdout measures causal return for that campaign. Separating the causes of a measured return needs a comparison that varies the proposed mechanism.

Inference (generic, not a claim about AppLovin's practices). If a platform subsidizes advertisers, for example with credits or discounted pricing, the subsidy buys room for trial and error. It does not buy unbiased labels (the conversion and revenue events used as training targets). Suppose labels come only from subsidized customers, cheap traffic or high-intent retargeting (showing ads to users who have already interacted with the advertiser, and who would often convert anyway). Then the learned relationships may not transfer to the broader market once full pricing applies. The early metrics to check are customer retention after subsidies end, the quality of matured labels the platform is permitted to use, calibration within each segment, and the marginal profit curve. Event counts alone say little.

Three problems that attractive numbers hide

Definitions for this section. pCTR is the model's predicted click probability for an impression. Clearing CPM is 1,000 × media cost / impressions: what the buyer actually paid, per thousand impressions. Publisher eCPM is 1,000 × publisher revenue / impressions. A cohort is the set of users acquired by a campaign in a given period. Attributed revenue is revenue that the attribution rule credits to the ad; it is not the same as incremental revenue, the revenue that would not have occurred without the ad (Chapter 4). For a cohort C with acquisition spend S_C, where user u was acquired at time t_u,

\text{D7 ROAS}(C)=\frac{\sum_{u\in C}R_u\big([t_u,\;t_u+168\,\text{h})\big)}{S_C},

where R_u(\cdot) is attributed revenue from user u in the half-open window. Compute it only after every user's 7-day window has fully elapsed. It remains an attributed return, not an incremental one.

  1. Higher pCTR with flat ROAS. If pCTR rises, clearing CPM rises with it and D7 ROAS stays flat, the model may simply be buying more clickable traffic at a higher price. Decompose the change into price and traffic mix before declaring a win on pCTR or offline AUC.
  2. Higher publisher eCPM. If MAX publisher eCPM rises, publishers earn more per thousand impressions. Total publisher revenue also depends on impression volume. Neither number shows that advertisers gained matching incremental profit; in the miscalibration example above, publisher revenue rises 44% while the bidder's profit falls.
  3. Subsidized growth. If subsidies bring in more advertisers and conversions, but retention falls once full pricing applies, the subsidy may have bought a training distribution that cannot be reproduced.

For a would-be entrant, technical replicability is only one dimension. The questions that need evidence are: which labels you are permitted to obtain, which supply you can access, how you identify each customer's incrementality, under what constraints marginal returns stay positive, and how long those conditions last.

Takeaways

  • MAX is a documented first-price, highest-CPM-wins auction; billing fires at render; every bidder whose bid was evaluated receives the final price of the auction in its win or loss notice (whether rejected bids, e.g. below the floor, also receive it is not documented).
  • AppLovin books net revenue, almost all of it from AppLovin Ads. Advertiser spend and take rate are not disclosed, and MAX fees are immaterial to revenue.
  • The auctioneer earns far more when its own bidder wins than from its fee on a rival's win. Neutrality is the claim to test, and loss-notice prices let any bidder test it.
  • Refinement raises profit at a constant win count; miscalibration moves spend to first order and profit only to second order. Neither win rate nor publisher eCPM identifies a better model.
