Auctions and bidding: from advertiser value to a bid

In this chapter

  • Name the decision-maker, contract and objective first: under the running $30 CPA contract the advertiser should buy nothing.
  • Derive the win probability from the distribution of the highest competing bid: a $6 CPM bid wins 30% of auctions.
  • Derive the advertiser's best bid when it pays the auction price: $4 CPM in first price, $8 CPM in second price.
  • Treat a budget as a cap, not a target: $2,000 leaves money unspent, while $500 lowers the bids to $3.16 and $4.47 CPM.
  • Read the general rules: bid shading, the budget multiplier and pacing as a feedback loop.

The gist. An auction decides whose ad is shown and what the winner pays. A buyer must first know what an impression is worth to it under its contract; for an advertiser, that is the extra profit the ad causes. Then comes one trade-off: a higher bid wins more often, but in a first-price auction it also raises the price of every win, so the best bid sits below value. In a second-price auction the winner pays the runner-up's price, so bidding your value is best. A budget is a limit, not a goal; a sensible campaign can leave money unspent. Pacing is the feedback loop that keeps spending on track through the day.

Start with whose money and whose objective

The decision-maker here is the advertiser (or an agent buying for it), and its objective is incremental contribution after advertising costs. Contribution is revenue minus goods, fulfillment and other variable costs; “incremental” counts only sales the ads caused.

\text{Advertiser objective}=\text{incremental sales contribution}-\text{total advertising bill}.

Take the contract from Chapter 1: the advertiser pays a=\$30 per billable attributed order. Per successful impression, a billable order occurs with probability q=0.0005; the ad causes an extra order with probability \Delta q=0.0002; each extra order is worth g=\$40 of contribution. These are teaching inputs, fixed as the bid changes.

\begin{aligned} \text{Incremental contribution per impression}&=\Delta q\,g=0.0002\times40=\$0.008,\\ \text{Expected bill per impression}&=q\,a=0.0005\times30=\$0.015,\\ \text{Advertiser gain per impression}&=0.008-0.015=-\$0.007. \end{aligned}

With D expected impressions the advertiser gains D(\Delta q\,g-q\,a)=-0.007D, so its best choice is zero impressions. No bid fixes a contract that loses on every impression; renegotiating, raising causal lift or stopping come first. A reselling network keeps a different ledger, its spread (collections minus publisher payments), and can profit while the advertiser loses. The optional network-spread appendix works that case, including its $7.50 and $6.3246 CPM bids.

A separate worked contract: the advertiser pays the auction cost

To study a bid worth making, change the contract: the advertiser buys impressions directly and pays exactly the auction price, with no fee or markup. It no longer pays the $30 CPA; causal lift and contribution per order are unchanged. This is an analytical baseline, not AppLovin's contract.

Assumptions: prices are dollars per impression unless marked CPM (per 1,000 impressions); every win displays; no floor or fees; expected-value decisions; competitors ignore our bid; every opportunity has the same value, unrelated to competing bids; causal lift is known (Chapter 6 covers measuring it).

Why does a $6 CPM bid win 30% of opportunities?

We win when our bid beats the highest competing bid, so the win probability is the chance that this competing bid falls below ours. If competitors bid $3, $5 and $9 CPM, the price to beat is Z = $9 CPM: a $6 CPM bid loses and a $10 CPM bid wins. Z is called the competitive threshold. With no floor price (a seller's minimum price) and at least one competitor,

Z=\max\{\text{competing bids}\},\qquad \text{win}\iff b\ge Z.

Ties (probability zero below) go to us. The threshold decides whether we win; the auction rule decides what we pay.

Z varies across auctions and is unknown in advance, so model it as random: Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L), meaning equal-length price intervals in $0–20 CPM are equally likely, on every opportunity, whatever we bid. We win exactly when Z lands in [0,b], an interval of length b out of L. So the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of Z is the win probability:

F(b)=P(Z\le b)=\frac{b}{L},\qquad 0\le b\le L.

A $6 CPM bid wins against thresholds in $0–6 and loses against $6–20: 6/20 = 30% and 14/20 = 70%. For b\ge L, F=1, so bidding above L buys nothing.

Across N opportunities, linearity of expectation (no independence needed) gives expected wins W:

\mathbb E[W]=N\,F(b)=1{,}000{,}000\times0.30=300{,}000\ \text{wins}.
Optional: the density view, and what changes outside this model

The uniform threshold has density f(z)=1/L on [0,L], so F(b)=\int_0^b f(z)\,dz=b/L. In other markets the win probability is still F(b)=P(Z\le b), just not b/L: if 80% of thresholds lie below $6 CPM, a $6 CPM bid wins 80%. Uniform individual bids do not give a uniform threshold: the maximum of n independent \mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) bids has CDF (b/L)^n. Floor prices, discrete bid steps, varied opportunity types and competitors who react to us each need their own model. Correlation across opportunities leaves \mathbb E[W] unchanged and changes only the spread of the realized count: positive correlation widens it, negative correlation narrows it.

First-price bidding: maximize the advertiser's contribution after cost

In a first-price auction the winner pays its own bid, so a higher bid buys more wins but pays more on each; here the best bid is $4 CPM, half the $8 CPM value. A loss yields neither contribution nor cost. Let C_1(b) be expected ad spend and J_1(b) expected incremental contribution after spend:

C_1(b)=NF(b)\,b,\qquad J_1(b)=NF(b)\,(w-b).

w-b is the gain per won impression; NF(b) is the expected number won.

Substitute F(b)=b/L and complete the square:

J_1(b)=\frac NL\bigl(wb-b^2\bigr)=\frac NL\left[\left(\frac w2\right)^2-\left(b-\frac w2\right)^2\right].

The squared term is never negative, so J_1 is largest at b_0=w/2=\$0.004 ($4 CPM), where J_1=(N/L)(w/2)^2=\$800. (subscript 0: before the budget). J_1 falls as the bid moves away from w/2 in either direction. The halving comes from the uniform distribution, not from first price itself.

J_1'(b)=(N/L)(w-2b) is zero at b=w/2, and J_1''(b)=-2N/L<0, so J_1 is strictly concave and w/2 is its unique maximum on [0,L].

The budget restricts the choice; it does not require full spend

A budget removes bids that cost too much; if the best bid survives, the budget changes nothing and money stays unspent.

  1. Spend grows with the bid: C_1(b)=N\frac bL\,b=\frac{Nb^2}{L}.
  2. For b\ge0, C_1(b)\le B\iff b^2\le BL/N\iff b\le b_B=\sqrt{BL/N}. So the allowed bids are 0\le b\le\min\{b_B,L\}.
  3. J_1 falls with distance from w/2. If w/2 is allowed, pick it. If not, pick the allowed bid closest to w/2, which is the top of the allowed range.
b_1^*=\min\left\{\frac w2,\ \sqrt{\frac{BL}{N}},\ L\right\}.
Worked example

$2,000 cap versus $500 cap

  1. B=\$2{,}000: b_B=\sqrt{2000(0.02)/10^6}=\$0.0063246, above w/2. The cap is slack. The advertiser bids $4 CPM, spends 10^6(0.004)^2/0.02=\$800, leaves $1,200 unspent and earns $800 after cost.
  2. B=\$500: b_B=\sqrt{500(0.02)/10^6}=\$0.00316227766, below w/2. The cap is binding, so b_1^* = $3.1623 CPM. Expected impressions are 10^6\times0.00316227766/0.02 = 158,113.88, contribution before spend is 158,113.88 × $0.008 = $1,264.91, spend is exactly $500, and J_1=1264.911064-500=\$764.91.

Check: $764.91 is below the unconstrained $800, as a tighter constraint requires.

Second price: pay the competing bid, so bid your value

In an ideal second-price auction the winner pays the threshold Z, not its bid, so bidding the full value w is best. A $6 CPM bid against a $4 CPM competitor wins either way, but pays $6 in first price and $4 in second price.

The expected payment needs the average threshold given that we win. For 0\le z\le b,

P(Z\le z\mid Z\le b)=\frac{P(Z\le z)}{P(Z\le b)}=\frac{z/L}{b/L}=\frac zb,

so a winning threshold is uniform on [0,b] and averages b/2. Expected spend is wins times average price, and the square completes as before:

\begin{aligned}C_2(b)&=N\frac bL\cdot\frac b2=\frac{Nb^2}{2L},\\ J_2(b)&=\frac NL\left(wb-\frac{b^2}{2}\right)=\frac{N}{2L}\left[w^2-(b-w)^2\right],\\ b_2^*&=\min\left\{w,\ \sqrt{\frac{2BL}{N}},\ L\right\}.\end{aligned}

The budget step matches first price: Nb^2/(2L)\le B\iff b\le\sqrt{2BL/N}, and J_2 falls with distance from w. At B=\$2{,}000 the cap is \sqrt{8\times10^{-5}}=\$0.0089443, above w, so the advertiser bids w = $8 CPM. It wins 400,000 impressions worth 400{,}000\times0.008=\$3{,}200, spends $1,600 and keeps $1,600. At B=\$500 the cap \sqrt{2\times10^{-5}} binds: $4.4721 CPM, 223,606.80 impressions, $1,788.85 contribution, $500 spend, $1,288.85 after spend. This holds competitors fixed; real bidders react to a rule change.

Beyond uniform prices: advertiser bidding and pacing

In the one-slot model, a first-price bidder trades off a lower payment against fewer wins. A second-price bidder can bid its value. When a budget multiplier exists, it scales the value used to set both bids. Keep the direct-buying assumptions, but let F(b)=P(Z\le b) be any continuous win probability with density f=F'. Objectives are per opportunity.

Bid shading in first price

j_1(b)=(w-b)F(b),\qquad j_1'(b)=(w-b)f(b)-F(b)=f(b)\Bigl[w-b-\frac{F(b)}{f(b)}\Bigr].

Where f(b)>0, an interior optimum solves

b=w-\frac{F(b)}{f(b)}.

The gap F/f is bid shading: fewer wins, each cheaper. If F is log-concave (\log F is concave, as for the uniform and for the maximum of independent uniforms), this equation has at most one root, and that root is the best bid. With no root the best bid is a corner: zero if w-b-F/f\le0 throughout, and the top of the support if it is positive throughout (for the uniform, w\ge2L gives b=L, the L in the min above). For the uniform, F/f=b gives w/2. If the threshold is the higher of two independent \mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) bids, F=(b/L)^2, f=2b/L^2, F/f=b/2, so b=w-b/2 and b=2w/3=\$0.0053333 ($5.3333 CPM). Per million requests it earns 10^6(w/3)(2w/(3L))^2=\$189.63; applying the uniform rule w/2 instead earns 10^6(w/2)(w/(2L))^2=\$160.00, 15.6% less.

Optional: proof that log-concavity gives a single best bid

Assume F is differentiable with f>0 on (0,U), F(0)=0, F(U)=1, and \log F concave there. Then (\log F)'=f/F is nonincreasing, so F/f is nondecreasing, and h(b)=w-b-F(b)/f(b) is strictly decreasing. Since j_1'=f\,h with f>0, j_1' changes sign at most once, and only from positive to negative. So j_1 rises up to the root of h and falls after it, making the root the global maximum on [0,U]. If h\le0 everywhere, j_1 never rises and b=0 is best. If h\ge0 everywhere, j_1 never falls on [0,U] and b=U is best; for the uniform, U=L. Bids above U win no more than U and pay more, and bids above w give j_1\le0=j_1(0), so neither region can hold a better bid.

Truthful bidding in second price

j_2(b)=\int_0^b(w-z)f(z)\,dz,\qquad j_2'(b)=(w-b)f(b).

The derivative is nonnegative below w and nonpositive above it, so bidding w is optimal for every F. This covers one slot. Multi-slot auctions (GSP, generalized second price), quality-weighted bids, floors and fees need their own models. Some major exchanges use first price; Google Ad Exchange announced its move in 2019.

The advertiser's budget multiplier

A binding budget makes the advertiser bid as if each impression were worth w/(1+\mu_A) instead of w. The budget multiplier \mu_A\ge0 is zero when the budget is slack, otherwise just large enough that expected spend equals B. Uniform thresholds give b=w/[2(1+\mu_A)] in first price and b=w/(1+\mu_A) in second price.

It is also the budget's price: contribution after cost gained per extra budget dollar. At our $500 cap, first price gives \mu_A=w/(2b_B)-1=0.008/(2\times0.0031623)-1\approx0.2649, and indeed w/[2(1+\mu_A)]=b_B reproduces the $3.1623 CPM bid; a $501 cap adds about $0.26. Second price gives \mu_A=w/b_B-1\approx0.7889 with b_B=\sqrt{2BL/N}. At $2,000 both caps are slack and \mu_A=0.

Optional: deriving the multiplier as the marginal value of budget

At a binding first-price cap spend is exactly B, so the best value is J^*(B)=(N/L)\,w\,b_B-B with b_B=\sqrt{BL/N}. Using db_B/dB=b_B/(2B) and N/L=B/b_B^2:

\frac{dJ^*}{dB}=\frac NL\,w\,\frac{b_B}{2B}-1=\frac{w}{2b_B}-1.

Setting 1+\mu_A=w/(2b_B) makes the scaled-value bid w/[2(1+\mu_A)] equal b_B, so the two definitions agree. In second price J^*(B)=(N/L)\,w\,b_B-B with b_B=\sqrt{2BL/N} and N/L=2B/b_B^2, giving dJ^*/dB=w/b_B-1, which matches b=w/(1+\mu_A). A finite check: J^*(B)=w\sqrt{NB/L}-B gives J^*(501)-J^*(500)=\$0.2643.

When opportunities differ, with values w_i, one multiplier serves them all: bid as if each value were w_i/(1+\mu_A). Intuition: the last budget dollar must earn the same everywhere, or money should move to where it earns more. That the rule is optimal whenever such a \mu_A exists is proved below (the sufficiency theorem of Everett, 1963). One exists here by the intermediate value theorem: under the continuity, compactness, and uniqueness assumptions in the proof below, total expected spend S(\mu) is continuous, nonincreasing and tends to 0; if S(0)>B>0, some \mu_A gives S(\mu_A)=B. Without that continuity a multiplier can fail to exist, which Everett also shows. In practice w_i is an estimate, and bidding on noisy estimates wins most often where the estimate is too high (the winner's curse). Fees belong in the bill, and objectives such as volume under a ROAS target are different problems.

Optional: why one multiplier is enough (sufficiency proof)

Why spend can vary continuously. For opportunity i, write F_i(b) for its win probability at bid b, C_i(b) for expected cost, and u=w_i/(1+\mu) for the scaled value. Its objective is uF_i(b)-C_i(b). Suppose its optimal bid is unique on a compact bid interval and the objective is continuous. For scaled values u_2>u_1 with optimal bids b_2,b_1, add the two inequalities saying that each bid is optimal at its own value. This gives (u_2-u_1)(F_i(b_2)-F_i(b_1))\ge0. The optimality inequality at u_1\ge0 then gives C_i(b_2)-C_i(b_1)\ge u_1(F_i(b_2)-F_i(b_1))\ge0. To see continuity, take a sequence of scaled values approaching u. Compactness gives a convergent subsequence of its optimal bids. Taking limits in the optimality inequalities shows the limit is optimal at u. Uniqueness forces every convergent subsequence to have that same limit. Finally, comparison with the zero-cost no-bid option gives 0\le C_i(b_i)\le u F_i(b_i)\le u, so spend tends to zero as \mu\to\infty. For a finite set of opportunities these properties carry over to total spend.

With opportunity i having value w_i, win probability F_i and expected cost C_i, the advertiser solves

\max_{\{b_i\ge0\}}\sum_i\bigl[w_iF_i(b_i)-C_i(b_i)\bigr]\quad\text{subject to}\quad\sum_i C_i(b_i)\le B.

Write V(b)=\sum_i[w_iF_i(b_i)-C_i(b_i)] and S(b)=\sum_iC_i(b_i). Suppose b^* maximizes V(b)-\mu S(b) for some \mu\ge0, is feasible (S(b^*)\le B), and either \mu=0 or S(b^*)=B. For any feasible b:

V(b)\le V(b)-\mu\bigl(S(b)-B\bigr)\le V(b^*)-\mu\bigl(S(b^*)-B\bigr)=V(b^*).

The first step uses \mu\ge0 and S(b)\le B; the second uses the maximization; the last uses \mu(S(b^*)-B)=0. Because V-\mu S is a sum of separate terms w_iF_i(b_i)-(1+\mu)C_i(b_i), each b_i^* maximizes its own term, which is the single-opportunity problem with value w_i/(1+\mu) after dividing by 1+\mu.

The same chain with a different budget B' shows that \mu is the budget's marginal value in general, not only in the uniform example. For every b with S(b)\le B', V(b)\le V(b)-\mu(S(b)-B')\le V(b^*)-\mu S(b^*)+\mu B'=J^*(B)+\mu(B'-B), using \mu S(b^*)=\mu B. So the best value at B' exceeds the best value at B by at most \mu(B'-B), and the slope of this upper bound is \mu. At an interior budget, dividing by a positive budget change gives an upper bound on the right derivative. Dividing by a negative change reverses the inequality and gives a lower bound on the left derivative. If the derivatives agree, dJ^*/dB=\mu.

Pacing keeps the objective while controlling spend over time

Pacing is online control of how fast money is spent: the bidder adjusts \mu_A from observed spend. The first-price bidder still needs a shading rule. The uniform rule used here depends on the shape of F but does not require knowing L to compute the shaded bid. Let t index control periods, c_t be spend in period t, s_t the target spend and \eta>0 a step size in 1/dollar:

\mu_{A,t+1}=\max\{0,\ \mu_{A,t}+\eta\,(c_t-s_t)\}.

Three things follow directly: overspending raises the multiplier and lowers bids; the floor at zero means bids never rise past the profit optimum just to spend money; and any resting point with \mu_A>0 has c_t=s_t. Split the day into ten periods of 100,000 opportunities. At the $2,000 cap the flat target is $200 per period; the $4 CPM optimum spends 10^5(0.004)^2/0.02=\$80; starting at zero, \mu_A stays zero and the day spends $800. At the $500 cap the target is $50, and spend equals $50 when 10^5b^2/0.02=50, i.e. b=\$0.0031623, which is the static budget-binding bid with \mu_A\approx0.2649. With \eta=0.0085 per dollar and the first-price bid w/[2(1+\mu_A)], the controller gets there in a few periods:

Away from it, the nonlinear update and the zero floor can produce bounded oscillations; the appendix's pacing simulation shows three step sizes, including one that oscillates. The rule also controls only expected spend; hard caps need the reservation and settlement mechanisms in Chapter 7.

Different decision-maker and contract

Here an intermediary maximizes advertiser payments minus media cost. It charges the advertiser $30 per billable attributed conversion and buys publisher impressions in auctions. Its value per impression is v=qa=\$0.015, not the advertiser's w=\Delta qg=\$0.008, and its $2,000 budget limits publisher payments, not the advertiser's bill. Its optimal bids of $7.50 and $6.3246 CPM can coexist with advertiser losses. None of this describes Axon.

Advertiser spend, publisher payment, and network receipts

A network that buys per impression and sells per conversion keeps a ledger that can be positive while the advertiser's is negative. Two published examples fix the prices. In Epom's ad-arbitrage example a network buys publisher impressions at $2 CPM and resells them at $5 CPM. In Kevel's CPA example 9,000 impressions yield 18 billable conversions at $5 each, $90 in total, or $90 ÷ 9,000 × 1,000 = $10 eCPM (revenue restated per 1,000 impressions). Our own combination, which neither source describes: buy Kevel's 9,000 impressions at Epom's $2 CPM.

The network pays for every impression but is paid only per conversion: if the same $18 of impressions yields 2 conversions, it collects $10 and loses $8. The advertiser's ledger depends on how many conversions the ad caused. If each caused order leaves $8 of contribution, all 18 caused gives 18 × $8 − $90 = +$54, but only 9 caused gives 9 × $8 − $90 = −$18. The network keeps $72 either way. Attributed conversions decide the bill; incremental ones decide the advertiser's gain (Chapter 6). Chapter 1 uses the same calculations for the running campaign.

The network's bid: same algebra, different value

The network’s objective has the same shape as the advertiser's objective with w replaced by v, so its budget-free first-price bid is v/2 = $7.50 CPM. These numbers are set by this reader; the closest published derivation is Section 3.2.1 of Zhang and Wang's “Statistical Arbitrage Mining for Display Advertising”, which uses a second-price setting with the bid as a cost bound.

Two contracts. A: the advertiser pays the network a=\$30 per billable attributed order. B: the network buys each impression in a first-price auction and pays its bid. A successful impression produces a billable order with probability q=0.0005, independent of the bid and threshold. So the network's expected revenue per impression is

v=q\,a=0.0005\times\$30=\$0.015\ \text{per impression}.

Keep the main line's N, L and uniform threshold, with every win displayed. Define M(b) as advertiser payments minus media cost before the network’s other expenses.

M(b)=N\frac bL\,v-\frac{Nb^2}{L}=\frac NL\bigl(vb-b^2\bigr)=\frac NL\left[\left(\frac v2\right)^2-\left(b-\frac v2\right)^2\right].

The peak is b_0=v/2=\$0.0075 ($7.50 CPM): 375,000 expected wins, 187.5 attributed conversions, $5,625 collected, $2,812.50 media cost, and a difference of Nv^2/(4L)=10^6\times0.015^2/0.08=\$2{,}812.50.

Advertiser incremental profit under the network’s bid

The network's bid uses q and a; the advertiser's outcome depends on \Delta q and g, which the network's objective never sees. Use the running inputs \Delta q=0.0002 (true incremental order probability per impression) and g=\$40 (contribution per incremental order). Per 10,000 impressions:

\begin{aligned} &\text{network bills 5 attributed orders:} && 5\times\$30=\$150\\ &\text{advertiser gains 2 incremental orders:} && 2\times\$40=\$80\\ &\text{advertiser incremental contribution profit:} && \$80-\$150=-\$70 \end{aligned}

At b^* (unrounded) the network wins 316,227.766… impressions. Collections are 158.113883… orders × $30 = $4,743.42; payments minus media cost equal $2,743.42; the advertiser gains 63.245553… incremental orders × $40 = $2,529.82, so its incremental contribution profit is −$2,213.59. The true incremental CPA, spend per ad-caused order, is $4,743.416… ÷ 63.245553… = $75 (qa/\Delta q=0.015/0.0002), far above the $40 an order is worth. The advertiser breaks even only if

\Delta q\,g\ge q\,a\iff\Delta q\ge\frac{0.0005\times30}{40}=0.000375=3.75\ \text{per }10{,}000.

Moving \Delta q from 2 to 3.75 per 10,000 leaves the network's bid and collections unchanged. \Delta q and q measure different events, and neither bounds the other.

\rho is the probability that a win becomes a successful impression; orders come only from successful impressions. Fix a first-price bid of $6 CPM with \rho = 50%: 300,000 wins, 150,000 impressions, and collections of 150,000 × 0.0005 × $30 = $2,250.

For the general formula, let \alpha be the probability a win is billed (\alpha=\rho display-based, \alpha=1 win-based) and k the payment divisor (k=1 first price, k=2 ideal second price, so the expected price per billed win is b/k; k is local to this box). Assuming display success is independent of Z:

\begin{aligned} C&=N\,\frac{b}{L}\,\alpha\,\frac{b}{k}=\frac{N\alpha b^2}{kL}, & M&=\frac{N}{L}\Bigl(\rho vb-\frac{\alpha b^2}{k}\Bigr),\\ b^*&=\min\Bigl\{\frac{k\rho v}{2\alpha},\ \sqrt{\frac{kBL}{N\alpha}},\ L\Bigr\}, & \text{advertiser}&=N\frac bL\rho\,(\Delta q\,g-q\,a). \end{aligned}

The first term comes from M'(b)=(N/L)(\rho v-2\alpha b/k)=0, a maximum since M''<0; the second from C\le B. Neither budget binds. The slider keeps \rho\ge50\%, so \alpha>0.

Maximizing total value is a different budget objective

A bidder that maximizes total value within a budget, rather than value minus cost, can rationally bid above value. Per request, let C(b) be expected cost: bF(b) in first price, \int_0^b z\,dF(z) in second price. The total-value problem is \max\sum_i v_iF_i(b_i) subject to \sum_iC_i(b_i)\le B. With a multiplier \lambda\ge0 the per-request term is vF(b)-\lambda C(b), and setting its derivative to zero (C'=F+bf or bf) gives

\text{Second price:}\quad b=\frac{v}{\lambda},\qquad\text{First price:}\quad b=\frac{v}{\lambda}-\frac{F(b)}{f(b)},

compared with b=v/(1+\mu) (second price) and b=v/(1+\mu)-F(b)/f(b) (first price) for the profit objective. The main line's sufficiency argument applies with \lambda in place of 1+\mu. Existence of the multiplier is again assumed. When both objectives spend exactly B, profit equals total value minus the constant B on that boundary, so they pick the same bids and a supporting profit multiplier \mu yields a supporting total-value multiplier \lambda=1+\mu; at $2,000 in our example both bid $6.3246 CPM. They split when the budget is loose: the profit bidder stops at \mu=0 and leaves money unspent, while the total-value bidder keeps buying, and with \lambda<1 its second-price bid v/\lambda exceeds value. If the threshold distribution must be learned from win/loss feedback, see learning the auction prices in Chapter 5.

Budget pacing is online control, not even spending

The pacing rule from the main line finds the network's budget multiplier from spend alone, but whether it settles depends on the step size. The bid follows from the multiplier: b=v/(1+\mu_t) in second price, or v/\bigl(2(1+\mu_t)\bigr) in uniform first price. This is an illustration, not a stability proof (feedback control in RTB advertising studies real controllers).
