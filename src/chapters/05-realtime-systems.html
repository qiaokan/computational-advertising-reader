Real-time advertising systems

Organize the system around three pipelines

The control pipeline handles contracts, campaigns, creatives, budgets, and releases; the online pipeline performs eligibility checks, retrieval, prediction, and bidding before the deadline; the event pipeline handles delivery, billing, attribution, training, and reconciliation. The distinctive difficulty of an ad system is not which database to choose for each of these boxes, but whether they can reach a verifiable, consistent account of “what happened” and “how much is owed.” A successful HTTP response proves neither that an impression was delivered nor that a conversion occurred, much less that the advertiser benefited.

Request deadlines and per-stage computation

OpenRTB is a family of protocols for programmatic bidding. Its tmax is the upper time limit within which the exchange will accept a response, including network latency; price uses CPM, even if a transaction buys only one impression. Times or auction types in default examples must not be taken as industry facts. OpenRTB 2.6 request and bid objects

Suppose a request in this example specifies tmax=120ms. This is a teaching assumption, not an industry service-level metric. The online service should work backward from this deadline to budget time for network, feature reads, retrieval, inference, budget admission, and encoding. A model's own inference P99 (the 99th percentile of the latency distribution) of 20ms does not guarantee that it can return end-to-end across regions before the deadline; changes in candidate count, cache misses, and queueing tail latency jointly determine the actual completion rate.

Degradation must have economic semantics. Using default values when features are missing, switching to a smaller model, or simply not bidding carry different value biases and risks. When key configuration is stale, the budget has no available quota, or creative review status is unknown, blindly continuing to bid can cause real spend and contract breaches. Only by defining applicable conditions, maximum exposure, and observable reason codes for each degradation path can you judge whether “improved availability” has merely moved losses into the ledger.

How a single request in the unified example is booked

Continue with the budget-constrained first-price bid 6.324555 CPM, i.e., a maximum media cost of about 0.006324555 dollars each, with no extra fees for now. Assume the upstream contract gives a determinable billing rule and maximum obligation.

First, the system establishes business identity using fields such as exchange, request_id, imp_id, bid_id, and records the campaign, model, features, configuration, and experiment versions. The budget system approves and reserves the maximum possible spend for this bid before allowing the bid to be sent. Accumulating millions of monetary amounts in floating point is a bad idea: internally use integer amounts with an explicit scale or fixed-precision decimals, and convert and round as agreed at protocol boundaries.

A win notice may then be received. OpenRTB's nurl indicates winning the auction, and does not guarantee that the ad has been delivered, seen, or is billable. burl indicates entering a billable state according to the exchange's business policy. Whether these notices are sent, how delayed they are, and what basis is used in disputes must be defined by the integration contract, rather than assuming every interface implements all optional fields. OpenRTB 2.6 bid notification semantics

After a win, the network continues to hold the corresponding open obligation; upon receiving a contractually recognized billing signal, it converts the actual billed amount into spend and releases the difference between the reservation cap and the actual amount. This assumes the actual amount does not exceed the reservation cap; if it does, the premise of a cost cap has been broken, and the case should go to exception and risk handling, without continuing to claim that the original hard-budget guarantee holds. Duplicate callbacks may trigger only one accounting change. Not receiving an impression pixel does not mean there is no external debt; you cannot simply wait out a TTL (a locally set time-to-live / timeout) and then spend the amount again. Late notices and settlement discrepancies must enter a pending-reconciliation state until there is sufficient basis to discharge the obligation.

Clicks and conversions enter a separate event pipeline. The publisher is paid per impression and the advertiser pays per billable conversion; the event types, windows, and payment timing differ. After the media ledger confirms spend, the network may have to wait days to learn whether that purchase produced a collectible conversion, and may also encounter chargebacks or refunds. The system must store media payables, advertiser receivables, and training/attribution events separately, and cannot use a single “conversion success” field to stand in for all three.

Final reconciliation compares protocol events, publisher or exchange settlements, and advertiser bills. Making internal queue consumers idempotent does not prove end-to-end exactly-once across external links. You need replayable events and reversal records, clear correction authority, and the ability to explain the source of every discrepancy.

How delivery probability changes the bid

This section continues with first-price auctions: b is the nominal bid price in dollars per billable unit (per successful display in Case A, per win in Case B), v=0.015 is the network's expected revenue per successful impression; Z is the competitive threshold, uniformly distributed on [0,L], L=0.02; F(b)=b/L is the win rate. N=1,000,000 is the number of requests, B=2,000 is the expected media budget. π_A, π_B are the expected collections minus media cost per request under the two contracts, and C_A and C_B are the expected media costs per request; the letters A and B in these subscripts are only contract labels, distinct from the budget symbol B.

Now relax the numerical model's assumption that “a win means display and billing.” Using the same campaign, let the probability of a successful impression after winning be ρ=0.9; assume it is unrelated to the bid and the competitive threshold, and that attributed conversions and network collections arise only after a successful impression. Below we compare two fictional contracts devised for teaching; this is not a claim that OpenRTB or MAX uses either one by default. The model objective is still to maximize network net profit under the budget constraint.

Case A pays the publisher only after a successful impression. The expected net profit and media cost per request are then:

\pi_A(b)=\rho(v-b)F(b),\qquad C_A(b)=\rho bF(b).

The constant ρ scales both collections and cost, so the no-budget optimum is still b=v/2. With these parameters this strategy is expected to spend 2,531.25 dollars, so the budget still binds, hence b=√(BL/(ρN))=0.006666667 dollars, i.e., 6.666667 CPM. Expected wins are about 333,333.33 and successful impressions 300,000, producing 150 attributed conversions, network collections of 4,500 dollars, media spend of 2,000 dollars, and a gross spread of 2,500 dollars.

Case B's contract creates a media payment obligation upon winning the auction, charging even if the display later fails; this does not mean automatically interpreting the protocol's nurl as billing evidence. Then:

\pi_B(b)=(\rho v-b)F(b),\qquad C_B(b)=bF(b).

The no-budget optimum drops to b=ρv/2=0.00675 dollars, with expected spend of 2,278.125 dollars, so the 2,000-dollar budget still binds. Cost is not scaled by ρ, so the budget-constrained bid is still 6.324555 CPM; successful impressions are about 284,604.99, attributed conversions about 142.302495, network collections 4,269.074841 dollars, and the gross spread 2,269.074841 dollars.

The difference between the two contracts directly changes the bid and returns, not merely the event names in reports. If the successful-impression probability varies with Z, the bid, or opportunity quality, you cannot directly pull the constant ρ outside the integral; the corresponding conditional probability and payment function must be remodeled. On the systems side, the reservation targets the maximum payment obligation under the contract; the hard quota reservation cannot be automatically discounted by 10% just because the expected display probability is only 90%.

Division of labor between expected-budget control and hard budgets

This section switches to actual ledger quantities instead of expected cost per request: let C be confirmed cumulative media spend, R the total of maximum possible payment obligations not yet discharged, and B the hard media budget for the same accounting period, all in dollars. Here R is the obligation reservation, not the revenue function R(C) from the “Products and cash flow” chapter. If every bid has a reliable cost cap, and reservation operations are serializable, or each node has only a local quota whose total is provably within the limit, then maintaining the following invariant can support a hard budget:

C+R\le B.

This requires that fees, unreported spend, expired bids that may still be accepted, and late billing all be included in the definition. Using “win probability × bid” as the reservation amount improves capital utilization, but what remains is statistical overspend control, not a proof of zero overspend on every path. Expiry of a multi-region lease also does not automatically discharge external obligations already incurred.

Safety must also be separated from fund availability: never releasing undischarged R keeps the budget safe but locks up quota. Safe release requires an authoritative final loss, cancellation, or reconciliation result, or a binding contractual cutoff rule that explicitly extinguishes remaining payment obligations; such evidence can appear before the cutoff date, and release need not wait until the deadline. An empirical upper bound on callback delay or a local TTL does not mean the payment obligation has terminated. If no confirmable final discharge mechanism exists, the system can only hold the reservation long-term, or accept statistical overspend within an explicit risk limit; the latter can no longer be called a per-path hard-budget guarantee.

In this example, reserving the maximum spend long-term for all one million potential bids would over-lock the budget; you need to promptly release clearly failed bids, shorten obligation confirmation time, and allocate quota to regions or shards according to supply quality. Conservative reservation reduces overspend risk but may cause under-delivery. This cost should be quantified as lost net profit, not evaluated only by lock contention rate or budget service QPS (queries per second).

If the advertiser separately requires a hard cap on the CPA bill, that is yet another problem: delayed post-click conversions still generate receivables. Merely shutting off new media procurement cannot guarantee that impressions already delivered will not generate further billable events. This should be handled through contractual terms, conservative control of remaining quota, and management of in-flight conversion obligations, rather than passing off the media ledger as the advertiser ledger.

Correctness boundaries for data and model releases

Decision logs need to link at least the following: bid opportunity identity, candidate eligibility and final action, the time or version of available features, predicted probabilities and valuations, bid and budget policy, experiment assignment, win/loss and rejection reasons, impression/click/conversion times, attribution rules, billing status, and subsequent adjustments. Not all fields can be retained indefinitely; access and retention policies must match data permissions.

Training-sample construction should be replayable to a specific version, and the model, calibrator, feature definitions, creatives, and budget-control parameters should be tracked together. Otherwise, if one release changes both the model and the attribution window, even if online results improve, you cannot tell why. Rolling back the model without rolling back features or the calibrator may not restore the old behavior either.

Shadow prediction first can reveal performance and distribution anomalies, but because the shadow model does not affect traffic selection, it cannot prove that it will make money online. After a canary rollout, check whether the traffic mix and price distribution have changed; evaluate under the same budget constraints and matured labels. A new policy picking different opportunities is itself part of the effect; you cannot enjoy the benefit of traffic selection while statistically pretending that both groups bought exactly the same samples.

A dashboard that drives action

Good monitoring points failures to specific economic consequences. Pre-deadline response rate and non-participation rate broken down by reason explain lost opportunities; the gap from win to billable explains delivery and interface risk; the amount and age of open obligations explain budget lock-up; duplicate billing, settlement discrepancies, and the overspend tail explain financial correctness; segment calibration, matured CPA/ROAS, and incremental contribution explain policy quality.

Signal Investigate first Conclusions you cannot draw directly
Win rate up while profit down Bids, competitive prices, traffic mix, fees The model is definitely worse, or definitely better
Clicks up while conversions down Creatives, landing pages, click authenticity, label maturity CTR weight should be increased further
Sudden drop in spend Eligibility, deadlines, quota, economic constraints All under-delivery is a pacing failure
Sudden drop in reported CPA Windows, attribution rules, event deduplication, backfill The customer received an equally large incremental lift
Offline gain, online decline Time leakage, calibration, traffic selection, delay, and release versions Just switch to a bigger model

Specific thresholds must be set by contracts, loss tolerance, and business objectives; there is no uniform industry number you can simply copy. Duplicate billing, budget violations, and changes in value definitions should each be able to trigger stop-loss independently; you cannot wait for conversion reports days later to discover that today's system is already out of control.
