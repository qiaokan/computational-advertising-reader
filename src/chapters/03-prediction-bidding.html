From advertiser value to bidding and budgets

In this chapter

Start with whose money and whose objective

The main decision-maker is the advertiser, or a buying agent instructed to act for it. The objective here is to maximize expected incremental contribution after advertising costs: contribution from sales caused by advertising, minus the advertiser's entire ad bill. Contribution is sales revenue less goods, fulfillment and other variable non-advertising costs; company fixed costs are outside this model. Spending the budget is a constraint, not a separate goal.

\text{Advertiser objective}=\text{incremental sales contribution}-\text{total advertising bill}.

An ad network's spread is a different ledger: advertiser payments collected minus publisher payments made, before the network's other costs. Optimizing that ledger does not automatically optimize the advertiser's outcome. The optional network-spread appendix explains the $7.50 and $6.3246 CPM network-spread examples, including the calculator. Those are network-optimal bids under a particular contract, not general advertiser recommendations or claims about Axon's internal objective.

The existing CPA contract: first check whether buying anything makes sense

Keep the contract from Chapter 1: the advertiser pays a=\$30 per billable attributed order. Let q=0.0005 be the probability of such an order per successful impression; let \Delta q=0.0002 be the true increase in purchase probability caused by showing the ad; and let g=\$40 be contribution per additional order. These probabilities and contribution are teaching inputs, held constant as the bid changes. Attribution assigns billing credit; it does not establish causation.

\begin{aligned} \text{Incremental contribution per impression}&=\Delta q\,g=0.0002\times40=\$0.008,\\ \text{Advertiser bill per impression, in expectation}&=q\,a=0.0005\times30=\$0.015,\\ \text{Advertiser gain per impression}&=0.008-0.015=-\$0.007. \end{aligned}

Let D be the expected number of successful impressions. Under this fixed-CPA contract the advertiser's expected incremental contribution after its bill is D(\Delta qg-qa)=-0.007D. If it can stop the campaign, the advertiser's optimum is zero impressions. Lowering the network's publisher payments does not lower the advertiser's fixed $30 CPA price. No derivative of network spread can turn this contract into a profitable one for the advertiser. Renegotiating the price, improving causal lift or stopping are economic choices that precede bid optimization.

Why does a $6 CPM bid win 30% of opportunities?

Two questions are separate: what price must we beat in one auction? and how often does a fixed bid beat it across many auctions? The first defines the competitive threshold; the second needs a probability model.

1. One auction: our bid against the highest competing bid

Assume every opportunity has at least one competing bid, and let Z be the highest of them. A floor price (reserve price) is the seller's minimum acceptable price; this model has none. Z is then the competitive threshold. We choose our bid b. If competitors bid $3, $5 and $9 CPM, then Z = $9 CPM: a $6 CPM bid loses and a $10 CPM bid wins. Our own bid is not part of the maximum that defines Z.

Z=\max\{\text{competing bids}\},\qquad \text{win}\iff b\ge Z.

Ties go to us (b\ge Z wins). Under the continuous distribution below an exact tie has probability zero, so the convention affects no result; with discrete price increments, real tie-breaking rules can matter. The threshold decides whether we win, not necessarily what we pay: in the default first-price auction the winner pays its own bid.

2. Many auctions: treat the unknown threshold as random

Different opportunities draw different competitors and bids, so Z varies across auctions, and the bidder does not know the next value in advance. The teaching model assumes Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) (\sim reads “is distributed as”): price intervals of equal length are equally likely. We further assume every opportunity's threshold has this same distribution and that our own bidding does not move it (we are a price taker). The assumption concerns the highest competing bid, not each competitor's bid.

On the $0–20 CPM range, each $2-wide interval has probability 2/20 = 10%; the intervals $0–2 and $12–14 are equally likely.

3. We win exactly when the threshold falls below our bid

For a fixed b we win exactly when Z\in[0,b], an interval of length b inside a range of length L. Let F be the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of Z, so F(b) is the win probability at bid b:

F(b)=P(Z\le b)=\frac{b-0}{L-0}=\frac{b}{L},\qquad 0\le b\le L.

The denominator is the whole price range, not our bid. A $6 CPM bid wins against thresholds in $0–6 (length 6) and loses against thresholds in $6–20 (length 14): probabilities 6/20 = 30% and 14/20 = 70%.

Outside the range, F(b)=0 for b<0 and F(b)=1 for b\ge L. Bidding above L buys no extra win probability and, under first-price, only raises the payment, so such bids are dominated.

4. CPM and per-event prices give the same probability

Every price in a comparison must use the same unit. Internally b, Z and L are in dollars per impression (the billable event in this direct-buying contract); a CPM quote is 1,000 times that. The subscript CPM marks a quote, not a different quantity:

\begin{aligned} b_{\text{CPM}}&=1000\,b,\qquad L_{\text{CPM}}=1000\,L,\\ \frac{b}{L}&=\frac{\$0.006}{\$0.02}=\frac{\$6\ \text{CPM}}{\$20\ \text{CPM}}=0.30. \end{aligned}

The factor 1,000 cancels. Dividing a $6 CPM quote by $0.02 per event mixes units and gives 300, which is not a probability.

The seller’s billing terms fix what a billable event is: a win under win-based billing, a successful impression under display-based billing. Here every win displays successfully, so the two counts coincide. Switching the billing basis changes ad spend; it does not change the win event Z\le b.

5. From win probability to expected wins

Let W be the number of opportunities won. With the same bid and the same marginal threshold distribution on every opportunity, linearity of expectation gives

\mathbb E[W]=N\,F(b)=1{,}000{,}000\times0.30=300{,}000\ \text{wins}.

Linearity needs no independence. Dependence across opportunities (for example, one competitor bidding on correlated traffic) changes only how widely the realized count fluctuates around 300,000. With successful display on every win, these wins are also 300,000 expected impressions.

Optional: the density derivation, and what changes outside this model

The uniform threshold has density f(z)=1/L on [0,L] and 0 elsewhere, where z denotes a possible value of Z. The total area is L\cdot(1/L)=1. Integrating the density over the winning interval recovers the CDF:

F(b)=\int_0^b f(z)\,dz=\int_0^b\frac{1}{L}\,dz=\frac{b}{L},\qquad 0\le b\le L.

In a non-uniform market the win probability is still F(b)=P(Z\le b), but not b/L: if 80% of thresholds lie below $6 CPM, a $6 CPM bid wins 80%, not 30%. Floor prices, discrete bids, heterogeneous opportunity types and competitors that react to our bids each need a different model. Note also that the maximum of n independent \mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) competing bids has CDF (b/L)^n, so uniform individual bids do not produce a uniform threshold.

First-price bidding: maximize the advertiser's contribution after cost

In first price, a winner pays its own bid b. Define J_1(b) as the advertiser's expected incremental contribution after ad spend across all N opportunities, and C_1(b) as its expected ad spend. A loss creates neither contribution nor cost in this model.

\begin{aligned} \text{Expected impressions}&=NF(b),\\ \text{Expected incremental contribution}&=NF(b)w,\\ C_1(b)&=NF(b)b,\\ J_1(b)&=NF(b)(w-b). \end{aligned}

The factor w-b is the advertiser's expected gain on one won impression. The factor NF(b) is how many impressions it expects to buy. A larger bid buys more impressions but raises the price on every first-price win. We optimize their product because we have explicitly chosen advertiser contribution after cost as the objective.

Using the uniform win probability, the objective becomes J_1(b)=(N/L)(wb-b^2). The squared cost arises from multiplying the number of wins Nb/L by the first-price payment b. First find the budget-free maximum, denoted b_0; the subscript 0 means “before applying the spend cap.” Primes below are derivatives with respect to b.

J_1'(b)=\frac NL(w-2b)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b_0=\frac w2=\$0.004=\$4\ \mathrm{CPM},\qquad J_1''(b)=-\frac{2N}{L}<0.

The same result follows without calculus by completing the square:

J_1(b)=\frac NL\left[\left(\frac w2\right)^2-\left(b-\frac w2\right)^2\right].

The first term is fixed. The squared term is nonnegative and vanishes only at b=w/2, so that bid gives the largest contribution when feasible. The negative second derivative establishes strict concavity, so this is the unique global maximum on [0,L] when it lies inside that interval, as it does here. Equivalently, the first derivative is positive below $4 CPM and negative above it: raising the bid first helps the advertiser, then hurts. Differentiation solves the selected objective; it cannot choose whose objective is appropriate. The rule b=w/2 relies on the uniform threshold distribution starting at zero. It is not a general first-price rule: changing the win-probability function changes the optimum.

The budget restricts the choice; it does not require full spend

A bid is feasible if it meets both the price range and the advertiser's spend cap: 0\le b\le L and C_1(b)\le B. With uniform thresholds, C_1(b)=Nb^2/L, so the budget allows bids up to \sqrt{BL/N}. Let b_1^* be the advertiser's best feasible first-price bid. For the nonnegative value in this model,

b_1^*=\min\left\{\frac w2,\sqrt{\frac{BL}{N}},L\right\}.
Worked example

$2,000 cap versus $500 cap

  1. At the budget-free advertiser optimum of $4 CPM, spend is 10^6(0.004)^2/0.02=\$800. A $2,000 cap is slack: it does not restrict the optimum. The advertiser keeps $1,200 unspent and earns $800 of incremental contribution after cost.
  2. With B=\$500, the $4 CPM bid is unaffordable. The budget is binding: b_1^*=\sqrt{500(0.02)/10^6}=\$0.00316227766, or $3.1623 CPM. This gives 158,113.88 expected impressions, $1,264.91 incremental contribution before spend, and J_1=1264.911064-500=\$764.91 after spend.

Check: without the budget, $800 is the maximum of the whole objective curve. The tighter $500 cap lowers it to $764.91. A larger budget cannot force the advertiser to buy unprofitable volume when stopping is allowed.

Ideal second-price bidding under the same advertiser objective

In ideal second price the winner pays the competing threshold Z, not its own bid. Define C_2(b) and J_2(b) as expected ad spend and incremental contribution after spend under this rule. For uniform thresholds and a positive bid within the range, the average price conditional on winning is b/2, because winning thresholds are uniform over [0,b]. Thus:

\begin{aligned}C_2(b)&=N\frac bL\frac b2=\frac{Nb^2}{2L},\\J_2(b)&=\frac NL\left(wb-\frac{b^2}{2}\right),\\J_2'(b)&=\frac NL(w-b),\qquad J_2''(b)=-\frac NL<0,\\b_2^*&=\min\left\{w,\sqrt{\frac{2BL}{N}},L\right\}.\end{aligned}

At B=\$2{,}000, the optimum is b_2^*=w=\$0.008 ($8 CPM). It buys 400,000 expected impressions, creates 400{,}000\times0.008=\$3{,}200 incremental contribution, spends $1,600 and leaves $1,600 after spend. At B=\$500, the optimum is $4.4721 CPM, with 223,606.80 expected impressions, $1,788.85 incremental contribution, $500 spend and $1,288.85 after spend. This comparison holds the competitive-threshold distribution fixed; it does not predict how competitors would react to a change of auction rules.

What a real objective still needs

This example maximizes contribution, rather than revenue, attributed conversions or full budget use. A business may instead maximize conversion volume subject to a return target, or impose minimum scale and other obligations; those are different optimization problems. A real buying agent must include its actual fees in the advertiser's bill and use values that vary by opportunity. A negative estimated incremental value calls for not bidding when no delivery obligation forces participation. The calculations above are a transparent benchmark, not evidence of how Axon or any particular platform chooses its internal objective.

For heterogeneous opportunities, write w_i for incremental contribution per successful impression on request i, F_i(b_i) for its win probability, and C_i(b_i) for its unconditional expected all-in advertiser cost. With the same display-success and separability assumptions, the advertiser problem is:

\max_{\{b_i\ge0\}}\sum_i\bigl[w_iF_i(b_i)-C_i(b_i)\bigr]\quad\text{subject to}\quad\sum_i C_i(b_i)\le B.

A budget controller (pacing) adjusts participation or bids as spend and opportunity value evolve. It should preserve the stated advertiser objective, and need not force full spend when profitable opportunities run out. The next section gives the advertiser's multiplier and pacing rule. The appendix's numerical network multipliers cannot be copied into this advertiser contract.

Beyond uniform prices: advertiser bidding and pacing

The $4 CPM result uses a particular distribution. For a general continuous competitive threshold, let F(b)=P(Z\le b) be the win-probability function and f(b)=F'(b) its density. Hold advertiser incremental value w constant conditional on the information used for this bid. The direct-buying, no-fee, successful-display assumptions remain in force. Define j_1(b) and j_2(b) as advertiser incremental contribution after cost per request, under first and ideal second price.

\begin{aligned} j_1(b)&=(w-b)F(b),& j_1'(b)&=(w-b)f(b)-F(b),\\ j_2(b)&=\int_0^b(w-z)f(z)\,dz,& j_2'(b)&=(w-b)f(b). \end{aligned}

The integration variable z is a possible competing price. In first price, an interior candidate with f(b)>0 satisfies b=w-F(b)/f(b); boundaries and the global objective still need checking. The reduction from value to bid is bid shading: accepting fewer wins to pay less per win. It depends on the price distribution. For uniform thresholds F/f=b, giving b=w/2. If the threshold is instead the maximum of two independent uniform bids on [0,L], then F=(b/L)^2 and F/f=b/2, so the first-price optimum for our w=0.008 is 2w/3=\$0.0053333\ldots ($5.3333 CPM). The objective rises then falls around this point.

In ideal second price, the derivative is nonnegative below w and nonpositive above it. Thus bidding w is optimal if that bid is allowed; it need not be uniquely optimal when the threshold distribution has gaps. This argument assumes one slot and the stated payment rule. Auctions with multiple ranked slots, quality weights, extra fees or other constraints need their own model.

The advertiser's budget multiplier

Let \mu_A\ge0 be the advertiser's budget multiplier; subscript A identifies this advertiser contract. For a request with unconditional expected ad cost c(b), adding the spend constraint to the contribution objective gives the per-request expression wF(b)-(1+\mu_A)c(b). The 1 accounts for the cash cost itself; \mu_A adds the cost of scarce budget. At a regular optimum, this dimensionless multiplier is the marginal gain in advertiser contribution-after-cost per extra dollar of budget.

\begin{aligned} \text{First price, interior candidate:}\quad b&=\frac{w}{1+\mu_A}-\frac{F(b)}{f(b)},\\ \text{Ideal second price:}\quad b&=\frac{w}{1+\mu_A}. \end{aligned}

These are interior conditions under the same density and boundary qualifications. For uniform first price the expression reduces to b=w/[2(1+\mu_A)]. At our binding $500 cap, first price gives \mu_A=w/(2b_1^*)-1\approx0.2649, while ideal second price gives \mu_A=w/b_2^*-1\approx0.7889. At $2,000 both caps are slack and \mu_A=0. These are advertiser multipliers, distinct from the appendix's network multipliers.

Pacing keeps the objective while controlling spend over time

Pacing is online control of how quickly money is spent. Let t index control periods, c_t be actual advertiser spend in that period, s_t its desired spend trajectory, and \eta>0 a step size in 1/dollar. A simple illustrative update is:

\mu_{A,t+1}=\max\{0,\mu_{A,t}+\eta(c_t-s_t)\}.

Overspending raises the multiplier and lowers bids; underspending lowers it, but never below zero. The zero boundary matters: bids can return to the advertiser-profit optimum without being forced beyond it just to spend money. For example, split our million opportunities into ten equal periods. At the $2,000 cap, a flat target is $200 per period, while the $4 CPM first-price optimum spends only $80 per period. Starting at zero, the multiplier stays at zero and the day spends $800, preserving the advertiser's optimum.

This update illustrates control direction, not a stability or hard-budget guarantee. Delayed bills, traffic changes and interactions can produce oscillations; target trajectories should adapt to remaining budget and opportunity value. Actual hard spending caps require the reservation and settlement mechanisms in Chapter 5. Define the event the model predicts

Let x be the information available when the bid is placed (user, context, ad slot and ad). Define three indicators: S=1 if the impression succeeds; K=1 if the user clicks; Y=1 if a qualifying conversion occurs within the agreed observation window (the period after the ad in which a conversion counts, for example 7 days); each is 0 otherwise. The Chapter 1 CPA contract (checked above and used in the appendix) bills post-click attributed purchases, so a billable event requires K=1 and Y=1. The billable conversion probability per successful impression is

q(x)=P(K=1,Y=1\mid x,S=1)=P(K=1\mid x,S=1)\cdot P(Y=1\mid K=1,x,S=1).

This is the chain rule; it does not assume clicks and conversions are independent. If the contract also counts view-through conversions (conversions after an impression without a click), add the qualifying K=0 branch. CTR (click-through rate) is clicks per impression, and pCTR is a model's predicted CTR. CVR (conversion rate) and pCVR are the observed and predicted conversion rates (Chapter 2). Always state the denominator: P(Y=1\mid K=1,x,S=1) is per click, while q(x) is per successful impression. If a model already predicts conversions per impression, do not multiply by CTR again.

When q(x) feeds the CPA billing model in the appendix, we also assume that, given x and a successful impression, response probabilities do not depend on the bid b or the competitive threshold Z (defined in the threshold section). Failed displays produce no conversions. The expected number of billable conversions from one bid request, before we know whether we win, is therefore F(b)\,\rho\,q(x), where F(b) is the win probability and \rho=P(S=1\mid\text{win}) the display success rate. q(x) itself is not a per-request probability.

For a network paid per billable conversion at settlement price a, v(x)=q(x)\,a is the expected revenue per successful impression. With this chapter's teaching values q=0.0005 and a=\$30, v=\$0.015. For the advertiser's direct-buying objective, the value is instead w(x)=\Delta q(x)g, where \Delta q(x) is causal purchase-probability lift per successful display at information x and g is contribution per additional order. Attribution probability q(x) alone does not identify that lift. Response prediction, value prediction and incrementality (uplift) prediction have different targets.

Model metrics. Positives are samples in which the target event occurred; negatives are samples in which it did not. AUC (area under the ROC curve) is the probability that a randomly drawn positive scores higher than a randomly drawn negative, with ties counting one half. It measures ranking, not whether the probability values are right. A model is calibrated if, among samples predicted at probability p, the observed frequency is p once their labels have matured, i.e., once the observation window has closed and late conversions are counted.

Log loss scores probabilistic predictions. For a label y\in\{0,1\} and predicted probability p\in(0,1), the per-sample loss is -\bigl[y\ln p+(1-y)\ln(1-p)\bigr]; a lower average over a fixed evaluation set is better. The score depends on the evaluation sample, the label definition and any sample weights, and it does not substitute for delivery profit.

What each stage of the model pipeline does

Candidate retrieval narrows the eligible ads to the few worth scoring; ranking then estimates response and value for that smaller set; finally, value estimates, budgets, eligibility rules and the auction rules produce the decision. If retrieval drops a high-value ad, no downstream model can recover it, so improving ranking AUC alone may never touch the system's main bottleneck. Meta's Andromeda, described publicly in 2024, shows staged retrieval followed by ranking. It is one industrial example, not a claim about every platform or about Axon's current architecture (Chapter 6). Meta 2024 Andromeda

Ad features typically describe the advertiser and campaign (an advertiser's unit of budget, targeting and goal), the creative (the actual image, video or text shown), the ad slot, the user or context, past interactions and time. What matters is not the feature list but whether each feature is available at decision time, permitted for use, stably covered, and identical in meaning in training and serving. New ads and creatives have no feedback history, so the model must rely on transferable content and context features, and a controlled exploration budget must buy them impressions. Otherwise past winners keep accumulating labels and new candidates never get the chance to prove themselves.

For post-click conversion, ESMM (Entire Space Multi-Task Model) trains on all impressions using the chain impression → click → conversion: it is supervised on click labels (pCTR) and click-and-convert labels (pCTCVR = pCTR × pCVR), so its pCVR tower is fitted over all impressions without ever dividing one estimate by another. This addresses sample selection bias: a CVR model trained only on clicked samples is applied to all impressions. ESMM does not by itself fix selection bias created by the serving policy (data logged under one policy used to learn or evaluate another), delayed feedback (conversions that arrive after the label is read), or causal identification. Original ESMM paper

Accept a model only after checking ranking, calibration, stability across segments and business outcomes together. AUC measures one property of ranking, but bidding needs absolute probabilities, because value is a probability times a price. Doubling every predicted probability (while staying at or below 1) leaves ranking and AUC unchanged; under linear value bidding with a fixed coefficient and no cap, it also doubles every bid. Good overall calibration does not imply calibration on slices such as expensive publishers, new users or one country. Google's 2013 industrial paper already treated sampling, calibration and confidence as system problems; a larger model does not make them disappear. If negatives were downsampled for training, predicted probabilities must be corrected back to the true base rate (Chapter 4 gives the formula). Google click prediction paper

Learning the auction prices needed by the bidder

The main advertiser model assumed the threshold distribution was known. The following explains how auction feedback constrains learning it. A censored price is only known to lie in an interval. A likelihood is the probability or density the model assigns to the observed data; independent log-likelihood terms add.

Why price feedback cannot be used directly as a regression label

In ideal second-price, let Z be the competitive threshold, b the logged bid and x the decision-time information, with conditional CDF F(z\mid x) and density f(z\mid x). Assume a win reveals Z (it is the price paid), while a loss reveals only Z>b. Winning prices are selected by the policy: they are exactly the thresholds that fell below our bids. A model trained only on these wins estimates a selected price distribution. At a fixed bid, it excludes all thresholds above that bid. The direction of error for a particular fitted model depends on what it estimates and how it extrapolates. Let \ell be the log-likelihood (natural log) and “win” and “loss” the sets of won and lost requests; each term uses that request's own Z, b and x. For conditionally independent requests the censored log-likelihood is

\ell= \sum_{\mathrm{win}}\log f(Z\mid x) +\sum_{\mathrm{loss}}\log\bigl(1-F(b\mid x)\bigr).

Each won request contributes the density at its observed threshold; each lost request contributes the probability that the threshold exceeded its bid. This is valid only if wins and losses are determined purely by the price comparison, and if, once the price-relevant policy history used for bidding is included in the logged x, the bid carries no further information about the threshold: P(Z\le z\mid x,b)=F(z\mid x) (non-informative censoring). If the policy used unlogged, price-relevant information, the observation model must be extended. Timeouts, creative rejections, budget filtering and users leaving the page are not “the market price exceeded the bid” and must not be coded as such losses. The price-distribution chapter of the RTB monograph is a good entry point to the censoring problem. RTB monograph, Chapter 3

In first-price, our payment b is not the competitive threshold; on a win it is only an upper bound on it. With win/loss feedback alone, a win tells us Z\le b and a loss tells us Z>b, so every observation is censored and the log-likelihood becomes

\ell= \sum_{\mathrm{win}}\log F(b\mid x) +\sum_{\mathrm{loss}}\log\bigl(1-F(b\mid x)\bigr).

This is a binary-response likelihood for the win probability. Without a parametric model it identifies F only at the bids actually placed, so learning the whole distribution requires variation in b. It is never valid to label your own winning bid as the competitors' price. Some exchanges return extra signals (for example, the minimum bid that would have won), so check the actual interface.

Follow the money through three ledgers

Take the position of an ad network: a company that buys opportunities to show ads from publishers (owners of apps and websites with ad slots) and resells them to advertisers (businesses that want their ads shown). Three amounts are easy to conflate: what the network collects from advertisers, what it pays publishers, and what the advertiser earns from the sales the ads produce. Two published examples pin down the first two; then we add the advertiser's ledger. The billing units below are defined in Chapter 2 and restated here at first use.

Step 1 · Who pays how much per 1,000 impressions?

An impression is one display of an ad. CPM (cost per mille) quotes a price per 1,000 impressions, so $2 CPM means $0.002 per impression. In Epom's example the network buys publisher inventory (the publisher's supply of ad impressions) at $2 CPM and sells it to advertisers at $5 CPM, keeping a $3 CPM spread. Both sides are billed per impression. Source: Epom, “Ad arbitrage” section

For every 1,000 impressions the network receives $5 and pays the publisher $2. The remaining $3 is the media spread, before servers, sales staff and other operating costs. The example says nothing about whether the advertiser's $5 bought enough business.

Step 2 · What if the advertiser pays only per conversion?

A conversion is an action the advertiser values, such as a purchase or an app install. CPC (cost per click) and CPA (cost per action) contracts bill the advertiser only when that click or action occurs, not per impression. Kevel's documentation uses 9,000 impressions, 18 billable events and $5 per event. Kevel allows the billable event to be a click or another action; we take the CPA reading and treat each event as a conversion that the contract recognizes. Source: Kevel, CPC/CPA worked example under “What Is eCPM?”

eCPM (effective CPM) restates revenue earned under any contract as revenue per 1,000 impressions:

QuestionCalculationAnswer
How much does the advertiser pay in total?18 conversions × $5$90

The contract is still $5 per conversion. The $10 eCPM converts revenue already earned into a per-1,000-impression figure so it can be compared with the price paid for impressions. It does not turn the contract into a CPM contract, and it does not guarantee that the next 9,000 impressions will earn the same.

Step 3 · Collect per conversion, pay per impression

The following combined example is constructed for this reader; neither source describes this transaction. Keep Kevel's 9,000 impressions, 18 conversions and $5 CPA, and assume the network bought those impressions at Epom's $2 CPM.

Whose ledgerCalculationAmount

This is “buy per impression, sell per conversion.” The network pays for every impression it buys but is paid only when a contractual conversion occurs. If the same $18 of impressions yields only 2 conversions, the network collects $10 and loses $8. Conversion prediction tells the network which impressions are worth buying; it cannot make conversions happen.

Step 4 · The network kept $72. Did the advertiser make money?

We cannot tell yet. We know only that the advertiser spent $90. We do not know what each extra order is worth to the advertiser, or how many of the 18 recorded conversions the ad actually caused.

Two terms separate these questions. A conversion is attributed to an ad when an agreed rule (for example, “purchase within 7 days of a click”) credits it to that ad; attribution decides what gets billed. A conversion is incremental if it would not have happened without the ad; incrementality decides what the advertiser actually gained. Teaching assumption: each incremental order leaves $8 of contribution, meaning revenue minus cost of goods, fulfillment and other variable costs, before ad spend. The attributed conversions used for billing are not the incremental conversions. Measuring the latter requires causal methods, covered in Chapter 4.

The three ledgers are now separate. The publisher counts revenue from selling impressions; the network counts collections minus media cost; the advertiser counts incremental contribution minus the ad bill. That sets up the bidding question: for impressions not yet bought, how would the network bid if it maximized its own spread, and how could this differ from the advertiser’s choice?

Extension A: why does the formula change under “ideal second-price”?

An ideal second-price auction charges the winner the competitive threshold Z instead of its own bid b; floor prices, bid increments and fees are ignored. If you bid $6 CPM and the highest competing bid is $4 CPM, first-price charges $6 CPM and ideal second-price charges $4 CPM. You win either way; only the payment differs.

For a fixed b the win rate is still b/L. Conditional on winning, Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,b), so the expected payment per win is \mathbb E[Z\mid Z\le b]=b/2. With the default \rho=1:

\begin{aligned} C(b)&=N\cdot\frac{b}{L}\cdot\frac{b}{2}=\frac{Nb^2}{2L},\\ M(b)&=\frac{N}{L}\Bigl(vb-\frac{b^2}{2}\Bigr),\qquad M'(b)=\frac{N}{L}\,(v-b),\qquad M''(b)=-\frac{N}{L}<0. \end{aligned}

Ignoring the budget, the best bid is b_0=v: bid your value, the familiar truthful-bidding result for second-price auctions. Then apply the budget and the price range:

b^*=\min\Bigl\{v,\ \sqrt{\frac{2BL}{N}},\ L\Bigr\}=\min\{0.015,\ 0.0089443\ldots,\ 0.02\}\ \to\ \$8.9443\ \text{CPM}.

With the same $2,000 budget, the win rate is about 44.72%, the advertiser bill $6,708.20, the platform gross spread $4,708.20 and the advertiser incremental contribution profit −$3,130.50. The Lagrangian condition is now b=v/(1+\mu), so the budget's shadow price rises to \mu=v/b^*-1\approx0.677. More impressions mean more attributed orders billed; with these loss-making parameters the advertiser loses more.

The comparison holds the threshold distribution and the other inputs fixed, not the advertiser bill. In reality a change of auction rule also changes competitors' bids, so this is not a prediction of market-level outcomes.

First-price and second-price auctions cost different amounts

This restates the main-line auction argument for a different objective: the network uses its CPA receipts v in place of advertiser incremental contribution w.

The following simplified decision model is original to this reader; by default every win yields a successful impression. Let Z\ge0 be the competitive threshold, the highest competing bid, which we must meet or beat to win; b\ge0 our bid; and v\ge0 the expected monetary value of winning and being shown. We win if and only if b\ge Z; ties have probability zero in this continuous model. For fixed x, F(b\mid x)=P(Z\le b\mid x) is the conditional CDF of the threshold, i.e., the win rate at bid b, and f(z\mid x)=\partial F(z\mid x)/\partial z is its density. From here on we fix x and drop it, writing F(b) and f(b); in integrals z denotes a value of Z and dF(z)=f(z)\,dz.

Bids, values and payments are in dollars per won-and-displayed opportunity; multiply by 1,000 for a CPM interface. Assumptions: the bidder is risk-neutral, maximizing expected net profit; given x, v does not depend on Z; one bid does not change later responses; there are no fees, ties or dynamic feedback; and there is no budget yet. Let \pi_1(b) and \pi_2(b) be the expected net profit per request under first-price and second-price, counting zero revenue and zero cost on a loss; primes denote derivatives in b. Formulas containing F/f apply only to interior candidates with f(b)>0; the allowed bid range, its boundaries and the global optimum must still be checked.

In a first-price auction the winner pays its own bid. Expected net profit per request is

\pi_1(b)=(v-b)F(b),\qquad \pi_1'(b)=(v-b)f(b)-F(b).

An interior optimum therefore satisfies

b=v-\frac{F(b)}{f(b)}.

This is an implicit equation, not a fixed discount. The gap F(b)/f(b) is the bid shading: bidding below value trades a lower payment per win against fewer wins. The second-order condition is \pi_1''(b)=-2f(b)+(v-b)f'(b)<0 at the candidate. A convenient sufficient condition for a unique global maximum is that F/f is nondecreasing (F log-concave): then \pi_1'(b)=f(b)\bigl[(v-b)-F(b)/f(b)\bigr] changes sign once, from positive to negative. The optimum always lies in [0,v], because any b>v earns \pi_1(b)\le0\le\pi_1(0). For uniform thresholds F(b)/f(b)=b, so b=v/2. That halving is a property of the uniform, not of first price:

Worked example

First-price bid when the threshold is the higher of two bids

Given: value v=\$0.015 per impression ($15 CPM); two competitors whose bids are independent and each \mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) with L=\$0.02 per impression, so the threshold Z is the larger of the two; first price; no budget.

  1. Threshold distribution. We win when both competing bids are at most b, so F(b)=(b/L)^2, f(b)=2b/L^2 and F(b)/f(b)=b/2 (uniform: b). \log F=2\log b-2\log L is concave, so the first-order condition has one root and it is the global maximum.
  2. First-order condition: b=v-\frac{F(b)}{f(b)}=v-\frac{b}{2}\ \Longrightarrow\ b=\frac{2v}{3}, so b=2\times0.015/3=\$0.010 per impression.
  3. Win rate F(0.010)=(0.010/0.02)^2=0.25; expected profit per request \pi_1=(0.015-0.010)\times0.25=\$0.00125.
  4. The uniform rule b=v/2 applied to this market instead: F(0.0075)=(0.0075/0.02)^2=0.140625 and \pi_1=0.0075\times0.140625=\$0.00105469.

Result: bid $10 CPM, shading one third of value instead of one half. Over 1,000,000 requests that earns $1,250; bidding $7.50 CPM earns $1,054.69, 15.6% less.

Check: neighbouring bids do worse: $9 CPM gives 0.006 × 0.2025 = $0.001215 and $11 CPM gives 0.004 × 0.3025 = $0.00121 per request. With J such competitors F=(b/L)^J and b=Jv/(J+1): more competition, less shading. Under ideal second price the optimum stays b=v whatever F is.

In an ideal second-price auction the winner pays the threshold. Expected net profit per request is

\pi_2(b)=\int_0^b(v-z)\,dF(z),\qquad \pi_2'(b)=(v-b)f(b).

\pi_2'\ge0 for b<v and \pi_2'\le0 for b>v, so under the assumptions above b=v is optimal (truthful bidding) whenever bidding v is allowed. If the threshold has no probability mass near v, nearby bids do equally well and the optimum is not unique. This does not mean every ad system should bid truthfully. GSP (generalized second-price auction) sells several ranked slots with different position values, and mechanisms with quality scores (multipliers that weight bids by predicted ad quality) must be modeled under their own rules. Real transactions also involve floor prices, fees, discrete bid increments and internal auctions (an intermediary first runs its own auction among its advertisers, then bids externally with the winner). Google Ad Exchange announced its move to first-price auctions in 2019, which is enough to show that second-price textbooks are not an operating manual for current markets. Write the two budget objectives separately

Index the bid requests in the planning period by i. For request i, b_i is the bid, v_i the expected value of winning and being shown, F_i the threshold CDF, and C_i(b_i) the expected media cost of the request, counting zero spend on a loss. B is the total media budget for the period. Sums run over all requests; “s.t.” means “subject to.” Note that C_i(b) is a per-request cost, whereas C in the optional calculator is the total over requests.

Per request, expected cost is C(b)=b\,F(b) under first-price and C(b)=\int_0^b z\,dF(z) under ideal second-price. To maximize total value within the budget:

\max_{b_i}\sum_i v_iF_i(b_i) \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_i C_i(b_i)\le B.

Attach a multiplier \lambda\ge0 to the budget constraint. The Lagrangian separates across requests, with per-request term v\,F(b)-\lambda\,C(b) (subscript dropped). \lambda is measured in value dollars per budget dollar. Under the usual regularity conditions it is the shadow price of the budget: the increase in optimal total value per extra budget dollar. Using C'(b)=F(b)+b\,f(b) for first-price and C'(b)=b\,f(b) for second-price, the interior first-order conditions with \lambda>0 and f(b)>0 are

\text{Second-price:}\quad b=\frac{v}{\lambda}, \qquad \text{First-price:}\quad b=\frac{v}{\lambda}-\frac{F(b)}{f(b)}.

To maximize net profit under the same budget instead, solve \max_{b_i}\sum_i\bigl[v_iF_i(b_i)-C_i(b_i)\bigr] s.t. \sum_i C_i(b_i)\le B. The per-request term becomes v\,F(b)-(1+\mu)\,C(b), where \mu\ge0 is the budget multiplier for this objective: the 1 stands for the cost itself and \mu for the extra cost of budget scarcity. Under the same interior conditions:

\text{Second-price:}\quad b=\frac{v}{1+\mu}, \qquad \text{First-price:}\quad b=\frac{v}{1+\mu}-\frac{F(b)}{f(b)}.

Complementary slackness, \mu\bigl(\sum_i C_i(b_i)-B\bigr)=0, pins down \mu. A slack budget gives \mu=0, which recovers the budget-free profit optimum; otherwise \mu is set so that expected spend equals B. In the uniform example with B = $2,000, \mu\approx0.186 under first-price and \mu\approx0.677 under second-price (worked examples in the next section).

The difference between the objectives is economic, not notational. If both objectives exhaust the budget at the same bids, comparing the conditions gives \lambda=1+\mu\ge1. But a total-value bidder can end up with \lambda<1; its second-price bid v/\lambda then exceeds v, so it knowingly buys impressions that cost more than they are worth, because its objective counts value, not profit. With a slack budget the total-value objective has no reason to economize at all: it buys every opportunity with positive value, and “set \lambda=0 in the division formula” is not a valid step. Budget constraints, ROAS constraints (a floor on return on ad spend; see Chapter 2) and CPA constraints (a cap on average cost per action) are also different problems. Transplanting multipliers between papers often produces strategies that look alike but mean different things.

All of this optimizes expectations under a known threshold distribution. If the distribution is unknown, it must be learned; if only win/loss outcomes are observed, the problem changes again (see Learning the auction prices). Recent research on first-price bidding with budgets distinguishes these feedback regimes, and its guarantees do not automatically transfer to production markets. With the $2,000 budget the total-value objective also bids up to the cap, so the two objectives yield the same bid. More generally, suppose both objectives' optima exhaust the same budget B on the same feasible set. On the boundary \sum_i C_i=B, net profit equals total value minus the constant B, so both objectives select the same point on that boundary, even with heterogeneous opportunities. We make no claim about other tied optima off the boundary. This is not a general equivalence: a net-profit objective may deliberately underspend while a total-value objective keeps buying, and any change to the feasible set or the constraints requires solving again. The budget here also constrains expected spend only; realized requests and payments are random, and enforcing a hard cap on every realized path is a separate system responsibility.

Budget pacing is online control, not even spending

Pacing controls the rate of spend over time. The simplest illustrative update is

\mu_{t+1}=\max\{0,\ \mu_t+\eta\,(c_t-s_t)\}.

Here t indexes control periods (for example, minutes). \mu_t\ge0 is the budget multiplier in period t, not the bid; the bid is derived from it, for example b=v/(1+\mu_t) under ideal second-price, or b=v/(1+\mu_t)-F(b)/f(b) under first-price, which is v/\bigl(2(1+\mu_t)\bigr) for uniform thresholds. c_t is actual spend and s_t target spend in period t. The step size \eta>0, in units of 1/dollar, converts a spend error into a multiplier change, and \max\{0,\cdot\} keeps the multiplier nonnegative. Overspending raises \mu and lowers bids; underspending does the reverse. The update is projected subgradient descent on the Lagrangian dual: by the envelope theorem the dual objective's derivative in \mu is budget minus spend, so a step against it moves \mu by +\eta\,(c_t-s_t). It sets the direction of control, not stability: delayed billing data, traffic cycles and interacting control loops can make it oscillate. Feedback control in RTB advertising
