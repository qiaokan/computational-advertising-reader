Products and cash flow

In this chapter

  • Specify any ad product on five distinct dimensions: context, creative, transaction type, billing event, objective.
  • Price one campaign as CPM, CPC or CPA with the same expected payment, and say who absorbs click risk and conversion risk under each.
  • Trace $100 of advertiser spend through net and gross revenue recognition, and see why an 80% and a 20% gross margin can hide identical gross profit.
  • Decide whether to scale a budget by comparing the return on the additional spend with the break-even return (one over the contribution margin), and correct attribution-based ROAS for over- or under-crediting.

Specify an ad product along five distinct dimensions

People often list “search, feed, RTB, CPC, performance advertising” as if they were categories of one kind. They are not. Search names the user context (a results page for a typed query); a feed is another context, a scrolling stream of content with ads inserted between items. RTB (real-time bidding) names a transaction mechanism: each individual ad opportunity is auctioned in milliseconds while the page or app loads. CPC (cost per click) names the billing event. Performance advertising names the optimization goal: the advertiser pays for, and judges results by, measurable downstream actions such as installs or purchases, as opposed to brand advertising, which buys reach and awareness. If you force these into one mutually exclusive taxonomy, the objective function you build downstream will be wrong.

A product can combine choices from several dimensions. Take a rewarded video: an ad the user opts into inside a game in exchange for an in-game reward such as extra lives. The publisher can sell that slot through a first-price auction (the winner pays its own bid; see Prediction and bidding) and be paid per impression, while the advertiser's goal is a target D7 ROAS. ROAS (return on ad spend) is revenue divided by ad spend, defined precisely below; the D7 version counts revenue from the first seven days after a user is acquired. These descriptions do not conflict, because each belongs to a different dimension. Note also that the reward does not require the user to install the advertised app, so a user who is willing to finish the video does not necessarily intend to buy anything. Google AdMob rewarded ads documentation

Contexts differ in how intent becomes visible. In search, the query states part of the user's current need. A feed has to infer opportunities from context, past behavior, and how users have responded to creatives. Open-web and in-app advertising must additionally join supply, user identity and conversion events across companies. These differences change how hard prediction is and what the product can do. They do not show that any context inherently earns a higher incremental return.

Advertisers buy a delivery service under constraints

A campaign is the advertiser's unit of purchase. To run one, the platform needs at least six groups of inputs:

  • business goals and the conversion events that measure them;
  • budget and flight dates (the start and end of delivery);
  • permitted audiences and geographies;
  • creatives and landing pages;
  • attribution rules and value postbacks (server-to-server messages that report each conversion, and its value, back to the ad platform);
  • billing and settlement terms.

Below the campaign, ad groups usually carry finer targeting, bidding or creative combinations, but the hierarchy differs across platforms. Do not treat one platform's menu structure as a universal data model.

“Optimize for purchases” is still underspecified. Does a purchase mean an order placed, a payment completed, or an order still valid after the refund period? Is value the merchandise revenue, the merchant's commission, or contribution profit? Does D7 revenue mean revenue accumulated over the first seven days, or revenue on day seven only? In this reader, D7 ROAS uses the half-open window [t_0,\ t_0+168\text{ h}), where t_0 is the moment of acquisition (for app campaigns, the install). It equals the cohort's cumulative attributed revenue in that window divided by the ad spend that acquired the cohort. The metric is only valid once the window has fully elapsed for every user in the cohort. Other platforms draw the day boundaries differently, so redefine the metric for each interface.

Changing one field definition can change offline training labels, delivery behavior and reports without any change to the model. Advertiser onboarding is therefore more than a configuration form. Events, currency, time zone, deduplication keys, attribution windows, refunds and target constraints together must form an executable contract.

Creative is a decision variable too. The same user can click and retain very differently depending on the creative shown. A pure “user value model” ignores creative supply, review latency, creative fatigue (response decays as the same audience sees a creative repeatedly) and landing-page quality. A platform that reliably finds high-CTR creatives may simply be rewarding clickbait, so the product needs user-experience and downstream-quality metrics as guardrails: constraints that must not degrade while the primary metric is optimized.

Publishers sell attention and pay for it with product quality

Publishers and app developers track revenue per thousand ad requests (often called RPM or request eCPM). They also track fill rate (the share of ad requests that receive an ad), ad latency, ad load (how many ads are shown per session or per unit of content) and user retention. If every filled request renders exactly one impression, request RPM equals fill rate times impression eCPM, where eCPM is revenue per thousand impressions (defined below). Raising revenue per ad at the cost of users opening the app less often erodes future supply. The seller therefore optimizes a combination of ad revenue, user experience and future usage, not each auction in isolation.

Guaranteed contracts add a further layer. Suppose a publisher has committed to deliver one million qualifying impressions this month. Each qualifying opportunity can either count toward the contract or be sold on the spot market (the real-time auction). The comparison is between spot revenue and the shadow value of the opportunity: the Lagrange multiplier on the contract's delivery constraint, i.e. the marginal value of one more qualifying impression toward avoiding an under-delivery penalty. Supply forecasting, admission control (deciding whether to accept a new guaranteed contract given forecast supply), under-delivery penalties and allocation across overlapping contracts all enter this decision. Google Ad Manager draws exactly this line: programmatic guaranteed reserves and commits inventory, while a preferred deal carries no volume guarantee. Roles and money flows

The main functional roles are:

  • DSP (demand-side platform): buys inventory and optimizes delivery on behalf of advertisers.
  • SSP (supply-side platform): manages and sells a publisher's inventory.
  • Ad exchange: the marketplace that runs auctions matching buyers to sellers.
  • Ad server: selects which ad to show for a request, delivers it and logs the result.
  • Ad network: a company that bundles several of these roles, typically buying from publishers and selling to advertisers.
  • Mobile ad mediation: software in the app that decides which of several demand sources fills each ad request. Each demand source usually ships an SDK (software development kit, a library embedded in the app). Mediation either runs a bidding auction, in which all sources bid simultaneously, or a waterfall, which calls demand sources one at a time in a configured priority order until one fills.
  • MMP (mobile measurement partner), e.g. Adjust or AppsFlyer: sits in the feedback loop and provides measurement and attribution (crediting an install or purchase to an ad; see Attribution, experiments and data). Money does not pass through the MMP.

Sources: IAB (Interactive Advertising Bureau) 2025 commerce media white paper, glossary appendix, Adjust integration documentation.

Keep billing events and optimization objectives apart

The common pricing units are:

  • CPM (cost per mille): price per thousand impressions. An impression is one successful display of an ad.
  • CPC (cost per click): price per click.
  • CPI (cost per install): price per app install.
  • CPA (cost per action, or per acquisition): price per contractually defined action, such as a purchase.
  • CTR (click-through rate): clicks divided by impressions.
  • CVR (conversion rate): conversions divided by a denominator you must state. CVR per click and conversion probability per impression are different quantities; the second equals CTR times the first for post-click conversions.
  • eCPM (effective CPM): some revenue or value restated per thousand impressions, \text{eCPM}=1000\times\text{amount}/\text{impressions}. It is a unit conversion for comparing across contracts, not necessarily the price on any invoice.

Use the running example from the roadmap chapter. An ad network buys impressions in auctions and charges the advertiser a=\$30 per billable conversion. An impression is clicked with probability p_{\text{click}}=0.01 (the CTR), and a click converts with probability p_{\text{conv}}=0.05 (post-click CVR), so a billable conversion follows an impression with probability q=0.01\times0.05=0.0005. The network's expected revenue per impression is v=q\,a=0.0005\times\$30=\$0.015, an eCPM of $15. That does not mean the network bids $15 CPM. The optional network-spread appendix derives bids for a network maximizing its own spread. Those bids use its CPA receipts and are not advertiser recommendations. The main bidding lesson instead uses the advertiser’s incremental contribution and an explicitly different direct-buying contract. Nor is the advertiser billed per impression: it still pays $30 per conversion. Valuation, bid and payment are three separate quantities.

The billing event decides who bears response risk: the risk that paid-for impressions yield fewer clicks or conversions than forecast. The network's media cost is set in the auction regardless of how it bills, so any shortfall the advertiser does not pay for comes out of the network's margin.

Worked example

One campaign, three billing events

Given: a hypothetical campaign in which the network has already won and paid for I=1{,}000{,}000 impressions (not the N bid requests above); forecast p_{\text{click}}=0.01, p_{\text{conv}}=0.05, q=0.0005; CPA price a=\$30 per conversion. Set each price so the expected payment at the forecast is the same. Outcomes are independent across impressions.

  1. Prices with equal expected payment per impression, q\,a=\$0.015: per conversion, per click, per 1,000 impressions, \begin{aligned} \text{CPA} &= a = \$30\\ \text{CPC} &= p_{\text{conv}}\,a = 0.05\times\$30 = \$1.50\\ \text{CPM} &= 1000\,q\,a = \$15 \end{aligned}
  2. Expected campaign payment, identical by construction: CPM 1{,}000\times\$15; CPC 10{,}000\text{ clicks}\times\$1.50; CPA 500\text{ conversions}\times\$30; each \$15{,}000.
  3. Sampling noise with the forecast rates correct. CPM: payment is fixed once impressions are delivered, standard deviation $0. CPC: \$1.50\sqrt{I p_{\text{click}}(1-p_{\text{click}})}=\$1.50\sqrt{9{,}900}\approx\$149.25 (1.0% of the mean). CPA: \$30\sqrt{I q(1-q)}=\$30\sqrt{499.75}\approx\$670.65 (4.5% of the mean).
  4. Forecast error, which dominates sampling noise at this scale. If the true CTR is 0.005, there are 5,000 clicks and 250 conversions: the advertiser pays $15,000 under CPM, $7,500 under CPC, $7,500 under CPA. If instead the true CVR is 0.025, there are 10,000 clicks and 250 conversions: it pays $15,000, $15,000 and $7,500.
  5. Advertiser's realized cost per conversion in those two scenarios: CPM $60 and $60; CPC $30 and $60; CPA $30 and $30.

Result: same expected payment, different risk bearer. Under CPM the advertiser bears both click and conversion risk; under CPC the network bears click risk and the advertiser conversion risk; under CPA the network bears both.

Check: the advertiser's payment scales with the ratio of true to forecast rates for exactly the events it pays for: CPC payment = $15,000 × (true CTR ÷ 0.01); CPA payment = $15,000 × (true q ÷ 0.0005). Both scenarios halve q, so CPA pays $7,500 in both.

Equal expected payments are a modelling convenience. A party that absorbs variance and forecast error charges for it, so in practice CPA and CPC prices carry a risk premium relative to the CPM equivalent. Shifting the billing event also shifts incentives: under CPA the network's revenue depends on the advertiser's conversion definition and reporting; under CPM the advertiser must police impression quality itself.

Define the return metrics precisely:

  • ROAS (return on ad spend) = revenue under an agreed definition (usually attributed revenue, i.e. revenue credited to ads by the attribution rules) ÷ ad spend. It is often quoted as a percentage: 300% means 3.0.
  • iROAS (incremental ROAS) = incremental revenue ÷ ad spend, where incremental revenue is the revenue that would not have occurred without the ads. Keep the two numerators distinct.
  • tCPA (target CPA): the average cost per conversion that an automated bidding algorithm tries to achieve across a campaign.
  • tROAS (target ROAS): the ROAS that an automated bidding algorithm tries to achieve.

Optimizing toward a CPA target does not mean being billed per conversion, and it does not guarantee that every conversion costs less than the target. Google's tCPA, for example, sets bids automatically to hit an average conversion-cost target, while the advertiser is typically still billed per click. Google tCPA documentation

LTV (lifetime value: the total value a customer generates over a horizon) also needs a definition before use. State at least: the horizon; whether it measures revenue or contribution profit; whether refunds are deducted; whether it is discounted; and whether acquisition ad spend has already been subtracted. Treating revenue LTV as a profit valuation systematically overstates what you can afford to bid.

Three ledgers and the limits of scaling

Each party keeps its own ledger. The advertiser's ledger records the business value gained and what was paid for it. The network's ledger records what it collects from advertisers and what it pays publishers and service providers. The publisher's ledger records what it earns from selling inventory and what it loses in user experience and future usage. All three reports can show growth while total real economic value does not rise.

The platform's gross margin, the advertiser's campaign return and the payoff from further scaling are three different questions. A platform can hold an 80% gross margin while the advertiser earns less with every extra dollar it spends. Below, one ad budget is entered into two income statements, and then we examine how scaling changes each side's returns. Throughout, amounts are for one fixed period, “spend” means the amount billed to the advertiser in that period, and refunds, credits and taxes are ignored.

1. What is the denominator of the platform's 80% gross margin?

Four accounting terms first.

  • Take rate: the amount the platform keeps after paying the publisher, divided by total advertiser spend (sometimes called gross billings).
  • Gross vs net revenue recognition: a platform that recognizes revenue gross books the full advertiser spend as revenue and the publisher payout as a cost. One that recognizes revenue net books only the amount it keeps.
  • Cost of revenue: costs directly incurred to deliver the service, such as serving infrastructure, data and payment processing. Under gross recognition it also includes the publisher payout, often called TAC (traffic acquisition cost).
  • Gross profit = recognized revenue − cost of revenue. Gross margin = gross profit ÷ recognized revenue.
Worked example

$100 of spend under net and gross recognition

Given, for one period: advertiser spend S=\$100; publisher payout P=\$75; the platform's direct service costs D=\$5 (serving, data, payment processing), all classified as cost of revenue. Compare two platforms with identical cash flows, one that qualifies for net recognition and one that qualifies for gross recognition (the control test below decides which; neither chooses).

  1. Take rate, a cash-flow ratio identical for both: (S-P)/S=(\$100-\$75)/\$100=25\%.
  2. Net recognition: revenue =S-P=\$25; cost of revenue =D=\$5; gross profit =\$25-\$5=\$20; gross margin =\$20/\$25=80\%. The $75 payout appears in neither line.
  3. Gross recognition: revenue =S=\$100; cost of revenue =P+D=\$75+\$5=\$80 (the $75 is TAC); gross profit =\$100-\$80=\$20; gross margin =\$20/\$100=20\%.
  4. Gross profit per dollar of advertiser spend, under both: \$20/\$100=20\%.

Result: same cash, same $20 gross profit; reported gross margin is 80% under net recognition and 20% under gross.

Check: the two margins share the numerator and their denominators are S-P and S, so gross margin (gross) = take rate × gross margin (net) =0.25\times0.80=0.20.

The 80% is measured against the $25 the platform recognizes: each recognized dollar leaves $0.80 after cost of revenue. It does not mean the platform keeps 80% of the ad budget, nor that 80% is final profit; R&D, sales, taxes and the cost of capital still come out. Before comparing two ad companies' gross margins, align their revenue recognition and cost classification. Here 80% versus 20% reflects no difference in efficiency at all.

Calling the platform an “agent” in the contract does not by itself justify net recognition. IFRS 15, the international revenue standard, and ASC 606, its US GAAP counterpart, apply the same test. The entity identifies each specified good or service promised to the customer (for an ad platform, typically the ad placement) and asks whether it controls that good or service before it is transferred to the customer. If it does, it is a principal and recognizes revenue at the gross amount of consideration; if not, it is an agent and recognizes only its fee or commission, i.e. the consideration net of what it passes to the other party. Three indicators support, but do not replace, the control assessment: primary responsibility for fulfilling the promise; inventory risk before or after transfer (for example, buying impressions up front with no matching advertiser order); and discretion in setting the price. One company can be a principal for some offerings and an agent for others. IFRS Interpretations Committee on principal versus agent, Summary of IFRS 15 principal-versus-agent requirements

2. Why does the advertiser's profit fall while its sales rise?

Switch to the advertiser's ledger. Its revenue, such as the $300 of incremental sales in the table below, is what consumers pay the advertiser for goods or services. It has nothing to do with the $25 of revenue the platform recognizes.

Let m be the advertiser's contribution margin before advertising: the fraction of each incremental sales dollar left after goods, fulfillment and other variable non-advertising costs, before ad spend and fixed costs. Take m=40\%, so each additional $100 of sales leaves $40 to pay for advertising and contribute to profit. Assume m is constant across volume (variable costs are linear in sales).

Below, “profit” always means incremental contribution profit after ad spend (incremental sales × m − ad spend), not the company's net income. Each column's revenue is assumed to be truly incremental, measured over the same observation period with the same revenue definition.

3. Average iROAS hides the loss on the additional budget

Let C be ad spend, R the incremental sales revenue it generates, and m the contribution margin defined above. Let \Delta C be additional spend and \Delta R the additional incremental revenue it generates. Here “marginal iROAS” means the finite-increment ratio \Delta R/\Delta C; only in the limit \Delta C\to0 does it become a derivative.

Contribution profit is mR-C, which for C>0 and m>0 is non-negative exactly when R/C\ge 1/m. So the break-even iROAS is 1/m: 2.5 at m=40\%, 5 at m=20\%, 1.25 at m=80\%. If m\le0, no iROAS breaks even.

Worked example

Should the advertiser add the extra $50?

Given: m=0.4; incremental revenue R=\$300 at C=\$100 and R=\$375 at C=\$150, same window and revenue definition.

  1. Break-even iROAS: 1/m=1/0.4=2.5.
  2. Marginal iROAS of the increment: \Delta R/\Delta C=(\$375-\$300)/(\$150-\$100)=\$75/\$50=1.5.
  3. Profit change from the increment: \Delta\Pi=m\,\Delta R-\Delta C=0.4\times\$75-\$50=-\$20.
  4. Average iROAS of the whole budget: \$375/\$150=2.5=1/m, so contribution profit at $150 is 0.4\times\$375-\$150=\$0.

Result: do not add the $50. It lowers contribution profit by $20, although the cumulative iROAS of 2.5 still meets the break-even threshold.

Check: profit at $100 is 0.4\times\$300-\$100=\$20 and at $150 is $0, a change of −$20. Equivalently, the increment needed \Delta R\ge2.5\times\$50=\$125 and delivered $75.

The cumulative 2.5 sits at break-even only because the early high-return spend subsidizes the late low-return spend. The trading analogue: a scaled-up strategy's marginal capacity may not cover its costs while the book's average return hides the loss from the scale-up itself.

Optional derivation of break-even ROAS and a fitted response curve

If ROAS is computed from attributed revenue A instead, you cannot apply 1/m directly. For A>0, define the attribution-to-incrementality ratio \kappa=R/A (true incremental revenue ÷ attributed revenue). Contribution profit is then m\kappa A-C, so for \kappa>0 and m>0 the attributed ROAS at which incremental contribution breaks even is

\frac{A}{C}=\frac{1}{m\kappa}.

The formula treats \kappa as constant over the spend range under consideration; in practice the marginal \kappa of new spend can differ from the average. \kappa must be estimated separately, usually from a randomized experiment; the attribution report cannot reveal it. It is not necessarily below 1: attribution can also miss genuinely caused sales (for example, conversions on another device or outside the attribution window).

More generally, write incremental revenue as a function of spend, R(C): the revenue difference relative to not advertising, so R(0)=0. Let \Pi(C) be contribution profit after ad spend and R'(C) the marginal incremental revenue per additional dollar. Hold m fixed and ignore other volume-dependent costs:

\begin{aligned} \Pi(C) &= m\,R(C) - C \\ \Delta\Pi &= m\,[R(C+\Delta C) - R(C)] - \Delta C \\ \text{scale by a finite }\Delta C\text{ iff } &\ \Delta\Pi > 0 \\ \text{positive marginal profit iff } &\ m\,R'(C) > 1 \end{aligned}

At equality the derivative alone gives no gain from an infinitesimal increase. Use the finite-change formula for a proposed spend increase. Let R be differentiable and concave, with no other constraint (such as a budget cap) binding. If m\,R'(0)\le1, not advertising is optimal: C^*=0. If m\,R'(0)>1 and m\,R' eventually falls below 1, the optimum is interior and satisfies m\,R'(C^*)=1; the example's data satisfy both conditions, as the bracket below shows. The three points R(0)=0, R(100)=300, R(150)=375 prove that the $150 plan is less profitable than the $100 plan, but not that $100 is optimal. Concavity adds a bracket: the chord slopes 3 on [0,100] and 1.5 on [100,150] give R'(0)\ge3>2.5 and R'(150)\le1.5<2.5, so 0<C^*<150. Since R'(100) can lie anywhere in [1.5,3], the data cannot say whether C^* is above or below $100. Pinning it down requires a functional form.

Worked example

An assumed concave curve through the three points

Given: assume R(C)=\alpha C-\beta C^2 (dollars), the simplest concave form with R(0)=0, fitted through R(100)=\$300 and R(150)=\$375; m=0.4. This is an illustration, not an estimate.

  1. Fit: 100\alpha-10{,}000\beta=300 and 150\alpha-22{,}500\beta=375. Subtracting 1.5 times the first from the second gives -7{,}500\beta=-75, so \beta=0.01 and \alpha=4: R(C)=4C-0.01C^2, increasing for C<\$200.
  2. Marginal and average iROAS: R'(C)=4-0.02C; R(C)/C=4-0.01C. The marginal falls twice as fast as the average.
  3. Optimum: m\,R'(C^*)=1\Rightarrow 4-0.02C^*=2.5\Rightarrow C^*=\$75. Then R(75)=\$300-\$56.25=\$243.75 and \Pi(75)=0.4\times\$243.75-\$75=\$22.50.
  4. Average iROAS reaches 1/m where 4-0.01C=2.5, i.e. at C=\$150, exactly where \Pi=0.

Result: under this curve the optimal budget is $75, with profit $22.50; the $100 plan earns $20 and the $150 plan $0. The average iROAS at the optimum is 3.25, so a “healthy” average says nothing about whether you are at the optimum.

Check: for a quadratic the chord slope equals the derivative at the interval midpoint, so the chord on [100,150] is R'(125)=4-2.5=1.5, matching the previous example; and C^*=75 lies inside the bracket (0,150).

Scaling up raises platform gross profit by $10 and cuts the advertiser's contribution profit by $20. This does not prove the platform harms customers deliberately; customer retention, competition and the prospect of future budgets all constrain it. It does prove that growth in the platform's current-period revenue, a high platform gross margin and growth in customer profit do not automatically move together.

Suppose the system objective is “maximize sales subject to cumulative iROAS ≥ 2.5.” The $150 plan satisfies the constraint, exactly at the boundary (the constraint is inclusive), and has higher sales, so the system chooses it. Maximizing advertiser contribution profit would choose the $100 plan instead. An algorithm can execute its objective flawlessly and still produce a commercially bad outcome.

5. The strongest assumption: revenue must be truly incremental

“The platform attributes the order to the ad” and “the order would not have happened without the ad” are different claims. Google's documentation likewise separates standard attributed conversions from incremental conversions measured in experiments. Google Conversion Lift measurement definition

Suppose attributed revenue is $300 but the revenue the ads actually caused is only $200. Then the first $100 of spend already loses money:

\text{incremental contribution profit} = \$200\times0.4-\$100 = -\$20.

The \kappa formula gives the same verdict: \kappa=200/300=2/3, so the break-even attributed ROAS is 1/(0.4\times2/3)=3.75, above the reported 3.0.

To judge a platform like AppLovin's Axon (see the Axon chapter), the useful question is: under the same measurement definition, contribution margin and budget, how much does it raise the customer's true incremental contribution profit, and how much of that advantage survives as the budget grows? The platform's accounting gross margin cannot answer it.

Your trading experience transfers directly to auditable P&L, risk limits and capacity curves. One intuition does not transfer: that an impression is a homogeneous asset you can store and resell without loss. An impression perishes the instant it is served, its value depends on the advertiser and the creative, feedback arrives with a delay, and the counterfactual (what would have happened without the ad) is never directly observed. What gets called statistical arbitrage in advertising usually carries unhedged behavioral, attribution and settlement risk.
