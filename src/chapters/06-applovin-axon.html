AppLovin and Axon

This chapter sorts claims into three bins and labels each one. Public fact: stated in an SEC filing or in AppLovin's own documentation (a company statement, not an independent audit). Inference: our reasoning from those facts. Unknown: not determinable from public sources. All pages were retrieved on November 11, 2026.

What public sources confirm about the products

Vocabulary first. AppLovin is the ad-tech company under discussion (Chapter 1). A publisher is an app developer that sells ad slots inside its app; an advertiser buys them; an impression is one ad display. An SDK (software development kit) is a library compiled into the app; ad networks reach in-app inventory through SDKs. Mediation software sits in the publisher's app and decides, for each ad request, which of many demand sources fills it (Chapter 2). Under a waterfall, mediation calls ad networks one after another in a configured priority order; under in-app bidding, networks submit real-time bids for the same request and the highest bid wins. MAX is AppLovin's mediation product, which the company calls its in-app bidding solution. Axon is AppLovin's ad prediction and bidding engine; Axon Ads Manager is the advertiser-facing product it powers.

Public fact. AppLovin describes Axon as an AI advertising engine whose predictive models evaluate potential impressions. AppLovin then bids on each impression according to the value Axon assigns to it for a given advertiser, aiming at that advertiser's return-on-ad-spend (ROAS) goal. The 2025 Form 10-K says Axon Ads Manager generates substantially all of AppLovin's revenue. Axon official description, AppLovin 2025 10-K

Public fact. MAX's partner documentation describes a unified first-price auction. The MAX auction server merges real-time bids with the non-bidding networks in the publisher's waterfall. After the auction, the app loads the winning ad. If the load succeeds, MAX sends the winner a win notice; if it fails, MAX sends a loss notice. MAX calls the winner's billing URL only after the ad renders. MAX auction flow

Do not generalize this sequence. It is MAX's interface semantics. OpenRTB, the IAB Tech Lab's real-time bidding protocol, has analogous win and billing callbacks (nurl, burl), but each exchange decides when they fire and what they commit the buyer to (Chapter 5). MAX's choices do not redefine those terms for other OpenRTB integrations.

Public fact. The 10-K states that AppLovin is an agent, not a principal, in its advertising arrangements: it does not control the inventory before it passes to the advertiser and is not primarily responsible for delivering it. Under US GAAP an agent reports revenue net, so reported revenue is advertiser payments minus amounts paid or payable to publishers. Revenue is recognized when an impression is delivered (impression-based arrangements) or when a specified action such as a click or install occurs (action-based arrangements). The price itself is set dynamically from the advertiser's campaign goals, not as a fixed price per impression or per action. Revenue recognition note, AppLovin 2025 10-K

Inference. Because revenue is net, you cannot recover total advertiser spend, and therefore cannot compute the platform's take rate (amount retained as a share of advertiser spend, Chapter 2), from reported revenue alone. Likewise, "MAX mediation is free for publishers" on the MAX website does not mean MAX is free to everyone in the chain. The same FAQ says bidding networks pay AppLovin a fee on the ad revenue they generate, and the 10-K says MAX revenue is "a percentage of client spend." MAX website FAQ, AppLovin 2025 10-K

Seeing data is not the same as being allowed to train on it

Public fact (company statement). AppLovin's Axon page lists the inputs it says Axon uses: device and network information; engagement data about apps, ads and other digital properties, collected by AppLovin or shared by partners; advertisers' own user data, if they choose to share it; and win/loss notices from MAX, which every bidder receives for the impressions it bid on. The page also says AppLovin does not collect, and its models do not ingest, other bidders' bid information or other bidders' user data. Axon data description

Public fact. Adjust is AppLovin's MMP (mobile measurement partner): a third party that advertisers use to attribute installs and in-app events to the ads that drove them (Chapter 2, Chapter 4). The 10-K says Adjust is operated by a wholly owned subsidiary and that data generated by Adjust's services is not shared with AppLovin or used to optimize its recommendation engine (the 10-K's term for Axon) unless a customer directs it. Separately, AppLovin sold its Apps business, its own portfolio of mobile games, to Tripledot; the sale closed on June 30, 2025. AppLovin 2025 10-K

These are the company's own statements and disclosures. Nobody outside the company has audited the data pipeline in public.

Inference. The phrase "data flywheel" bundles four separate propositions. Each has a different evidentiary status:

Owning a measurement company does not mean owning all of its customers' data for training. Merging the four propositions into one "flywheel" assumes the competitive advantage that the argument is supposed to prove.

Unknown. Public materials do not reveal Axon's current model architecture, parameter count, feature coverage, training frequency, loss function or budget controller. Nor can they separate how much of a given quarter's growth came from the model, the creatives, supply access, customer mix or attribution rules. The architecture diagrams and algorithms elsewhere in this reader are tools for understanding the problem, not a reverse-engineering of Axon.

How a prediction advantage becomes retained profit

Start from a standard result: for a single decision-maker, the optimal value of extra information is nonnegative. Let \Pi(\mathcal I) be the set of feasible policies (same objective, actions and budget constraints) that are measurable with respect to information \mathcal I. Then

\mathcal I_{\text{old}}\subseteq\mathcal I_{\text{new}}\;\Rightarrow\;\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{old}})\subseteq\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{new}})\;\Rightarrow\;\max_{\pi\in\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{new}})}\mathbb E[u(\pi)]\ \ge\ \max_{\pi\in\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{old}})}\mathbb E[u(\pi)].

The new decision-maker can always ignore the extra information and replay the old policy. The result needs every one of these premises:

  • Competitors' strategies and the distribution of clearing prices stay fixed.
  • The objective, the action set and constraints such as budget are unchanged.
  • Acquiring and using the information costs nothing.
  • The new information is available at decision time and refines the old (the old signal can be recovered from the new one).
  • Conditional valuations are accurate, and the policy actually optimizes on them.

A higher offline AUC (ranking quality on held-out logs) establishes none of this. It does not show that the new information set contains the old one, that probabilities are calibrated, or that decisions are optimal. Still less does it show that the gain survives once the market reacts.

Inference. Suppose a new model finds more valuable opportunities. It can stop buying low-value impressions, and it can bid more for high-value ones. Bidding more raises publisher revenue. Competitors and publishers' floor prices (the minimum price a seller accepts, Chapter 3) keep adjusting, and advertisers may push for better pricing. How the resulting rent is split depends on whether the information is exclusive, whether access to it is stable, whether the model generalizes, whether customers have alternatives, and how the platform charges. Both "a model lead turns entirely into profit" and "auctions compete away every advantage" overstate the case.

To test for a moat, do not ask whether a competitor can build a similar model. Ask whether, on the same supply surface (the inventory one can bid on), with comparable data permissions, label maturity windows (how long one waits before treating a conversion label as final) and budget constraints, new advertisers and additional budget keep producing incremental contribution. Model, measurement, supply access, creatives, execution cost and customer relationships all have to enter the explanation together. Correct for survivorship bias too: a showcase of the surviving high-return customers proves nothing about the rest.

Inference (generic, not a claim about AppLovin's practices). If a platform subsidizes advertisers, for example with credits or discounted pricing, the subsidy buys room for trial and error. It does not buy unbiased labels (the conversion and revenue events used as training targets). Suppose labels come only from subsidized customers, cheap traffic or high-intent retargeting (showing ads to users who have already interacted with the advertiser, and who would often convert anyway). Then the learned relationships may not transfer to the broader market once full pricing applies. The early metrics to check are customer retention after subsidies end, the quality of matured labels the platform is permitted to use, calibration within each segment, and the marginal profit curve. Event counts alone say little.

Three problems that attractive numbers hide

Definitions for this section. pCTR is the model's predicted click probability for an impression. Clearing CPM is 1,000 × media cost / impressions: what the buyer actually paid, per thousand impressions. Publisher eCPM is 1,000 × publisher revenue / impressions. A cohort is the set of users acquired by a campaign in a given period. Attributed revenue is revenue that the attribution rule credits to the ad; it is not the same as incremental revenue, the revenue that would not have occurred without the ad (Chapter 4). For a cohort C with acquisition spend S_C, where user u was acquired at time t_u,

\text{D7 ROAS}(C)=\frac{\sum_{u\in C}R_u\big([t_u,\;t_u+168\,\text{h})\big)}{S_C},

where R_u(\cdot) is attributed revenue from user u in the half-open window. Compute it only after every user's 7-day window has fully elapsed. It remains an attributed return, not an incremental one.

  1. Higher pCTR with flat ROAS. If pCTR rises, clearing CPM rises with it and D7 ROAS stays flat, the model may simply be buying more clickable traffic at a higher price. Decompose the change into price and traffic mix before declaring a win on pCTR or offline AUC.
  2. Higher publisher eCPM. If MAX publisher eCPM rises, publishers earn more per thousand impressions. Total publisher revenue also depends on impression volume. Neither number shows that advertisers gained matching incremental profit; higher prices can mean the opposite.
  3. Subsidized growth. If subsidies bring in more advertisers and conversions, but retention falls once full pricing applies, the subsidy may have bought a training distribution that cannot be reproduced.

For a would-be entrant, technical replicability is only one dimension. The questions that need evidence are: which labels you are permitted to obtain, which supply you can access, how you identify each customer's incrementality, under what constraints marginal returns stay positive, and how long those conditions last.
