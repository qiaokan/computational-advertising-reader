The gist. An ad system turns a stream of one-off chances to show an ad into money, under contracts that say who pays whom for which event. Five pieces interact: a model guesses what each impression will lead to, a contract says what is billed, an auction and a budget decide what is bought, real-time systems carry out and record each decision, and experiments check whether the ads caused any sales.

Five layers decide what happens to every impression

An ad system prices, allocates and settles one-off impression opportunities while its feedback is delayed and incomplete. An impression opportunity is one chance to show one ad to one user in one slot at one moment. Five layers interact, and each has a chapter:

  • A model estimates a conditional expectation, such as the probability that this impression leads to a purchase (Prediction and data).
  • The product contract decides who pays whom for which event, and so who bears which risk (Products and cash flow).
  • Auctions and budgets decide which opportunities are bought, and at what price (Auctions and bidding).
  • Systems execute each decision before its deadline and record it exactly once (Real-time systems).
  • Experiments are the only layer that tests whether the advertising caused sales that would not otherwise have happened (Measurement).

Profit depends on all five; a gain in one layer can be undone by another. The landscape places them in the industry: ad formats, who sells to whom, and where the money is. The case study, AppLovin and Axon, uses three product names: Axon is AppLovin's prediction and bidding engine; MAX is its mediation product, which runs an auction among ad networks for each ad slot in a publisher's app; Adjust is a mobile measurement partner (MMP) that AppLovin owns; MMPs are normally independent third parties that record app installs and in-app events and credit them to ads.

Two reading paths

Read the gist path first: it covers the whole system in about three hours. It reads chapters 1–3 and the case study. Collapsed calculations are available when you want to work through the numbers. For chapters 4–6 it reads only the gist level: the “In this chapter” box, the gist paragraph, and the first sentence of each section, which states that section's claim. The full day reads everything; chapter 7 is optional unless you build serving systems. Chapter 10 is for lookup: reading list, glossary and errata. Terms in the table: CPM, CPC and CPA are prices per 1,000 impressions, per click and per action (such as a purchase); oCPM bills per 1,000 impressions while the platform bids toward the advertiser's target price per action (chapter 3). ROAS (return on ad spend) is revenue credited to ads divided by ad spend. In a first-price auction the winner pays its own bid; in a second-price auction, the highest competing bid.

A fictional campaign used throughout

A shopper taps a shoe store’s ad in an app and buys a pair of shoes. The store is the advertiser, the app owner is the publisher, and the ad network connects them.

  • Attributed conversion. The report counts the purchase for the campaign because it follows an ad click within the campaign’s attribution window, the time allowed between the click and the purchase.
  • Billable conversion. The contract charges $30 for an attributed purchase that meets the billing rules. That $30 is the advertiser’s ad spend for this event.
  • Incremental conversions. These are the additional purchases the campaign generates. A randomized holdout experiment estimates how many extra purchases the campaign produces across a group. If this shopper would have bought the shoes anyway, their purchase adds zero incremental conversions.

Billing follows the contract. To decide whether to keep spending, the store compares its ad spend with the contribution from incremental purchases after product and fulfillment costs. Google Ads on attributed and incremental conversions · Google Ads on paying for conversions

Optional worked example for 1,000 impressions

One campaign, with one set of numbers, runs through the whole reader. Its numbers are teaching assumptions, not AppLovin's rates or industry benchmarks.

Roles. The advertiser is a merchant that pays for advertising. The publisher owns the app or website with the ad slots. The ad network (later also called the platform) sits between them: it buys impressions from publishers and bills advertisers.

Events, in order. A bid request is the publisher's invitation to bid on one opportunity. A win means the network won that auction. An impression means the ad was displayed. A click means the user tapped it. A conversion is the agreed target action, here a purchase. Attribution credits an order to an ad by fixed rules; here only orders that follow a click on the ad, within the contract's attribution window, are credited. A billable attributed conversion is a credited order that also passes the contract's checks (deduplication, refunds, chargebacks) and is billed exactly once. Attribution is a crediting rule, not a measurement of cause: a credited order may have happened without the ad.

The contract. The network charges the advertiser a=\$30 per billable attributed conversion. This is a CPA (cost per action) contract. The network buys the impressions from publishers in auctions and pays the auction price, written P per 1,000 impressions, whatever the advertiser is billed. Each order is worth g=\$40 of pre-advertising contribution to the advertiser: sales revenue minus the cost of goods, fulfillment and other variable costs, before ad spend and fixed costs. There are N=1{,}000{,}000 similar bid opportunities per day and a daily budget B=\$2{,}000 for buying impressions. Whose budget that is depends on the contract. In chapter 4's main line the advertiser buys directly, so B caps its spend. Under the CPA contract it caps the network's payments to publishers (chapter 4's optional network appendix), not the advertiser's bill, which is $30 per conversion however much the network paid.

Worked example

What one impression is worth, and to whom

Given: p_{\text{click}}=0.01 clicks per impression, p_{\text{conv}}=0.05 billable conversions per click, a=\$30 per billable conversion, g=\$40 per order, \Delta q=0.0002 incremental purchases per impression.

  1. Billable conversions per impression (only post-click orders are credited, so there is no no-click term): q=p_{\text{click}}\,p_{\text{conv}}=0.01\times0.05=0.0005.
  2. Ad spend: the network's expected revenue per impression, which is the advertiser's expected bill, v=qa=0.0005\times\$30=\$0.015. Per 1,000 impressions this is $15, the network's eCPM (effective revenue per thousand impressions; CPM, cost per mille, is a price per 1,000 impressions).
  3. Contribution from attributed conversions per impression: qg=0.0005\times\$40=\$0.020, or $20 per 1,000 impressions.
  4. Incremental contribution per impression: w=\Delta q\,g=0.0002\times\$40=\$0.008, or $8 per 1,000 impressions.
  5. Expected counts per 1,000 impressions: 1{,}000\times0.01=10 clicks and 1{,}000\times0.0005=0.5 billable conversions.

Result: per 1,000 impressions, the expected ad bill is $15, contribution from attributed orders is $20, and incremental order contribution is $8. The advertiser gains $8 of contribution and pays $15, leaving a $7 expected loss.

Check: count first, then price. 10 clicks × 0.05 = 0.5 conversions; 0.5 × $30 = $15 billed and 0.5 × $40 = $20 credited, matching steps 2–3. The ratio of contribution from attributed orders to ad spend is g/a=40/30=4/3, and $20/$15 = 4/3.

The causal lift \Delta q is assumed here because no routine report reveals it. It is the difference in purchase probability between showing the ad and not showing it, for the same opportunity. Attribution credits every post-click order whether or not the ad caused it; estimating \Delta q takes a randomized experiment (chapter 6). Here \Delta q<q, so attribution over-credits the ad. In general it can also miss orders the ad did cause (here, purchases without a click), so the gap q-\Delta q is a net figure.

Worked example

Ad spend, publisher payment, and advertiser profit

Given: the values per 1,000 impressions above ($15 in ad spend, $20 in contribution from attributed purchases, $8 in incremental contribution) and the publisher payment P per 1,000 impressions, left symbolic because the auction sets it.

  1. Contribution from attributed purchases minus ad spend. This calculation uses the attributed count as an input. 1{,}000\,(qg-qa)=\$20-\$15=+\$5.
  2. Incremental profit: incremental contribution minus ad spend. 1{,}000\,(\Delta q\,g-qa)=\$8-\$15=-\$7.
  3. Network: bill collected minus media cost, \$15-P. It is positive only if P<\$15, i.e. the network pays publishers less per 1,000 impressions than its eCPM.
  4. Publisher: receives P, before its own costs and any loss of user experience.
  5. Advertiser break-even based on incremental contribution: \begin{aligned}\Delta q\,g\ge qa&\iff\Delta q\ge\Delta q^{*}=\frac{qa}{g},\\ \Delta q^{*}&=\frac{0.0005\times30}{40}=0.000375.\end{aligned}

Result: attributed purchases suggest a $5 gain per 1,000 impressions. Under the assumed incrementality, the advertiser loses $7. The campaign pays for itself only if \Delta q\ge0.000375, almost twice the assumed 0.0002.

Check: payments are transfers, so they cancel when the advertiser’s incremental profit, the network’s receipts less media cost, and the publisher’s receipts are summed: (\$8-\$15)+(\$15-P)+P=\$8, exactly incremental contribution, for any P. Summed on the attributed view, the ledgers show $20; the $12 difference is contribution from attributed purchases minus incremental contribution, 1{,}000\,(q-\Delta q)\,g=1{,}000\times0.0003\times\$40.

Attribution is blind to the one parameter that decides whether the advertiser should keep paying. If \Delta q doubles to 0.0004, incremental contribution becomes 0.0004\times\$40=\$0.016 per impression, $16 per 1,000, and the incremental profit turns to \$16-\$15=+\$1 per 1,000. The bill stays $15 and the calculation based on attributed purchases still gives +$5.
