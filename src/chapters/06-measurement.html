Attribution, experiments and observability

In this chapter

  • Tell attribution, a credit rule that drives billing, from incrementality, the causal effect that decides whether spend is profitable.
  • Read a holdout experiment end to end: attributed ROAS 5, incremental ROAS 2, and a −$1,000 profit estimate whose 95% interval, [−$2,830, +$830], spans zero.
  • Convert an effect per assigned user into an effect per exposed user, and see why comparing exposed users with the control group doubles the effect in the example.
  • Match the experiment to the question: a model A/B test ranks two policies; only a channel test says whether advertising pays at all.
  • Know what privacy rules (ATT, SKAdNetwork, AdAttributionKit) and fraud do to the labels and bills you observe.

The gist. Attribution rules assign conversion credit for reporting and optimization. Billing follows the contract, which may use qualifying attributed conversions. A randomized holdout experiment estimates incremental conversions by comparing outcomes between groups with and without the campaign. Privacy restrictions, reporting delays, and invalid traffic affect what can be measured.

Attribution assigns credit; experiments measure causation

Attribution is a bookkeeping rule that assigns each conversion to one ad touchpoint (a click or an impression), because billing and optimization need an owner for every conversion. The common rule is last touch: the last qualifying click within a lookback window wins, and an unclicked impression can claim the conversion within a shorter view-through window if no click qualifies. Windows vary by contract; the timeline uses typical app-install defaults. For mobile apps an MMP (mobile measurement partner; Chapter 3) does the matching: its SDK inside the advertiser's app reports installs and in-app events, networks send it their click and impression claims, and it sends the winner a postback (a server-to-server notice of the attributed event).

  1. t = 0
    Impression. The ad renders; the network sends the MMP an impression claim.
  2. click
    Click. The user taps; the network sends a click claim and opens the app store.
  3. ≤ 7 d
    Install = first open. The SDK reports the first launch, not the download. The MMP credits the last click in the past 7 days; with no click, an impression in the past 24 hours.
  4. minutes
    Install postback to the winning network; losing networks typically hear nothing.
  5. later
    In-app events. Purchases inherit the install's attribution and trigger further postbacks.

Incrementality asks how many conversions exist because the ad ran. The two disagree in both directions: a real conversion can be credited to the wrong touchpoint, and a correctly credited one may have happened anyway. Last-touch and view-through rules systematically reward ads placed next to purchases users were already going to make, such as retargeting existing customers or spraying cheap impressions to collect view-through credit.

Attribution answers

Which touchpoint gets credit for this conversion? Attribution rules assign credit for reporting and optimization. A contract can use qualifying attributed conversions or installs as billable events.

Incrementality answers

How many conversions exist because the ad ran? Defined for a population relative to what would have happened without the campaign. A randomized holdout can estimate the effect and its uncertainty. The estimated effect can be negative.

Rich observational data does not substitute for randomization: in large Facebook experiments, observational methods did not reliably recover the experimental effects, because targeting uses signals the analyst cannot see that also predict purchases. Gordon et al., observational methods versus large Facebook experiments

A holdout experiment measures what the ads caused

A holdout experiment randomly splits one eligible population into a treatment group, where the campaign may deliver, and a control group (the holdout), where it may not; the difference in outcomes is what the campaign caused. The example is a self-contained advertiser campaign, not the running network campaign. The contribution rate is the share of net revenue (after refunds) left after non-advertising variable costs. ROAS and iROAS are as defined in Chapter 3: revenue credited to the campaign, and revenue it caused, each divided by ad spend. Assume correct randomization, a matured window, and no interference between the groups.

What a 95% confidence interval tells you

Suppose a holdout test records 1,200 purchases in treatment and 1,000 in control, with 100,000 users in each group. The observed difference is 200 purchases. If you ran the same test again with fresh users from the same population, both groups’ counts could change even if the campaign’s true effect stayed fixed. The standard error estimates the standard deviation of the observed difference across repetitions.

A 95% confidence interval is built by a procedure that would cover the fixed true effect in about 95 out of 100 repetitions, under the assumptions of the calculation. Each repetition produces its own estimate and interval. In this test, the interval runs from about 109 to 291 additional purchases per 100,000 assigned users. That describes uncertainty about the campaign’s average effect. A prediction for the observed purchase difference in a future test would require a separate calculation.

  1. Compute the estimate. Equal group sizes let us subtract the purchase counts directly. The estimated lift is 1,200 − 1,000 = 200 purchases.
  2. Estimate the noise. One user contributes either zero or one purchase, with variance p(1-p) at purchase probability p. Multiply by the number of users to get each group’s count variance, using the observed purchase rates of 1.2% and 1.0%. Both counts can fluctuate independently, so their variances add even though we subtract the counts. Take the square root to get a standard error of \sqrt{1{,}185.6+990}\approx46.64 purchases.
  3. Compute the margin of error. With large independent groups, the sampling error is approximately normal. The middle 95% of a standard normal lies between −1.96 and +1.96. Multiply 1.96\times46.64\approx91.4 purchases, then subtract and add that margin to 200 to get approximately [108.6, 291.4].

Compare the interval with the decision threshold. Zero purchases is the threshold for a positive sales effect. Break-even is 250 purchases because each extra order contributes $20 before ads and the test spent $5,000. The interval clears zero and spans 250. The data support a positive sales effect, while both a loss and a profit remain compatible with the test at this confidence level.

Worked example

Holdout experiment: from attributed ROAS to a decision

Given: 100,000 users per group. Over the same matured window, treatment makes 1,200 purchases and control 1,000. Ad spend is $5,000, all in treatment. Attribution credits 500 purchases to the campaign. Net revenue is $50 per purchase and the contribution rate 40%, so each purchase contributes \$50\times0.40=\$20 before ad spend. Each user buys at most once, independently of other users.

  1. Attributed ROAS: 500\times\$50\div\$5{,}000=5.
  2. Lift (equal group sizes, so counts compare directly): 1{,}200-1{,}000=200 incremental purchases per 100,000 users assigned to treatment.
  3. iROAS: 200\times\$50\div\$5{,}000=2. Each incremental revenue dollar yields $0.40 of contribution, so break-even iROAS is 1\div0.40=2.5.
  4. Incremental contribution after ad spend: 200\times\$20-\$5{,}000=-\$1{,}000. Break-even lift: \$5{,}000\div\$20=250 purchases.
  5. Non-incremental credit: at the point estimate, at least 500-200=300 credited purchases would have happened anyway (if the ad never suppresses a purchase).
  6. Standard error of the lift. Each group's purchase count is binomial with variance n p(1-p), and the variances of independent groups add (derivation below). With the observed rates: 100{,}000\times0.012\times0.988=1{,}185.6 and 100{,}000\times0.010\times0.990=990, so \text{SE}=\sqrt{1{,}185.6+990}=\sqrt{2{,}175.6}=46.6. The lift is 200/46.6=4.3 standard errors from zero.
  7. 95% interval: 200\pm1.96\times46.64=200\pm91.4 (unrounded SE), i.e. [108.6,\ 291.4]\approx[109,\ 291] purchases.
  8. Map through the linear formulas: iROAS =\text{lift}\times\$50/\$5{,}000 gives [1.09,\ 2.91]; contribution =\$20\times\text{lift}-\$5{,}000 gives [-\$2{,}828,\ +\$828]\approx[-\$2{,}830,\ +\$830].
  9. Decision. Break-even (250) lies inside the interval, so the sign of profit is unresolved.

Result: the campaign causes purchases (lift 200, 95% CI [109, 291]); its profit is −$1,000 at the point estimate with 95% CI ≈ [−$2,830, +$830], while attributed ROAS says 5.

Check: both ROAS figures equal purchases × $50/$5,000 = purchases/100, so their ratio is 500/200 = 2.5. Contribution also equals \$5{,}000\times(0.40\times\text{iROAS}-1): at iROAS 1.086 that is −$2,828, matching step 8.

Optional: the standard error of a difference in purchase rates

Assumptions. Each of the n_1 treatment users buys (at most once) with probability p_1, each of the n_0 control users with probability p_0, all users independently.

Variance. One user's purchase indicator is Bernoulli with mean p and variance \mathbb{E}[Y^2]-p^2=p-p^2=p(1-p). The treatment count X_1 is a sum of n_1 independent indicators, and variances of independent variables add, so \operatorname{Var}(X_1)=n_1p_1(1-p_1); likewise for X_0. The difference in rates \hat\Delta=X_1/n_1-X_0/n_0 has mean p_1-p_0 (unbiased) and, since the groups are independent,

\operatorname{Var}(\hat\Delta)=\frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1}+\frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n_0}.

With n_1=n_0=n the lift in counts is n\hat\Delta=X_1-X_0, with variance n p_1(1-p_1)+n p_0(1-p_0): the formula in step 6. The p's are unknown, so the observed rates 0.012 and 0.010 are plugged in. Per assigned user the standard error is 46.6/100{,}000=0.000466.

Interval. \hat\Delta is a difference of two averages of many independent indicators, so by the central limit theorem it is approximately normal; with about 1,000 purchases per group the approximation is good, and \pm1.96 standard errors covers the true lift with probability about 95%. With repeat purchases, use the variance of purchases per user. With variable order values, estimate uncertainty in contribution directly. If you randomize clusters, such as regions, account for dependence within each cluster.

Assignment is not exposure

Many users assigned to treatment never see the ad (no slot won, app never opened, ad scrolled past). Dividing the effect per assigned user by the exposure rate gives the effect per exposed user; comparing exposed users with the control group does not. Write T for random assignment (1 = treatment), D for actual exposure, and c=P(D=1\mid T=1) for the exposure rate in treatment. The intent-to-treat effect (ITT) is the difference in mean outcome by assignment, \bar Y_{T=1}-\bar Y_{T=0}. It needs only randomization, so analyze it first. The effect of treatment on the treated (TOT, per exposed user) is the Wald ratio

\tau_{\text{TOT}}=\frac{\bar Y_{T=1}-\bar Y_{T=0}}{c},

valid if, in addition, control users cannot be exposed, assignment changes purchases only through exposure (the exclusion restriction), and c>0. Reason: treatment users who would not be exposed contribute zero to the ITT, so the ITT is c times the average effect among those who would be (derivation below).

Worked example

ITT, TOT and the naive comparison with exposure logs

Given: the holdout experiment above, plus hypothetical exposure data. In treatment, 40,000 users were exposed and bought 800 times; the other 60,000 bought 400 times. Ghost-ad logs (described below) flag the 40,000 control users who would have been exposed; they bought 600 times, and the other 60,000 control users bought 400 times.

  1. ITT per assigned user: (1{,}200-1{,}000)/100{,}000=0.002 purchases, SE 46.64/100{,}000=0.0004664.
  2. Exposure rate \hat c=40{,}000/100{,}000=0.40. TOT: 0.002/0.40=0.005 purchases per exposed user, SE 0.0004664/0.40=0.001166 (treating \hat c as known; it is estimated from 100,000 users). z=0.005/0.001166=4.3, the same as ITT: rescaling changes the unit, not the evidence.
  3. Totals agree: 100{,}000\times0.002=40{,}000\times0.005=200 purchases, so iROAS is still 2.
  4. Naive comparison: 800/40{,}000-1{,}000/100{,}000=0.020-0.010=0.010 per exposed user, i.e. 400 "incremental" purchases, twice the truth. The ghost logs show why: would-be-exposed control users buy at 600/40{,}000=0.015 against 0.010 overall. Delivery reaches people who were more likely to buy anyway, and that gap of 0.005 is exactly the bias.

Result: TOT = 0.005 purchases per exposed user; the naive estimate (0.010) is 2× too high.

Check: \hat c\times\tau_{\text{TOT}}=0.40\times0.005=0.002=\text{ITT}, and naive − TOT = 0.010 − 0.005 = 0.005, the gap in step 4.

In an auction, withholding the campaign from a control user does not leave the slot empty: the next-best ad wins, so every effect here is relative to the ads the user would otherwise see, not to no ad. Ghost ads (Johnson, Lewis and Nubbemeyer, 2017) log exposure in control too: the ad server runs the auction with the campaign included, records a "ghost impression" whenever the campaign would have won and rendered, then serves the runner-up. Comparing exposed with ghost-exposed users estimates TOT directly and more precisely, if the ghost logic replicates real delivery exactly.

Optional: deriving the Wald ratio and the naive bias, and what ghost ads add

Notation. For user i, D_i(t) is whether the user would be exposed under assignment t, and Y_i(d) the purchase outcome under exposure d. \tau_{\text{TOT}}=\mathbb{E}[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\mid D_i(1)=1].

Assumptions. (A1) Randomization: T_i is independent of (D_i(\cdot),Y_i(\cdot)). (A2) No interference: user i's outcomes do not depend on others' assignments. (A3) Exclusion: assignment affects purchases only through exposure, so the observed outcome is Y_i(D_i(T_i)). (A4) One-sided noncompliance: D_i(0)=0, because control cannot be served. (A5) c=P(D_i(1)=1)>0.

Wald ratio. By A1 and A3, \mathbb{E}[Y\mid T=1]=\mathbb{E}[Y_i(D_i(1))]; by A1, A3 and A4, \mathbb{E}[Y\mid T=0]=\mathbb{E}[Y_i(0)]. So

\begin{aligned} \text{ITT}&=\mathbb{E}\bigl[Y_i(D_i(1))-Y_i(0)\bigr] =\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf 1\{D_i(1)=1\}\,(Y_i(1)-Y_i(0))\bigr]\\ &=P(D_i(1)=1)\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\mid D_i(1)=1\bigr]=c\,\tau_{\text{TOT}}. \end{aligned}

The second equality holds because the difference is zero for users with D_i(1)=0. By A1, c=P(D=1\mid T=1), which the treatment group's exposure logs estimate. Dividing by c (A5) gives the Wald ratio.

Naive bias. Exposed treatment users are those with D_i(1)=1, so

\mathbb{E}[Y\mid D=1,T=1]-\mathbb{E}[Y\mid T=0] =\tau_{\text{TOT}}+\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[Y_i(0)\mid D_i(1)=1\bigr]-\mathbb{E}\bigl[Y_i(0)\bigr]\Bigr).

The bracket is how much more likely the would-be-exposed are to buy without the ad; it is positive when delivery targets likely buyers. In the example it is 0.015-0.010=0.005.

What ghost ads add. Ghost logs observe D_i(1) in control too, so TOT can be estimated as exposed treatment minus ghost-exposed control: 0.020-0.015=0.005. Its standard error, from the two binomial counts on 40,000 users each, is \sqrt{800(0.98)+600(0.985)}/40{,}000=\sqrt{1{,}375}/40{,}000=0.000927, so z=0.005/0.000927=5.4. That standard error is 20.5% below the Wald ratio's 0.001166; the variance ratio (0.001166/0.000927)^2=1.58 is worth 58% more users, because the 120,000 never-exposed users only added noise. The logs also test exclusion: never-exposed users buy at 400/60,000 in both groups, consistent with assignment acting only through exposure. The older placebo design, which shows control users a public-service ad (PSA), fails under optimized delivery: the optimizer delivers the PSA to different users than it would deliver the real ad, so the two exposed groups are not comparable.

Model tests and channel tests answer different questions

A model A/B test, which randomizes traffic between two delivery policies, can show that policy B beats policy A; only a channel incrementality test, which compares advertising with not advertising or with a different budget, can show that advertising pays at all. Geo experiments randomize regions instead of users and come in several designs (go dark, heavy-up, budget levels); choose the question first and the design second. Google GeoX experiment types

Ad experiments leak between arms: a shared budget makes the arms compete for one quota, one arm's bids move the other arm's prices, and users cross devices and regions. Document how each leak is handled, and read results only at equal, mature label age (Chapter 5).

Report both parties' metrics. A network paid per attributed conversion earns on easy-to-attribute conversions, incremental or not; the advertiser gains only from incremental ones (Chapter 4's network appendix). A policy judged on network revenue alone drifts toward the former.

Privacy and fraud limit what can be observed

Privacy rules and fraud decide which outcomes can be observed at all, and the gaps are not random. On iOS two distinct Apple mechanisms matter. ATT (App Tracking Transparency, iOS 14.5, 2021) requires user permission, through a system prompt, before an app tracks the user across other companies' apps and websites or reads the IDFA (Identifier for Advertisers, the per-device ad identifier that made user-level attribution possible). SKAdNetwork (SKAN) and its successor AdAttributionKit (iOS 17.4) are Apple's privacy-preserving attribution APIs, which per Apple need no ATT prompt: the device itself decides which ad gets credit for an install and sends the network a signed, delayed postback with no user identifier. For small campaigns, crowd-anonymity thresholds blank or coarsen fields such as the conversion value (an app-set integer from 0 to 63 encoding post-install activity). Postbacks arrive after each conversion window closes (or the app locks it early), plus a random delay. The windows start at the app's first launch, which may itself come up to 60 days after install, so the last postback can come 41 days after first launch. Apple ad attribution

Optional: the SKAdNetwork 4 postback schedule

AdAttributionKit keeps the same three windows. Postbacks are signed and carry a unique identifier (SKAdNetwork transaction-id, AdAttributionKit postback-identifier), so receivers can reject forgeries and replays.

  1. before day 0
    Engagement and install. The install must follow a StoreKit-rendered ad tap within 30 days or a view-through impression within 24 hours, and the user must first launch the app within 60 days of installing it.
  2. day 0
    First launch. The conversion windows below are counted from here.
  3. days 0–2
    Window 1. The app may raise its conversion value; the postback carries the fine value (0–63) if the crowd is large enough, otherwise a coarse value (low, medium, high), or none at the lowest crowd tier. Sent 24–48 hours after the window closes.
  4. days 3–7
    Window 2. Coarse value only; not sent at the lowest crowd tier. Sent 24–144 hours after close.
  5. days 8–35
    Window 3. Coarse value only; not sent at the lowest crowd tier. The last postback can arrive as late as day 41 after first launch (35 days + 144 hours).

For training, a missing postback cannot be labeled "not converted"; postbacks without user identifiers cannot be joined back to users without extra assumptions; a signed event proves authenticity, not incrementality; and missingness depends on consent rates and campaign size. A new platform that subsidizes impression purchases has bought delivery, not training labels it can join across customers under long-term consent.

Check fraud layer by layer, because each layer is a separate attack surface: is the supply real, was the impression rendered, was the click human, is the conversion valid, was attribution hijacked, was the purchase refunded? In click injection, for example, Android malware detects an install in progress and fires a fake click just before first open, taking last-touch credit for an install that was happening anyway. Supply-chain standards cover only the first layer: ads.txt (app-ads.txt for apps) lists, on the publisher's own domain, which ad systems may sell its inventory; sellers.json lists the sellers each SSP (supply-side platform; Chapter 2) or exchange pays; the OpenRTB SupplyChain object lists every hop a bid request passed through. None guarantees traffic quality. IAB ads.txt, IAB sellers.json and SupplyChain FAQ

As in trading, positive expected value per trade does not guarantee survival: correlated fake events, retroactive window changes, chargebacks and paying publishers before advertisers pay all concentrate risk. A fraud model is one layer; per-source exposure limits, settlement reconciliation, clawback rights and gradual ramp-up of new sources are the others.
