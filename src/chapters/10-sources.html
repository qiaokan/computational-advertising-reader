An erratum in the RTB monograph's second-price example

The RTB monograph's budget-constrained second-price bid (its Section 5.3.4) counts the win probability twice, so the published bid coefficient is too high and overspends the budget. Expected cost per bid request already includes zero on losses; multiplying it by the win probability again undercounts spend. For example, a 30% win rate and a $3 average payment per win imply $0.90 per request; a second multiplication gives $0.27. The correct coefficient is \sqrt{6LB/N}, valid for B/N\le L/6. The published one overspends by at least 18% wherever the correct one applies, and by 43.6% at the running example's numbers. The rest of the monograph is a sound reference; the proof below is for readers checking the book.

Optional: proof and numerical impact of the monograph erratum

The monograph is “Display Advertising with Real-Time Bidding (RTB) and Behavioural Targeting” by Jun Wang, Weinan Zhang and Shuai Yuan (arXiv:1610.03013v2, 122 pages; arXiv date July 15, 2017, title page July 18). Symbols

Notation follows Auctions and bidding. The monograph's symbol, where different, is in brackets.

  • Z: the competitive threshold, the highest competing bid on one bid request. We win if our bid is at least Z [market price z].
  • L>0: the upper bound of Z, with Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) [l].
  • b\ge 0: our bid on one request. Z, L and b are dollars per impression (×1,000 for CPM).
  • F(b)=P(Z\le b): the win rate at bid b; f=F' [w(b), p_z].
  • r\in[0,1]: the request's predicted click-through rate (pCTR), the probability that a won impression is clicked. Here r is a probability, not money.
  • b(r): the bidding function, mapping pCTR to a bid.
  • N: bid requests over the campaign's life [T]. B: total media budget in dollars. B/N is the budget per request.
  • C(b)=E[Z\,\mathbf{1}\{Z\le b\}]: expected payment per request, counting zero on a loss, as in chapter 4's second-price section. \mathbf{1}\{\cdot\} is the indicator; E is over Z. The monograph's cost function \bar c(b) (Eq. 5.5) is the expected payment given a win, \bar c(b)=E[Z\mid Z\le b], so C(b)=\bar c(b)\,F(b).
  • \lambda>0: the Lagrange multiplier on the budget. The objective counts clicks, so \lambda is clicks per dollar and 1/\lambda is dollars per click.

Model and correct solution

The monograph's special case: an ideal second-price auction (the winner pays Z; no floor, fees or bid increments); every win is one billable impression; r\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1), independent of Z; a risk-neutral buyer that maximizes expected clicks subject to expected spend =B. Nothing assumes the bid is linear in r; linearity falls out of the first-order condition.

Derivation

Budget-constrained click maximization under second price

Given: Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) in dollars per impression; pCTR r\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1), independent of Z; N requests; budget B dollars.

  1. Win rate and costs for 0\le b\le L. The per-request cost integrates the payment over winning thresholds only; it already contains the win probability.
    \begin{aligned} F(b)&=\frac{b}{L},\\ \bar c(b)&=E[Z\mid Z\le b]=\frac{b}{2},\\ C(b)&=\int_0^b z\,\frac{dz}{L}=\frac{b^2}{2L}\\ &=\bar c(b)\,F(b). \end{aligned}
    For b>L the bid always wins and C(b)=E[Z]=L/2, so bidding above L is equivalent to bidding L.
  2. The program (the monograph's Eq. 5.11 with u(r)=r):
    \begin{aligned} &\max_{b(\cdot)}\; N\int_0^1 r\,F\bigl(b(r)\bigr)\,dr\\ &\text{s.t.}\quad N\int_0^1 C\bigl(b(r)\bigr)\,dr=B. \end{aligned}
  3. The Lagrangian separates across r. Maximize rF(b)-\lambda C(b) pointwise. Since C'(b)=b\,f(b), the derivative is (r-\lambda b)\,f(b): positive below r/\lambda, negative above. So
    b(r)=\min\!\left(\frac{r}{\lambda},\,L\right).
    This is the monograph's Eq. 5.32 and the total-value bidder's second-price rule b=v/\lambda in chapter 4's appendix, with value v=r measured in clicks. (A sufficiency argument for the pointwise maximizer is in chapter 4's one-multiplier proof.)
  4. The budget binds. With r/\lambda\le L for all r, expected clicks are N\int_0^1 r\cdot\frac{r}{\lambda L}\,dr=\frac{N}{3\lambda L}, decreasing in \lambda; so the optimum spends the whole budget and the equality constraint is right.
  5. Solve the budget equation, writing c^*=1/\lambda^* for the bid coefficient:
    \begin{aligned} N\int_0^1 \frac{(r c^*)^2}{2L}\,dr&=\frac{N (c^*)^2}{6L}=B\\ \Longrightarrow\quad c^*=\frac{1}{\lambda^*}&=\sqrt{\frac{6LB}{N}}. \end{aligned}
  6. Validity. The step-5 integral assumed no bid exceeds L, i.e. c^*\le L, which is 6LB/N\le L^2, i.e. B/N\le L/6. For L/6<B/N<L/2 the bid \min(rc,L) costs L/2-L^2/(3c) per request, so c=L^2/\bigl(3(L/2-B/N)\bigr); it equals L at B/N=L/6, so the two regimes join continuously. For B/N\ge L/2 the budget never binds: bidding L everywhere costs E[Z]=L/2 per request.

Result: b^*(r)=r\sqrt{6LB/N} for B/N\le L/6 (dollars per impression; the coefficient is dollars per click).

Check: (i) Units: L and B/N are both dollars, so \sqrt{6LB/N} is dollars, per click because r is clicks per impression. (ii) Marginal cost: spend S=N c^2/(6L) and clicks K=N c/(3L) give dS/dK=c, so at the optimum the last click costs exactly 1/\lambda^*, as a shadow price should. (iii) Numerical double integration of E[Z\,\mathbf{1}\{Z\le rc^*\}] over (r,Z), a Monte Carlo run, and a grid search over linear coefficients all reproduce the closed form and the optimum.

Where the monograph goes wrong

The monograph's budget constraint (Eqs. 5.11 and 5.33, PDF pp. 56 and 58) is N\int \bar c(b(r))\,F(b(r))\,dr=B. That is correct: Eq. 5.5 (p. 54) defines c as the conditional payment, so \bar c\,F=C. But Eq. 5.35 (p. 58) evaluates \bar c(b) as \int_0^b z/L\,dz=b^2/(2L), dropping Eq. 5.5's denominator F(b). That is C(b), not \bar c(b). Eq. 5.36 (p. 59) then multiplies by F(b)=b/L again, making the per-request cost b^3/(2L^2). With b=r/\lambda:

\begin{aligned} \int_0^1\frac{(r/\lambda)^3}{2L^2}\,dr&=\frac{1}{8\lambda^3L^2}=\frac{B}{N}\\ \Longrightarrow\quad \lambda_{\mathrm{paper}}&=\frac12\left(\frac{N}{BL^2}\right)^{1/3},\\ c_{\mathrm{paper}}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{paper}}}&=2\left(\frac{L^2B}{N}\right)^{1/3}. \end{aligned}

These are Eqs. 5.37 and 5.38. The linear form b=r/\lambda survives; only the coefficient is wrong. The first-price result in the same section, b(r)=r\sqrt{3LB/N} (Eq. 5.29, p. 58), uses the per-request cost b\,F(b)=b^2/L correctly, so the slip is confined to the second-price case. Pitfall: conditional vs per-request cost

“Expected cost” has two meanings: given a win, E[Z\mid Z\le b], and per request, E[Z\,\mathbf{1}\{Z\le b\}]. They differ by a factor F(b). A budget constraint needs the per-request one: the win rate enters exactly once. Units will not catch a second factor of F, which is dimensionless; scaling will. Under a uniform threshold, per-request second-price spend must grow as b^2; a formula that grows as b^3 has counted the win rate twice.

Size of the error

Bidding with c_{\mathrm{paper}}, the true expected spend per request is c_{\mathrm{paper}}^2/(6L) as long as c_{\mathrm{paper}}\le L. Divided by the budget per request:

\begin{aligned} \frac{c_{\mathrm{paper}}^2/(6L)}{B/N}&=\frac{4\,(L^2B/N)^{2/3}}{6L\,B/N}\\ &=\frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{LN}{B}\right)^{1/3}. \end{aligned}

The published coefficient keeps every bid at or below L exactly when 8L^2B/N\le L^3, i.e. B/N\le L/8. The factor decreases in B/N, so on that range it is at least \tfrac23\cdot 8^{1/3}=\tfrac43. On L/8<B/N\le L/6 some published bids exceed L; true spend is then L/2-L^2/(3c_{\mathrm{paper}}) per request, and the factor keeps falling to 3-6^{1/3}\approx1.183 at B/N=L/6. Wherever the correct closed form applies, the published one overspends by at least 18%, and by more as the budget tightens. This is not a boundary effect.

Worked example

The erratum at the running example's numbers

Given: threshold bound L=\$0.02 per impression ($20 CPM); N=1{,}000{,}000 requests; media budget B=\$2{,}000; pCTR r\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1) as in the monograph's special case. Values are carried unrounded and rounded only for display.

  1. Budget per request and validity: B/N=\$0.002. This is below L/6=\$0.00333, so the correct closed form applies, and below L/8=\$0.0025, so no published bid exceeds L either.
  2. Correct coefficient: \begin{aligned} c^*&=\sqrt{6LB/N}=\sqrt{6\times0.02\times0.002}\\ &=\sqrt{0.00024}=\$0.015492 \end{aligned} per click. A request with pCTR r gets a bid of r\times\$15.49 CPM.
  3. Published coefficient: \begin{aligned} c_{\mathrm{paper}}&=2(L^2B/N)^{1/3}\\ &=2\,(0.0004\times0.002)^{1/3}\\ &=2\times(8\times10^{-7})^{1/3}\\ &=2\times0.0092832=\$0.018566 \end{aligned} per click: a bid of r\times\$18.57 CPM, 19.8% higher.
  4. True expected spend at the published coefficient: \begin{aligned} N\,\frac{c_{\mathrm{paper}}^2}{6L}&=10^6\times\frac{4\times(8\times10^{-7})^{2/3}}{0.12}\\ &=10^6\times\frac{3.44710\times10^{-4}}{0.12}\\ &=\$2{,}872.58. \end{aligned}
  5. Clicks. Expected clicks are Nc/(3L): 258,199 at c^* and 309,439 at c_{\mathrm{paper}}. The extra 51,240 clicks cost $872.58, or $0.0170 each, above the optimum's marginal cost of 1/\lambda^*=\$0.0155 per click.
  6. In a fluid approximation with $2,000 available and expected spend accruing at a constant rate, the published bidder runs out after 2000/2872.58=69.6\% of the flight, so the approximation gives 69.6% of 309,439 clicks, or 2B/c_{\mathrm{paper}}=215{,}443, against the optimum's 2B/c^*=258{,}199: 16.6% fewer clicks for the same money.

Result: the published coefficient spends $2,872.58 in expectation against a $2,000 budget (1.436×); the fluid approximation gives 16.6% fewer clicks at the same spend. Actual hard-cap delivery also depends on the order and size of realized payments.

Check: the overspend formula gives \tfrac23(LN/B)^{1/3}=\tfrac23\times10^{1/3}=\tfrac23\times2.15443=1.436290, and 1.436290\times\$2{,}000=\$2{,}872.58, matching step 4. The click shortfall is 1-c^*/c_{\mathrm{paper}}=1-0.015492/0.018566=16.6\%. Unitless case L=1, B/N=0.01 (below L/8): coefficients 0.244949 and 0.430887, overspend factor \tfrac23\times100^{1/3}=3.094.

The main text derives its budget results independently and does not reuse this example.

CVR / pCVRConversion rate; the denominator (per click, per impression or per install) must be stated. pCVR is the model's predicted value.
D7 ROASA cohort's attributed revenue in the first 168 hours after acquisition (for apps, the install), divided by its ad spend; valid only once the window has closed for every user. Ch. 3
Deal typesDirect deal (negotiated by hand, fixed price and volume); programmatic guaranteed (fixed price, reserved volume, automated execution); private auction (invitation-only; the same thing as a PMP, see that entry); open auction (any qualified buyer). Ch. 2
Delayed feedbackConversions arriving hours to days after the impression, so recent training examples carry incomplete labels: a negative may still convert. Ch. 5
Demand sourceAny buyer that can fill an ad request: an ad network, a DSP or a direct campaign.
DSPDemand-side platform: the buyer's software, which receives bid requests across exchanges, predicts what each impression is worth to its advertisers and bids within their budgets. Ch. 2
Dual bidTwo prices, one for a frequent shallow event (e.g. install) and one for a rare deep event (e.g. purchase); the platform buys only if both targets look met, capping the variance of a young deep-event model at some cost in volume. Ch. 3
eCPMEffective CPM: expected revenue or value per 1,000 impressions, whatever the pricing unit. The auction ranks bids made on different events by it, e.g. 1000\times\text{pCTR}\times\text{pCVR}\times\text{CPA bid}. Ch. 2
Fill rateThe share of ad requests that receive an ad.
First-price auctionAn auction in which the winner pays its own bid. Ch. 4
Floor priceThe seller's minimum acceptable price for an opportunity; bids below it lose. Also called a reserve price.
Four pointsThe four events every pricing product fixes on the path from impression to business result: where the auction ranks, where the advertiser is billed, where it bids and what it is judged on. The ranked-to-billed segment is the seller's risk, billed-to-bid the payer's, bid-to-judged the advertiser's. Ch. 2, Ch. 3
Ghost adsAn experiment design that logs, for control users, the impressions the campaign would have won, so the effect on users who would actually be exposed can be estimated without placebo ads. Ch. 6
Gross spreadA term defined in this reader: an ad network's collections from advertisers minus its media procurement cost; it is before the network's other costs, so it is not profit. Ch. 4 appendix
Gross vs net revenueRevenue recognition: a platform that controls the service before it reaches the customer (a principal under IFRS 15 and ASC 606) books the full advertiser spend as revenue (gross); an agent books only what it keeps (net). Ch. 3
GSPGeneralized second-price auction for several ranked slots, as in search ads: each winner pays the minimum bid needed to keep its position, i.e. the next-ranked bid (quality-weighted where quality scores apply). Bidding one's value is not a dominant strategy and in general not an equilibrium. Ch. 2
Guaranteed dealA contract that fixes price and volume in advance and commits the publisher to deliver the qualifying impressions, with a remedy for shortfall; executed by software it is called programmatic guaranteed. Ch. 2
Hard capA spend limit that must hold on every realized path, not just in expectation; enforced by reserving each bid's maximum possible cost before the bid is sent. Pacing and expected-budget constraints do not guarantee it. Ch. 7
HoldoutA randomly selected group withheld from a campaign to serve as the control when measuring what the campaign caused. Ch. 6
IABInteractive Advertising Bureau, the industry body whose Tech Lab publishes OpenRTB, ads.txt and sellers.json.
IDFAIdentifier for Advertisers: Apple's per-device advertising identifier, readable only after ATT consent. Ch. 2
ImpressionOne rendered display of an ad. A “successful impression” here means the display event the contract counts actually completed; a won auction can still fail to render.
Incremental impression value (w)Advertiser contribution before advertising cost caused by one successful impression, w=\Delta q\,g=\$0.008 in the running example: causal purchase-probability lift times contribution per added order. The value the advertiser bids on in chapter 4; distinct from a network's CPA receipts v=qa. Ch. 4
IncrementalityThe change in an outcome caused by showing ads, measured against a randomized counterfactual; also called lift. Contrast with attribution. Ch. 6
InterstitialA full-screen ad shown at a natural break, such as between game levels.
IPSInverse propensity scoring: an off-policy estimator that reweights each logged reward by \pi(a\mid x)/\mu(a\mid x), the ratio of the new policy's to the logging policy's action probability. Ch. 5
iROASIncremental ROAS: revenue caused by the ads (treatment minus control) divided by ad spend; break-even is one over the contribution margin. Ch. 3
ITTIntent-to-treat effect: the average effect of being assigned to treatment, whether or not the user was actually exposed. Ch. 6
LTVLifetime value: the value a user generates over a stated horizon. State whether it is revenue or contribution, the horizon, the discounting and whether acquisition cost is already subtracted. Ch. 3
lurlOpenRTB loss notice URL: the exchange calls it when the bid loses, optionally with a loss-reason code. Ch. 7
MAXAppLovin's mediation platform for app publishers. Ch. 8
MediationSoftware in a publisher's app that decides which of several demand sources fills each ad request, by in-app bidding, a waterfall or both. Ch. 2
MMPMobile measurement partner (e.g. Adjust, AppsFlyer): a third party whose SDK records installs and in-app events and attributes them to ad networks. Ch. 3
Mobile advertising IDA per-device identifier any app could read for advertising: Apple's IDFA and Google's advertising ID on Android. Since ATT, the IDFA reads as zeros without the user's permission. Ch. 2
Modelled conversionsConversions a platform estimates, from similar users it can observe, where it cannot link a conversion to an ad, and reports together with observed ones. Training labels built on them are partly model output. Ch. 2
nurlOpenRTB win notice URL: the exchange calls it when the bid wins. On its own it does not mean the ad rendered or is billable. Ch. 7
oCPM / oCPC / oCPXOptimized CPM or CPC: the advertiser states a target price per conversion, the platform turns it into a per-impression bid with its own pCTR and pCVR, and billing stays per impression or per click. oCPX names both. The advertiser bears the platform's estimation error. Ch. 3
OpenRTBThe IAB Tech Lab protocol for bid requests, bid responses and notices; this reader cites version 2.6.
Optimized biddingProducts in which the advertiser states a target per action (or a return target) and the platform bids per impression on its behalf: oCPX, Google's Smart Bidding (tCPA, tROAS), and campaign types that also automate audience and placement, such as Performance Max and Advantage+. Ch. 2
PacingOnline control of the spend rate over the budget period, e.g. by adjusting the budget multiplier toward a target spend trajectory; it works alongside, and does not replace, a hard budget cap. Ch. 4
Performance Max / Advantage+Google's and Meta's goal-based campaign types, in which the platform's models choose audience, placement and creative as well as the bid; Performance Max buys across all Google inventory. Ch. 2
PMPPrivate marketplace: an auction open only to invited buyers, often with a floor price; between open auctions and guaranteed deals. Ch. 2
PostbackA server-to-server message that reports a conversion, and possibly its value, to the ad network or MMP.
ProgrammaticBuying and selling ad opportunities through automated systems, one opportunity at a time; RTB is its auction form. Ch. 2
PSAPublic-service ad: a placebo ad shown to control users in older experiment designs; biased when an optimizer chooses who sees ads. Ch. 6
PublisherThe owner of the app or website whose ad slots (its inventory) are sold.
Reach and frequencyBrand-campaign planning units: reach is the number of distinct people who see the ad at least once; frequency is the average number of times each of them sees it. Ch. 2
Retail mediaAds sold by a retailer on its own site or app, such as Amazon's Sponsored Products; the retailer observes the sale itself, so its conversion labels are complete and timely. Also called commerce media. Ch. 2
Retrieval and rankingThe stages of ad selection: retrieval picks a small candidate set worth scoring; ranking then estimates response probabilities and value for each candidate. Ch. 5
Rewarded videoA video ad the user opts into in exchange for an in-app reward. Ch. 2
ROASReturn on ad spend: attributed revenue divided by ad spend. It ignores cost of goods and does not measure incrementality. Ch. 3
RPMRevenue per 1,000 ad requests (also called request eCPM); if each filled request yields one impression, RPM = fill rate × impression eCPM.
RTBReal-time bidding: an auction run separately for each ad opportunity while the user's page or app waits, within a deadline the exchange sets per request (OpenRTB tmax, commonly on the order of 100 ms). Ch. 2
SDKSoftware development kit: a library embedded in an app, e.g. by an ad network, mediation platform or MMP.
Second-price auctionAn auction in which the winner pays the highest competing bid, or the floor if higher. “Ideal second-price” in this reader ignores floors, bid increments and fees. Ch. 4
sellers.json / SupplyChain objectsellers.json is a file each SSP or exchange publishes listing the sellers it pays; the OpenRTB SupplyChain object lists every intermediary a bid request passed through. Ch. 6
SettlementThe final, contractually checked computation of who owes whom. Ch. 7
Shadow priceThe increase in the optimal objective per extra unit of a constrained resource, given by the constraint's Lagrange multiplier; for a budget, extra value (or clicks) per extra budget dollar. Written \mu_A for the advertiser's budget and \lambda or \mu in the appendix and the erratum. Ch. 4
SKAdNetworkApple's earlier privacy-preserving install-attribution API (SKAN), succeeded by AdAttributionKit. Ch. 6
SSPSupply-side platform: the seller's software, which offers each impression to many buyers, applies the publisher's floor prices and rules, and collects payment. Ch. 2
Statistical arbitrageHere, buying impressions on CPM while selling outcomes on CPA: profitable in expectation, but exposed to conversion, attribution and payment risk. Ch. 4 appendix
Take rateThe amount the platform keeps after paying publishers, divided by the total advertiser spend that flows through it. Ch. 3
tCPATarget CPA: the average cost per action an advertiser asks the bidder to aim for; a bidding goal, not a billing price. Ch. 2
Third-party cookieA browser identifier stored by a domain other than the site being visited, typically an ad-tech company embedded on many sites, so one browser can be recognized across them. Safari blocks them by default and Firefox blocks known trackers' cookies; Chrome still allows them unless the user blocks them. Ch. 2
tmaxOpenRTB bid-request field: the maximum time, in milliseconds, the exchange allows for bids to arrive, including network latency. Ch. 7
TOT / CACETreatment effect on the treated / complier average causal effect: the effect on users actually exposed. If control users cannot be exposed and assignment acts only through exposure (the exclusion restriction), it equals the ITT divided by the treatment group's exposure rate (the Wald ratio). Ch. 6
tROASTarget ROAS: the return on ad spend an advertiser asks the bidder to aim for; a bidding goal, not a billing contract. Ch. 2
View-through conversionA conversion credited to an impression that was not clicked; a click-through conversion follows a click. Ch. 6
Walled gardenA company that owns the users, the ad slots, the auction and the measurement, such as Google, Meta, Amazon or ByteDance; outsiders cannot see user-level data. Ch. 2
WaterfallA mediation method that calls demand sources one at a time in a configured priority order until one fills the request. Ch. 2
Win rateThe probability of winning at bid b, F(b)=P(Z\le b); as a metric, wins divided by bids. Ch. 4
Winner's curseThe auction's winner is the ad with the highest prediction, and the highest of several noisy unbiased predictions is biased upward: with ten equal ads and 20% noise, about 31%. Calibrate on won impressions and shrink noisy predictions. Ch. 5

November 12, 2026 (ten-chapter restructure and section review).
