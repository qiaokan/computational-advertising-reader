Start here: the map, two reading paths and one running campaign

In this chapter

  • Name the five layers of an ad system and what each one decides.
  • Choose a reading path: the three-hour gist path or the full day.
  • Derive the running campaign's three values per impression: a $0.015 expected ad charge, $0.020 of order contribution counted by the attribution report, and $0.008 of additional contribution generated by the ad.
  • Write the advertiser, network and publisher ledgers per 1,000 impressions, and see why the attribution report shows a $5 gain while the causal ledger shows a $7 loss.
  • State the advertiser's break-even lift, \Delta q^{*}=qa/g=0.000375.

The gist. An ad system turns a stream of one-off chances to show an ad into money, under contracts that say who pays whom for which event. Five pieces interact: a model guesses what each impression will lead to, a contract says what is billed, an auction and a budget decide what is bought, real-time systems carry out and record each decision, and experiments check whether the ads caused any sales. The running campaign below shows the trap the whole reader returns to: one impression has one value on the invoice, a larger value in the attribution report and a smaller value in reality. The advertiser's incremental profit is the contribution generated by the ad minus the ad bill.

Five layers decide what happens to every impression

An ad system prices, allocates and settles one-off impression opportunities while its feedback is delayed and incomplete. An impression opportunity is one chance to show one ad to one user in one slot at one moment. Five layers interact, and each has a chapter:

  • A model estimates a conditional expectation, such as the probability that this impression leads to a purchase (Prediction and data).
  • The product contract decides who pays whom for which event, and so who bears which risk (Products and cash flow).
  • Auctions and budgets decide which opportunities are bought, and at what price (Auctions and bidding).
  • Systems execute each decision before its deadline and record it exactly once (Real-time systems).
  • Experiments are the only layer that tests whether the advertising caused sales that would not otherwise have happened (Measurement).

Profit depends on all five; a gain in one layer can be undone by another. The landscape places them in the industry: ad formats, who sells to whom, and where the money is. The case study, AppLovin and Axon, uses three product names: Axon is AppLovin's prediction and bidding engine; MAX is its mediation product, which runs an auction among ad networks for each ad slot in a publisher's app; Adjust is a mobile measurement partner (MMP) that AppLovin owns; MMPs are normally independent third parties that record app installs and in-app events and credit them to ads.

Two reading paths

Read the gist path first: it covers the whole system in about three hours. It reads chapters 1–3 and the case study in full. For chapters 4–6 it reads only the gist level: the “In this chapter” box, the gist paragraph, and the first sentence of each section, which states that section's claim. The full day reads everything; chapter 7 is optional unless you build serving systems. Chapter 10 is for lookup: reading list, glossary and errata. Terms in the table: CPM, CPC and CPA are prices per 1,000 impressions, per click and per action (such as a purchase); oCPM bills per 1,000 impressions while the platform bids toward the advertiser's target price per action (chapter 3). ROAS (return on ad spend) is revenue credited to ads divided by ad spend. In a first-price auction the winner pays its own bid; in a second-price auction, the highest competing bid.

A fictional campaign used throughout

One campaign, with one set of numbers, runs through the whole reader. Its numbers are teaching assumptions, not AppLovin's rates or industry benchmarks.

Roles. The advertiser is a merchant that pays for advertising. The publisher owns the app or website with the ad slots. The ad network (later also called the platform) sits between them: it buys impressions from publishers and bills advertisers.

Events, in order. A bid request is the publisher's invitation to bid on one opportunity. A win means the network won that auction. An impression means the ad was displayed. A click means the user tapped it. A conversion is the agreed target action, here a purchase. Attribution credits an order to an ad by fixed rules; here only orders that follow a click on the ad, within the contract's attribution window, are credited. A billable attributed conversion is a credited order that also passes the contract's checks (deduplication, refunds, chargebacks) and is billed exactly once. Attribution is a crediting rule, not a measurement of cause: a credited order may have happened without the ad.

The contract. The network charges the advertiser a=\$30 per billable attributed conversion. This is a CPA (cost per action) contract. The network buys the impressions from publishers in auctions and pays the auction price, written P per 1,000 impressions, whatever the advertiser is billed. Each order is worth g=\$40 of pre-advertising contribution to the advertiser: sales revenue minus the cost of goods, fulfillment and other variable costs, before ad spend and fixed costs. There are N=1{,}000{,}000 similar bid opportunities per day and a daily budget B=\$2{,}000 for buying impressions. Whose budget that is depends on the contract. In chapter 4's main line the advertiser buys directly, so B caps its spend. Under the CPA contract it caps the network's payments to publishers (chapter 4's optional network appendix), not the advertiser's bill, which is $30 per conversion however much the network paid.

What billed, credited and caused mean

  • Billed is what the advertiser owes the network. The contract charges $30 for each credited order that passes its billing checks.
  • Credited means the attribution rule assigns an observed order to the ad. Here, an eligible purchase after an ad click within the attribution window gets credit. Credited contribution values each credited order counted in this example at $40 before ad spend. The worked numbers use the same set of orders for billing and credited contribution. A real report can also include credited orders that fail billing checks.
  • Caused refers to the additional purchases the ad generates compared with what would have happened without it. Caused contribution values that difference at $40 per additional order. We cannot observe both outcomes for the same customer, so the example assumes a population-level lift and the measurement chapter shows how to estimate it.

Suppose someone already planned to buy, clicked the ad, and placed a qualifying order. The network bills $30 and the report credits $40 of contribution. If the customer would have bought anyway, the ad added $0 of contribution from that order.

Worked example

What one impression is worth, and to whom

Given: p_{\text{click}}=0.01 clicks per impression, p_{\text{conv}}=0.05 billable conversions per click, a=\$30 per billable conversion, g=\$40 per order, \Delta q=0.0002 caused purchases per impression.

  1. Billable conversions per impression (only post-click orders are credited, so there is no no-click term): q=p_{\text{click}}\,p_{\text{conv}}=0.01\times0.05=0.0005.
  2. Billed: the network's expected revenue per impression, which is the advertiser's expected bill, v=qa=0.0005\times\$30=\$0.015. Per 1,000 impressions this is $15, the network's eCPM (effective revenue per thousand impressions; CPM, cost per mille, is a price per 1,000 impressions).
  3. Credited: the contribution the attribution report credits to the ad, per impression: qg=0.0005\times\$40=\$0.020, or $20 per 1,000 impressions.
  4. Caused: the contribution the ad actually adds, per impression: w=\Delta q\,g=0.0002\times\$40=\$0.008, or $8 per 1,000 impressions.
  5. Expected counts per 1,000 impressions: 1{,}000\times0.01=10 clicks and 1{,}000\times0.0005=0.5 billable conversions.

Result: per 1,000 impressions, the expected ad bill is $15, credited order contribution is $20, and incremental order contribution is $8. The advertiser gains $8 of contribution and pays $15, leaving a $7 expected loss.

Check: count first, then price. 10 clicks × 0.05 = 0.5 conversions; 0.5 × $30 = $15 billed and 0.5 × $40 = $20 credited, matching steps 2–3. The credited-to-billed ratio is g/a=40/30=4/3, and $20/$15 = 4/3.

The causal lift \Delta q is assumed here because no routine report reveals it. It is the difference in purchase probability between showing the ad and not showing it, for the same opportunity. Attribution credits every post-click order whether or not the ad caused it; estimating \Delta q takes a randomized experiment (chapter 6). Here \Delta q<q, so attribution over-credits the ad. In general it can also miss orders the ad did cause (here, purchases without a click), so the gap q-\Delta q is a net figure.

Worked example

Three ledgers per 1,000 impressions

Given: the values per 1,000 impressions above ($15 billed, $20 credited, $8 caused) and the publisher payment P per 1,000 impressions, left symbolic because the auction sets it.

  1. Advertiser, as the attribution report shows it: credited contribution minus bill. 1{,}000\,(qg-qa)=\$20-\$15=+\$5.
  2. Advertiser, causal ledger: caused contribution minus bill. 1{,}000\,(\Delta q\,g-qa)=\$8-\$15=-\$7.
  3. Network: bill collected minus media cost, \$15-P. It is positive only if P<\$15, i.e. the network pays publishers less per 1,000 impressions than its eCPM.
  4. Publisher: receives P, before its own costs and any loss of user experience.
  5. Advertiser break-even on the causal ledger: \begin{aligned}\Delta q\,g\ge qa&\iff\Delta q\ge\Delta q^{*}=\frac{qa}{g},\\ \Delta q^{*}&=\frac{0.0005\times30}{40}=0.000375.\end{aligned}

Result: the report says the advertiser gains $5 per 1,000 impressions; it actually loses $7. The campaign pays for itself only if \Delta q\ge0.000375, almost twice the assumed 0.0002.

Check: payments are transfers, so they cancel when the three causal ledgers are summed: (\$8-\$15)+(\$15-P)+P=\$8, exactly the caused contribution, for any P. Summed on the attributed view, the ledgers show $20; the $12 difference is credited minus caused contribution, 1{,}000\,(q-\Delta q)\,g=1{,}000\times0.0003\times\$40.

Attribution is blind to the one parameter that decides whether the advertiser should keep paying. If \Delta q doubles to 0.0004, the caused contribution becomes 0.0004\times\$40=\$0.016 per impression, $16 per 1,000, and the causal net turns to \$16-\$15=+\$1 per 1,000. The bill stays $15 and the attribution report still shows +$5.
