Products and cash flow

Ad products have five dimensions that must be specified separately

“Search, feed, RTB, CPC, performance advertising” are not categories at the same level. Search describes the user context, RTB describes a real-time bidding transaction mechanism, CPC describes the billing event, and performance describes the optimization goal. Mixing them into one mutually exclusive classification table will inevitably lead to errors in the downstream objective function.

Dimension Typical values Impact on product and technology
User context Search, content feed, web pages, in-game interstitials How intent is revealed, and how much ads disrupt the core product experience
Creative and interaction Text, images, video, native cards, playable ads How creatives are produced, reviewed, displayed, and measured
Transaction type Direct deals, programmatic guaranteed, preferred deals, private or open auctions Who commits to volume, and who bears the risk of under-delivery
Billing event Impressions, clicks, installs, purchases, and other actions When money is owed, and which party retains the response risk
Optimization objective Clicks, installs, revenue, contribution profit, incremental profit Model labels, observation windows, and control constraints

For example, a rewarded video that a user actively chooses to watch in a game can be procured via a first-price auction, with the publisher paid per impression, while the advertiser requires D7 ROAS (cumulative over the first seven days after acquisition) to hit some target. There is no contradiction here: each description belongs to a different dimension. Rewarded-ad rewards do not require the user to install the advertised app, so “willing to finish the video” and “having purchase intent” are not the same thing either. Google AdMob rewarded ads documentation

In search, the query directly reveals part of the current need; feeds must identify opportunities from context, historical behavior, and interactions between creatives and users; open-web and in-app advertising must additionally connect supply, identity, and events across companies. These differences change prediction difficulty and product constraints; they do not prove that any context inherently enjoys a higher incremental return on investment.

What advertisers actually buy is a constrained delivery service

From the advertiser's perspective, a runnable campaign needs at least six groups of inputs: business goals and conversion events; budget and flight dates; permitted audiences and geographies; creatives and landing pages; attribution and value postbacks; and billing and settlement terms. Ad groups usually carry finer-grained targeting, bidding, or creative combinations, but the exact hierarchy varies by platform. A platform's menu hierarchy should not be treated as a universal data model.

“Optimize for purchases” is still not precise enough. Is a purchase an order placed, a payment made, or a valid order after the refund period? Is value the merchandise revenue, the platform commission, or contribution profit? Does D7 (day 7) revenue mean cumulative revenue over the first seven days after acquisition, or only revenue on day seven? When this tutorial discusses D7 ROAS, it fixes an observation window of the first 7×24 hours after acquisition, closed on the left and open on the right, i.e., cumulative attributed revenue from the moment of acquisition until just before 7 full days have elapsed / the corresponding acquisition ad spend; before using this metric you must wait for the window to mature. Other platforms may use different date boundaries, which must be redefined per interface. A change in one field definition can change offline labels, delivery preferences, and reports without any change to the model. Advertiser onboarding is therefore not just a configuration form: events, currency, time zone, deduplication keys, windows, refunds, and target constraints must become an executable contract.

Creative is also a decision variable. The same user seeing different creatives may click and retain completely differently. Explaining performance solely through a “user value model” misses creative supply, review speed, creative fatigue, and landing-page experience. Even if the platform can reliably find high-click creatives, it may be optimizing for misleading engagement; the product must add user experience and downstream quality as guardrail metrics.

Publishers sell attention and bear the cost to their core product

Publishers or app developers care about revenue per thousand opportunities, but also about ad fill rate (the share of ad requests that receive an ad), wait time, ad load, and user retention. Pushing up revenue per ad while causing users to open the app less may damage long-term supply. Thus the seller's optimization target is not an isolated auction but a combination of ad revenue, experience, and future usage.

Guaranteed contracts add another layer to the problem. If a publisher commits to deliver one million qualifying impressions in a month, a given opportunity can either fulfill the contract or be sold on the spot market; the comparison is then between spot revenue and the shadow value of reserving the opportunity to fulfill the future commitment. Supply forecasting, admission control, fulfillment penalties, and cross-contract allocation all enter the decision. Google Ad Manager's distinction between programmatic guaranteed and preferred deals is precisely that the former reserves and commits inventory, while the latter carries no such volume guarantee. Roles and money flows

A DSP (Demand Side Platform) helps advertisers buy and optimize; an SSP (Supply Side Platform) helps publishers manage and sell inventory; an exchange is a marketplace that matches buyers and sellers; an ad server handles ad selection, delivery, and logging. An ad network can bundle multiple roles. Mobile ad mediation coordinates multiple demand sources, SDKs, and bidding or waterfalls (a waterfall calls demand sources sequentially in a configured priority order); an MMP (Mobile Measurement Partner) sits in the feedback loop, providing measurement and attribution, and is not an intermediary through which all money must pass. Billing events and optimization objectives must not be conflated

CPM is the price per thousand impressions; CPC is the price per click; CPI is the price per install; CPA is the price per agreed action. CTR is clicks divided by impressions. CVR is the conversion rate, but its denominator must be specified: CVR computed per click and conversion probability computed per impression are not interchangeable. eCPM is a quantity that converts some revenue or value into a per-thousand-impression figure; it is not necessarily the price on the bill.

For the network in this example, the value of an impression is 0.015 dollars, corresponding to a 15-dollar eCPM. This does not mean the network is willing to bid 15 dollars CPM in every auction, much less that the advertiser settles on a CPM basis. Valuation, bid, and payment are three separate variables.

Target CPA (tCPA) is usually the average acquisition cost the optimization algorithm aims to achieve; target ROAS (tROAS) is the target return on ad spend. A common definition of ROAS (Return on Ad Spend) is revenue under an agreed definition divided by ad spend. iROAS (incremental ROAS) refers specifically to true incremental revenue divided by ad spend; the revenue definitions of the two must be kept distinct. Optimizing for CPA does not mean billing per conversion, nor does it guarantee that each conversion costs less than the target. Google's tCPA documentation describes exactly the process of automatically choosing bids based on a conversion target. Google tCPA documentation

LTV (Lifetime Value, customer lifetime value) is likewise not a universal label that needs no definition. At minimum, specify the time horizon, whether it is revenue or contribution profit, whether refunds are deducted, whether it is discounted, and whether it includes advertising investment. Treating revenue LTV directly as a profit valuation will systematically overestimate the amount that can be bid.

Three ledgers and the limits of scaling

The advertiser's ledger looks at what business value was gained and how much was paid for it; the network's ledger looks at how much is collected from advertisers and how much is paid to publishers and service providers; the publisher's ledger looks at how much is earned by selling inventory and how much experience and future usage is lost. All three may grow on their respective reports without simultaneously increasing real economic value.

Platform gross margin, the advertiser's campaign return, and the benefit of further scaling are three different questions. A platform can maintain an 80% gross margin while the advertiser earns less and less the more it spends. Below, the same ad budget is placed into two income statements, and then we examine how scaling changes each side's returns.

1. What is the denominator of the platform's 80% gross margin?

The advertiser pays 100 yuan, the publisher receives 75 yuan, and the platform retains 25 yuan; the platform then pays 5 yuan of direct service costs out of that. Assume the substance of the platform's business qualifies for net revenue recognition, and that the entire 5 yuan is recorded as cost of revenue:

Where the money goesAmountMeaning
Publisher75 yuanRevenue earned for providing ad display opportunities
Platform direct service costs5 yuanAssumed in this example to be entirely cost of revenue
Platform gross profit20 yuanBefore deducting the platform's other operating expenses
Total100 yuanAdvertiser's total spend
take rate = 25 / 100 = 25%
Accounting gross margin = (25 − 5) / 25 = 80%
Platform gross profit / advertiser spend = 20 / 100 = 20%

Here take rate is defined as the amount retained by the platform as a share of advertiser spend. The denominator of the 80% gross margin is the 25 yuan of revenue recognized by the platform: for every yuan of recognized revenue, 80 cents remain after deducting cost of revenue. It does not mean the platform takes 80% of the ad budget, nor that the platform ultimately nets 80%; R&D, sales, taxes, cost of capital, and so on may still need to be deducted.

Net recognition cannot rest merely on a contract calling the platform an “agent.” The key is whether, for a specific performance obligation, the platform controls the relevant goods or services before they are transferred. A principal usually recognizes revenue gross; an agent recognizes its service fee or commission. IFRS explanation of principal versus agent

2. Why does profit fall when the advertiser's sales rise?

Now switch to the advertiser's ledger. The “300 yuan of revenue” is the revenue consumers bring the advertiser by purchasing goods or services, completely different from the 25 yuan of revenue recognized by the platform. Assume a pre-advertising contribution rate of 40%: for every additional 100 yuan of sales revenue, after deducting goods, fulfillment, and related variable costs, 40 yuan remain to pay for advertising and contribute profit.

The initial 100 yuan of spend brings 120 yuan of pre-advertising contribution, earning 20 yuan after ad spend. The additional 50 yuan brings only 30 yuan of pre-advertising contribution, losing 20 yuan on its own. The new orders do exist, but the money left after fulfilling them is not enough to cover the additional acquisition cost. Sales rise from 300 yuan to 375 yuan, yet contribution profit falls from 20 yuan to zero.

3. Average iROAS masks the loss from the additional budget

Here let C denote advertiser spend, R the corresponding true incremental sales revenue, m the fixed pre-advertising contribution rate; ΔC is the additional spend, and ΔR is the new revenue brought by the additional spend. In this paragraph, “marginal iROAS” refers to the ratio for a finite increment, ΔR/ΔC; only as the increment approaches zero does it correspond to the derivative. At a 40% contribution rate, a cumulative iROAS (true incremental revenue / ad spend) of 2.5 is exactly the threshold at which post-advertising contribution profit is zero. At a 20% contribution rate the threshold is 5, and at 80% it is 1.25; these thresholds require m > 0, and that the revenue is truly incremental.

Break-even iROAS = 1 / m = 1 / 40% = 2.5
Marginal iROAS of this additional budget = ΔR / ΔC = 75 / 50 = 1.5

When scaling further, the 2.5 threshold should be applied to the revenue brought by the additional budget. 1.5 is below the threshold, so this additional spend reduced profit; the cumulative average is still 2.5 only because the earlier high-return spend covers the later low-return spend. This is similar to scaling up a trading strategy: the returns on new capacity may no longer cover costs, and the average return across the entire position masks the loss from the scale-up itself.

If ordinary ROAS is computed from attributed revenue instead, you cannot directly apply 1/m. When attributed revenue is positive, define κ (kappa) as true incremental revenue / attributed revenue; when κ>0 and m>0, the attributed ROAS at which incremental contribution breaks even is 1/(mκ). This ratio must be estimated separately; it cannot be inferred from the attribution report itself, and it is not presumed to be necessarily less than 1.

Further, write true incremental revenue as a function of spend, R(C), representing the change in revenue relative to not advertising; R(0) = 0. Let Π(C) denote incremental contribution profit after ad spend, and ΔΠ the change in profit caused by the additional spend; R′(C) is the marginal incremental revenue from each additional unit of ad spend. The contribution rate m is fixed, and other volume-dependent costs are ignored:

Π(C) = mR(C) − C
ΔΠ = m[R(C + ΔC) − R(C)] − ΔC
Condition for a finite increment: ΔΠ > 0
Sufficiently small increment: mR′(C) > 1

If the curve is smooth and concave and there are no other constraints such as a budget cap, an interior optimum satisfies mR′(C) = 1. The original example gives only a few discrete points, which is enough to show that the 150-yuan plan is less profitable than the 100-yuan plan, but not enough to prove that the exact optimal budget is necessarily 100 yuan.

4. Platform gross profit can grow while customer profit falls

Now combine the two ledgers. Additionally assume that the platform always retains 25% of the budget, that direct service costs are always 5% of the budget, and that revenue continues to be recognized net:

MetricAd budget 100 yuanAd budget 150 yuan
Platform net revenue2537.5
Platform direct service costs57.5
Platform gross profit2030
Platform gross margin80%80%
Advertiser contribution profit200

This scale-up increases platform gross profit by 10 yuan and reduces the advertiser's contribution profit by 20 yuan. This does not prove that the platform necessarily harms customers on purpose: customer retention, competition, and long-term budgets constrain it. But it does prove that growth in the platform's current-period revenue, a high platform gross margin, and growth in customer profit do not automatically coincide.

If the system objective is to “maximize sales while keeping cumulative iROAS no lower than 2.5,” the 150-yuan plan still qualifies; between these two plans, maximizing advertiser contribution profit would choose the 100-yuan plan. An algorithm can execute its objective perfectly correctly and still produce a commercially undesirable result.

5. The strongest assumption: revenue must be truly incremental

“The platform attributes the order to the ad” and “without the ad, the order would not have happened” are not equivalent. Google's official documentation also distinguishes standard attributed conversions from incremental conversions measured through experiments. Conversion Lift measurement definition

For example, if reported attributed revenue is 300 yuan but the revenue truly created by the ad is only 200 yuan, then the initial 100 yuan of spend is already losing money:

True incremental contribution profit = 200 × 40% − 100 = −20 yuan

To judge the value of a platform like AXON, the truly useful quantity is: under the same measurement definition, contribution rate, and budget conditions, how much it can raise the customer's true incremental contribution profit; and how much of this advantage remains as the budget increases. The platform's accounting gross margin cannot substitute for this answer.

Your trading experience transfers directly to auditable P&L, risk limits, and capacity curves; the idea that “an impression is like a homogeneous asset that can be stored and resold without loss” does not. Impressions vanish instantly, their value depends on the advertiser and creative, feedback is delayed, and the counterfactual of not advertising cannot be directly observed. So-called statistical arbitrage in advertising usually bears unhedged behavioral, attribution, and settlement risk.
