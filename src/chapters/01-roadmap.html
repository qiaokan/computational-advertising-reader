A one-day learning roadmap

In this chapter

  • Name the five layers of an ad system and what each one decides.
  • Know the day's schedule and the six questions you should be able to answer by 17:00.
  • Derive the running example's per-impression numbers: q=0.0005, a $0.015 expected bill, $0.020 attributed and $0.008 incremental contribution.
  • Write the advertiser, network and publisher ledgers per 1,000 impressions, and see why the attribution report shows a $5 gain while the causal ledger shows a $7 loss.
  • State the advertiser's break-even lift, \Delta q^{*}=qa/g=0.000375.

Computational advertising prices, allocates and settles heterogeneous impression opportunities when feedback is constrained, delayed and incomplete. An impression opportunity is one chance to show one ad to one user in one slot at one moment. Five layers interact:

  • A model estimates a conditional expectation, such as the probability that this impression leads to a purchase.
  • The product contract decides who pays whom for which event.
  • Auctions and budgets decide which opportunities can be bought, and at what price.
  • Systems decide whether a decision executes before its deadline and is recorded correctly.
  • Experiments are the only layer that tests whether the advertising creates value customers will keep paying for.

Calling any single layer “the ad engine” ignores the constraints that actually drive profit.

This reader assumes your background in theoretical CS, distributed systems, ML platforms and trading. It does not re-teach gradient descent, indexing or consensus. What you lack is the domain's own models: what each party optimizes, what each logged event means, what can and cannot be observed, and how the money is split. By the end of the day you should be able to derive a simplified bidding policy on your own, review the design of an ad system, and spot where claims about Axon's moat run ahead of their evidence. One day will not substitute for years of running campaigns.

Company and product names used throughout: AppLovin is the ad-tech company under discussion. Axon is its ad prediction and bidding engine. MAX is its mediation product for publishers: for each ad slot in a publisher's app, it runs an auction across many demand sources (ad networks and other buyers) and returns the winning ad. Adjust is AppLovin's MMP (mobile measurement partner): a third party that records app installs and in-app events and attributes them to the ads that preceded them. The public facts about these products, and the limits on how their data may be used, are checked in AppLovin and Axon.

Terms used above: ROAS (return on ad spend) is attributed revenue divided by ad spend; it ignores the cost of goods and ignores whether the ad caused the sale. In a first-price auction the winner pays its own bid; in a second-price auction the winner pays the highest competing bid (or the seller's floor price, if that is higher). Settlement is the final, contractually checked computation of who owes whom.

When you finish, you should be able to answer six questions:

  1. How much is a given opportunity worth, and to whom?
  2. Which contract makes the platform bear conversion risk? (Under per-conversion billing the platform pays publishers for impressions but is paid only when a conversion occurs, so it absorbs the variance.)
  3. How should you bid under a budget?
  4. Why does a model that improves offline (on held-out logs) not necessarily make more money online?
  5. How do you keep auction wins, billing and attribution from being conflated in the books?
  6. What evidence would suffice to support the claim that “the model creates a durable commercial advantage”?

Expanding acronyms is not the goal. If all you can say is that DSP means demand-side platform (software that bids for impressions on behalf of advertisers), CTR means click-through rate (clicks per impression) and RTB means real-time bidding (a separate auction for each individual impression, run while the page or app loads), you have not met the day's goal.

A fictional campaign used throughout

The running campaign charges the advertiser for orders credited to its ads. Chapter 3 also considers direct buying, where the advertiser pays the auction cost. The two arrangements use the same assumptions about additional orders and different rules for the advertiser's bill.

Roles. The advertiser is a merchant that sells goods and pays for advertising. The publisher owns the app or website that provides ad slots. The ad network sits between them: it buys impressions from publishers and bills advertisers. Later chapters call it the platform; both words mean the same party here. (Products and cash flow splits the network into its functional roles.)

Events, in the order they occur.

  • A bid request is the publisher's invitation to bid on one ad opportunity.
  • A win means the network won that auction.
  • An impression means the ad was actually displayed. In general a win does not guarantee one (the ad can fail to load); this example assumes it does.
  • A click means the user tapped the ad.
  • A conversion is the agreed target action; here, a purchase.
  • Attribution credits an order to an ad according to fixed rules. Here only post-click orders count: an order is attributed only if the user clicked the ad first, within the contract's attribution window. The window's length is left unspecified; it does not change any number below.
  • A billable attributed conversion is an attributed order that also passes the contract's checks (attribution window, deduplication, refunds, chargebacks) and can therefore be billed. In this example each one is billed exactly once.

Attribution is a crediting rule, not a measurement of causal effect. An attributed order may have happened without the ad.

Conventions. Pre-advertising contribution per order is sales revenue minus the cost of goods, fulfillment and other variable non-advertising costs, before ad spend and fixed costs. “Expected” means the average over many repetitions of the same campaign. The budget derivations in later chapters constrain expected spend; that alone does not guarantee that realized spend stays under the cap (hard budgets are covered in Real-time advertising systems). All amounts are in US dollars; all counts and budgets are per day. Each chapter defines its own symbols; where a letter changes meaning between chapters, the local definition governs.

The campaign. The network charges the advertiser $30 per billable attributed conversion. This is a CPA (cost per action) contract: the price is per action, not per impression. The network buys impressions from publishers through auctions and pays for each one at the price the auction rule sets (first price by default; see Prediction and bidding). Each order is worth $40 of pre-advertising contribution to the advertiser. The network sees 1,000,000 similar bid opportunities per day and has a daily media budget of $2,000 for buying impressions.

Simplifying assumptions (later chapters relax some of them):

  • Opportunities are homogeneous: every one has the same q and \Delta q.
  • Every win produces a successful impression, so paying per win and paying per impression coincide. Real-time advertising systems drops this assumption.
  • Conversion probability does not depend on the bid or on the competitive threshold (the highest competing bid). This assumption holds the mix of conversion probabilities fixed as the bid rises.
  • Opportunities are independent, and repeated exposure of the same user has no interaction effects.
Worked example

What one impression is worth, and to whom

Given: p_{\text{click}}=0.01 clicks per impression, p_{\text{conv}}=0.05 billable conversions per click, a=\$30 per billable conversion, g=\$40 per order, \Delta q=0.0002 incremental purchases per impression.

  1. Billable conversions per impression (only post-click orders are attributed, so there is no no-click term): q=p_{\text{click}}\,p_{\text{conv}}=0.01\times0.05=0.0005.
  2. Network's expected revenue per impression: v=qa=0.0005\times\$30=\$0.015. Per 1,000 impressions this is $15, the network's eCPM (effective revenue per thousand impressions; CPM, cost per mille, is a price per 1,000 impressions).
  3. Contribution the attribution report credits to the ad, per impression: qg=0.0005\times\$40=\$0.020, or $20 per 1,000 impressions.
  4. Contribution the ad actually adds, per impression: \Delta q\,g=0.0002\times\$40=\$0.008, or $8 per 1,000 impressions.
  5. Expected counts per 1,000 impressions: 1{,}000\times0.01=10 clicks and 1{,}000\times0.0005=0.5 billable conversions.

Result: one impression carries three values: $0.015 billed, $0.020 attributed, $0.008 incremental. Per 1,000 impressions: $15, $20 and $8.

Check: count first, then price. 10 clicks × 0.05 = 0.5 conversions; 0.5 × $30 = $15 billed and 0.5 × $40 = $20 attributed, matching steps 2–3. The attributed-to-billed ratio is g/a=40/30=4/3, and $20/$15 = 4/3.

About \Delta q. For each eligible opportunity, \Delta q is the difference in purchase probability between showing the ad and not showing it for that same opportunity. We set it by assumption. Attribution reports cannot reveal it: they credit every post-click order, whether or not the ad caused it. Estimating it needs a randomized experiment, and even then a per-user lift divided by impressions per user equals \Delta q only under extra conditions. The lift must be scaled from assigned users to exposed users; the control group sees the next-best ad, not no ad; and effects must add up across a user's impressions (assumed here, rarely true in practice). Attribution, experiments and data covers the first two. That \Delta q < q here means attribution over-credits the ad. The gap q-\Delta q need not equal the rate of attributed-but-non-incremental orders, because attribution rules can also miss orders the ad did cause (here, any purchase without a prior click).

Worked example

Three ledgers per 1,000 impressions

Given: the per-1,000 values above ($15 billed, $20 attributed, $8 incremental) and a publisher payment of P dollars per 1,000 impressions (the network's media cost). P comes from the auction and is left symbolic here.

  1. Advertiser, as the attribution report shows it: attributed contribution minus bill. 1{,}000\,(qg-qa)=\$20-\$15=+\$5.
  2. Advertiser, causal ledger: incremental contribution minus bill. 1{,}000\,(\Delta q\,g-qa)=\$8-\$15=-\$7.
  3. Network: bill collected minus media cost, \$15-P. It is positive only if P<\$15, i.e. the network pays publishers less per 1,000 impressions than its eCPM.
  4. Publisher: receives P (before its own costs and any loss of user experience).
  5. Advertiser break-even on the causal ledger: \begin{aligned}\Delta q\,g\ge qa&\iff\Delta q\ge\Delta q^{*}=\frac{qa}{g},\\ \Delta q^{*}&=\frac{0.0005\times30}{40}=0.000375.\end{aligned}

Result: the report says the advertiser gains $5 per 1,000 impressions; it actually loses $7. The campaign pays for itself only if \Delta q\ge0.000375, almost twice the assumed 0.0002. Prediction and bidding derives the same threshold.

Check: payments are transfers, so they cancel when the three causal ledgers are summed: (\$8-\$15)+(\$15-P)+P=\$8, exactly the incremental contribution, for any P. Summed on the attributed view, the ledgers show $20; the $12 difference is the difference between credited and incremental contribution 1{,}000\,(q-\Delta q)\,g=1{,}000\times0.0003\times\$40.

What changes if \Delta q doubles to 0.0004? Incremental contribution becomes 0.0004\times\$40=\$0.016 per impression, $16 per 1,000, so the causal net turns to \$16-\$15=+\$1 per 1,000; 0.0004 clears the 0.000375 break-even. Nothing else moves: the bill stays $15, the attribution report still shows +$5, and the network's ledger is unchanged. Attribution is blind to the one parameter that decides whether the advertiser keeps paying.

All figures in the running example are teaching assumptions, not AppLovin's rates or industry benchmarks. Fractional conversion counts are expected values.

Pitfall

$2,000 caps the network's media spend, not the advertiser's bill. The advertiser pays a=\$30 per billable conversion regardless of what the network paid for the impressions, so its bill can exceed $2,000. An advertiser budget would be a second, independent constraint.
