Real-time advertising systems

In this chapter

  • Turn a 120 ms tmax, measured at the exchange, into an 80 ms compute deadline at the bidder.
  • Follow one bid through the win, display and billing notices, and say which of them creates a payment obligation.
  • Prove that the hard-budget invariant C+R\le B survives every event, and see why a timeout may never release a reservation.
  • Estimate with Little's law how much budget open reservations lock (about $1.49 at a 60 s billing delay), and what reserving expected cost instead risks ($0.48 at P99 if wins are independent, $21.62 if they move together).

The gist. This is an optional deep dive for engineers. Latency tails, retries and idempotency work here as anywhere else; this chapter covers only what advertising adds. A bid is worth nothing if it reaches the exchange after the auction deadline, and that deadline includes the network trip. Winning an auction is not the same as showing the ad, and showing it is not always the same as owing money: the exchange sends separate notices, late and sometimes twice. A hard budget must therefore hold back each bid's maximum cost until a notice proves what is owed. A timer proves nothing. Finally, a release that changes labels, windows or traffic mix changes what the system optimizes, not just how fast it runs.

The accounting examples use the optional CPA-network case from Chapter 4: a network buys publisher impressions and bills advertisers per attributed conversion. The ledger and reservation rules apply to any contract once its payment obligations are written down.

Three pipelines, tied together by one chain of events

An ad network runs three pipelines that must agree on what happened. The control pipeline manages campaigns (an advertiser's unit of budget, targeting and goals), creatives (the ad assets), budgets and configuration releases. The online pipeline answers each bid request before its deadline: eligibility applies hard filters (targeting, budget, approved creative, block lists, consent), retrieval narrows the eligible campaigns to a few hundred, then prediction, ranking and bidding run on a versioned, read-only online snapshot of configuration and features. The event pipeline records delivery, billing, attribution, training data and reconciliation. The diagram is a teaching architecture, not Axon's or any other company's implementation.

One impression spans milliseconds to months, and each event proves something different (time scales illustrative):

  1. 0 ms
    Bid request. The exchange sends the request; the tmax window is measured at the exchange.
  2. ≤ 120 ms
    Bid response. Must reach the exchange inside tmax. The budget reservation is made before the response leaves.
  3. + ms to s
    Auction result. Win notice (nurl) or loss notice (lurl), if the exchange sends them. A win is not a delivered or billable impression.
  4. + seconds
    Render. The creative loads on the device and fires the impression pixel, or fails.
  5. + s to hours
    Billing notice. burl fires when the impression becomes billable under the exchange's policy; retries and late arrivals are normal. The reservation becomes confirmed spend.
  6. + days
    Clicks and conversions. Attributed inside the attribution window; they create the CPA receivable. Refunds and chargebacks arrive later still.
  7. monthly
    Settlement. The exchange's statement, your log and the advertiser bill are reconciled; corrections are posted as compensating entries.

The bid deadline is measured at the exchange

A bid that arrives after tmax is discarded, and the window includes the network trip both ways. OpenRTB, the IAB Tech Lab's real-time bidding protocol, defines the fields this chapter uses (OpenRTB 2.6):

  • tmax (request): the maximum time, in milliseconds, the exchange allows for bids to be received, including internet latency.
  • price (bid): the bid, quoted as CPM (dollars per thousand impressions) even though it buys one impression.
  • nurl, lurl, burl (bid): the win, loss and billing notice URLs. The spec says a win does not necessarily mean a delivered, viewed or billable ad; burl is called when the impression becomes billable under the exchange's own policy. All three are optional.
  • ${AUCTION_PRICE}: a macro the exchange substitutes into notice URLs, giving the clearing price in the bid's units (CPM). The bidder writes its own notice URLs, so it embeds its own bid id in them.
Worked example

A 120 ms deadline, split into stage budgets

Given (all values illustrative, not an industry standard): tmax = 120 ms; network round-trip P99 of 30 ms for this exchange and region, measured as a round trip in the bidder's own logs; safety margin \delta = 10 ms. The bidder cannot see when the exchange sent the request, so it budgets from its own receipt time.

  1. Network allowance: t_{\text{net}} = 30\ \text{ms}
  2. Compute deadline after receipt: \begin{aligned}t_c &= t_{\max} - t_{\text{net}} - \delta\\ &= 120 - 30 - 10 = 80\ \text{ms}\end{aligned}
  3. Stage budgets (ms): decode and eligibility 10, feature reads 15, retrieval 10, inference 30, budget admission 5, pricing and encoding 5, slack 5. 10+15+10+30+5+5+5 = 80

Result: 80 ms of compute after receipt, passed to each stage as an absolute deadline. A stage that misses it takes its declared fallback, or the request gets no bid.

Check: 30 + 10 + 80 = 120 ms. The usual tail caveats apply: stage P99s do not add up to an end-to-end P99, and fan-out to many feature shards needs hedged reads or default features.

What advertising adds is that every fallback has a price. Default features, a smaller model and no bid each bias value differently. Bidding with stale configuration, an exhausted quota or a creative of unknown review status can create real spend and breach contracts. Give each fallback preconditions, a maximum dollar exposure and a logged reason code; otherwise "higher availability" may just move losses into the ledger.

Win, display and billing are different events

Only the event the contract names creates a payment obligation, and the system must record each such event exactly once. Continue the network example from the Chapter 4 appendix: a first-price auction (the winner pays its own bid) and the budget-constrained network bid of 6.324555 CPM, that is at most $0.006324555 for one impression, with no fees.

Before the response leaves, the system fixes the bid's business identity, (exchange, BidRequest.id, Imp.id, Bid.id), and records the campaign, model, feature, configuration and experiment versions. The exchange belongs in the key because request ids are unique only within one exchange, and Imp.id only within one request (it is often just 1). The budget service then reserves the bid's maximum cost.

Notices arrive at least once, so the deduplication key for a posting is that business identity plus the event type, never a transport message id. A transport id posts every exchange retry: a double payment and a settlement dispute. A key without the exchange merges two exchanges' notices that share an auction id: a real payable is dropped and its reservation never clears. A notice that repeats a key with a different ${AUCTION_PRICE} is not a duplicate. Send it to exception handling.

A nurl call means the bid won. It is not evidence of delivery or billing. A burl call means the impression became billable, typically on render or on view. At that point the network books the billed amount (${AUCTION_PRICE}/1000 for one impression) as spend and releases the rest of the reservation. If the billed amount ever exceeds the reserved cap, the hard-budget premise is broken: route the case to exception handling. A missing impression pixel does not prove that nothing is owed, so a local timeout cannot release the reservation.

Clicks and conversions arrive days later through a separate pipeline and create receivables, which can still be reversed by refunds. Keep three records apart: media payables owed to exchanges and publishers, advertiser receivables, and training and attribution events. A single "conversion success" field cannot serve all three. At settlement, your event log, the exchange's statement and the advertiser's bill are compared; discrepancies are fixed with compensating entries under a named authority, since deduplication covers only the events you received.

The contract decides which event is billable, and the reservation must cover the worst case under that contract. If 100 bids win and 90 ads display, win-based billing charges for 100 events and display-based billing for 90. Under either contract each open bid can still cost up to its full bid, so a hard-quota reservation cannot be cut by 10% because display succeeds 90% of the time. How display-based billing changes the optimal bid is worked out in the Chapter 4 appendix.

A hard budget is an invariant over reservations

A hard budget means confirmed spend can never exceed the cap on any path, and the only way to guarantee that with delayed billing is to reserve each bid's maximum cost at admission. All quantities are ledger dollars for one accounting period. Let C be confirmed media spend, R the sum of caps of bids that are open (admitted, not yet billed or released), and B the hard media budget. Bid i has cost cap c_i (the most it can ever be charged, fees included) and billed amount p_i. The rules are:

\begin{aligned} &\text{invariant:} && C+R\le B\\ &\text{admit } i \text{ only if } C+R+c_i\le B: && R \leftarrow R+c_i\\ &\text{first billing of open } i,\ p_i\le c_i: && C \leftarrow C+p_i,\; R \leftarrow R-c_i\\ &\text{authoritative loss or cancellation of open } i: && R \leftarrow R-c_i\\ &\text{win notice, duplicate, local timeout:} && \text{no change} \end{aligned}

Proof. Assume every bid has a reliable cap with p_i\le c_i, each bid moves from open to billed or released at most once, and each rule applies atomically. The at-most-once property comes from a per-bid state (open → closed) that is tested and changed in the same atomic step as the posting; the deduplication key only suppresses repeats of the same event. A billing notice for an already released bid, or a loss notice for an already billed one, goes to exception handling instead of the ledger. Initially C=R=0\le B. Suppose the invariant holds before an event; check each event type:

  • Admission: the new total is C+R+c_i, which the guard requires to be at most B.
  • Billing: the total changes by p_i-c_i\le 0.
  • Loss or cancellation: the total changes by -c_i\le 0.
  • Win notice, duplicate notice, timeout: no change.

By induction the invariant holds after every event. Because R is a sum of nonnegative caps of open bids, R\ge0, so C\le B. ∎ With many serving nodes, admission must be an atomic check-and-reserve: a serializable counter, or escrow, where node n holds quota Q_n with \sum_n Q_n\le B and keeps its own C_n+R_n\le Q_n. Then \sum_n (C_n+R_n)\le B, by the same proof per node. This requires every notice for bid i to be applied at the node that reserved it, and quota transfers between nodes to be atomic.

Worked example

One bid through the ledger at the last bid's worth of budget

Given: the Chapter 4 appendix bid of 6.324555 CPM, every win displays, burl is the contractual billing signal and lurl is authoritative for losses. The ledger uses whole micro-dollars (µ$, millionths of a dollar). The cap 6,324.555 µ$ is rounded up to c = 6,325 µ$, because rounding down would under-reserve on every bid. A billed 6,324.555 µ$ is also posted as 6,325 µ$, with the residue trued up at reconciliation. B = 2,000,000,000 µ$ ($2,000). Pitfall: releasing on a timer

Suppose event 5 had released j on its local timeout. Then R = 0, bid k is admitted (R = 6,325), and j's late burl posts 6,325 µ$ with no reservation behind it: C = 2,000,000,000 and C+R = 2,000,006,325 µ$ > B. If k also bills, confirmed spend overshoots by 6,325 µ$. One timeout costs one bid; a regional notice outage releases thousands.

A reservation may be released only on evidence that the obligation is gone: an authoritative loss notice, a cancellation, a reconciliation result, or a contractual cutoff that extinguishes any remaining payment. An empirical bound on callback delay, a local TTL or an expired lease is not such evidence. Without a discharge mechanism, the choice is between holding the reservation indefinitely and accepting statistical overspend within an explicit limit, which is no longer a hard budget.

A cap on the advertiser's CPA bill is a separate problem. Conversions from impressions already delivered keep arriving after buying stops, so that cap needs contract terms and explicit tracking of in-flight conversion obligations. The media ledger cannot stand in for it.

What full-cap reservation costs, and what the shortcut risks

Prompt loss notices make full-cap reservation cheap; reserving expected cost instead frees budget but gives up the guarantee. To size the first effect, use Little's law. Assume a steady state in which bids are admitted at rate \lambda per second (local symbol, not the appendix multiplier) and bid i stays open for time W_i with mean W. Over a long window of length T, the area under the open-count curve equals the sum of all holding times, about \lambda T\cdot W. Dividing by T gives the average number open:

\bar n_{\text{open}}=\lambda W,\qquad W=(1-F)\,T_{\text{loss}}+F\,T_{\text{bill}},

where F=b/L is the win probability, a loser waits T_{\text{loss}} on average for its loss notice and a winner waits T_{\text{bill}} for its billing notice, not its win notice.

Worked example

Budget locked by open reservations

Given: N = 1,000,000 bids spread evenly over one day; F = 0.316228 at the 6.324555 CPM bid with L = $0.02; cap 6,325 µ$; mean loss-notice delay 2 s and mean billing delay 60 s (illustrative).

  1. Arrival rate: \lambda = 1{,}000{,}000/86{,}400 \approx 11.574 bids per second.
  2. Mean holding time: \begin{aligned}W &= 0.683772\times 2 + 0.316228\times 60\\ &\approx 20.341\ \text{s}\end{aligned}
  3. Open reservations: \lambda W \approx 235.43, locking 235.43\times\$0.006325\approx\$1.49 of the $2,000 budget.
  4. At a 1-hour mean billing delay: W \approx 1{,}139.79 s, so about 13,192 reservations and $83.44 are locked.

Result: about 235 open reservations and $1.49 locked; billing delay, not loss-notice delay, dominates.

Check: winners contribute 11.574\times0.316228\times60\approx219.6 of the 235.43 open reservations, losers the remaining 11.574\times0.683772\times2\approx15.8.

The shortcut is to reserve expected cost, win probability times bid, instead of the cap. That admits about 1/F times as many concurrent bids near the end of a budget, but overspend is then controlled only statistically. Its tail depends on how correlated the outcomes are, and incidents are exactly what makes outcomes correlated.

Worked example

Reserve the cap or reserve the expected cost?

Given: remaining headroom H = $10 near the end of the period; bid b = $0.006324555 per impression, win probability F = b/L = 0.31622775; every admitted bid stays open until the headroom is used up, then all resolve; overshoot means confirmed spend above H.

  1. Full-cap reservation, c = 6,325 µ$, admits \lfloor 10{,}000{,}000 / 6{,}325 \rfloor = 1{,}581 bids. Even if all win, spend is 1{,}581 \times \$0.006324555 \approx \$9.999 \le H.
  2. Expected-cost reservation is \begin{aligned}F\,b &= b^2/L\\ &= 0.31622775 \times 0.006324555\\ &\approx \$0.002000 = 2{,}000\ \mu\$,\end{aligned} so k = 10{,}000{,}000 / 2{,}000 = 5{,}000 bids are admitted, with expected spend 5{,}000 \times \$0.002 = \$10.00 = H.
  3. Overshoot is b\,(W-kF), where W is now the number of wins (local symbol). If outcomes are independent, W\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(5{,}000,\,0.316228) with standard deviation \sqrt{5000 \times 0.316228 \times 0.683772} \approx 32.88. With the normal approximation (reasonable here since kF(1-F)\approx1{,}081), the P99 lies 2.326 standard deviations above the mean, so the P99 overshoot is \begin{aligned}&2.326 \times 32.88 \times \$0.006324555\\ &\approx \$0.48.\end{aligned}
  4. Perfectly correlated outcomes (a competitor drops out, or a pricing bug raises every win rate): all 5,000 win, spend 5{,}000 \times \$0.006324555 \approx \$31.62, overshoot \$31.62 - \$10 = \$21.62.

Result: expected-cost reservation admits 5,000 bids instead of 1,581; its overshoot is about $0.48 at P99 under independence and $21.62 (216% of headroom) when outcomes move together.

Check: the concurrency gain is 6{,}325/2{,}000 \approx 3.16 \approx 1/F = 3.162, and 5{,}000/1{,}581 \approx 3.16. The worst case also equals k\,b\,(1-F) = 5{,}000 \times 0.006324555 \times 0.683772 \approx \$21.62.

Optional: partial correlation, and how overspend grows with bids in flight

Between independence and the all-win case, let the win indicators X_1,\dots,X_k each have mean F and a common pairwise correlation \kappa\ge0. Then \operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)=\kappa F(1-F) for i\ne j, and

\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Var}(W)&=\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(X_i)+\sum_{i\ne j}\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)\\ &=kF(1-F)+k(k-1)\,\kappa F(1-F)\\ &=kF(1-F)\,\bigl(1+(k-1)\kappa\bigr).\end{aligned}

At k=5{,}000, even \kappa=0.01 multiplies the standard deviation by \sqrt{1+4{,}999\times0.01}\approx7.14, so the standard deviation of the overshoot rises from 32.88\times\$0.006324555\approx\$0.21 to about $1.48. The normal approximation no longer holds here: correlated wins follow a mixture, not a binomial, so the $0.48 P99 cannot simply be rescaled. The standard deviation alone shows how fragile that figure is.

Measure the cost of conservative reservation as lost gross spread, not as lock contention or budget-service load.

Releases can change what the system optimizes

In an ad system a release can change the objective itself, not just latency or error rates. Three traps are specific to advertising.

  • Labels must be matured. Train only on examples whose attribution window has closed, so that a missing conversion is a real negative (Chapter 5). If one release changes both the model and the attribution window, you cannot tell why online results moved. Version the model, the calibrator (the map from raw score to probability), feature definitions and budget-control parameters together, or a rollback may not restore the old behavior.
  • A shadow model cannot show profit. A shadow deployment scores live traffic without driving decisions. It exposes latency and score-distribution anomalies, but it buys nothing, so it cannot show that the model makes money.
  • A canary buys different traffic. When a new model controls a share of live bids, it changes which opportunities are won and at what prices. That selection is part of its effect. Compare arms under the same budget constraint, on matured labels, and check traffic mix and price distribution before reading the result (Chapter 6).

Decision logs make this analysis possible only if each bid opportunity links its feature versions, predictions, bid policy, experiment arm, outcome reasons and later billing and attribution events.

Monitoring should tie each signal to an economic consequence and a first check; thresholds come from contracts and loss tolerance. Duplicate billing, budget violations and changes to value definitions should each trigger a stop-loss on their own, because conversion reports arrive days too late.
