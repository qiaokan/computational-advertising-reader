Comprehensive exercises and answers

Ten questions, 100 points

Shared parameters for Questions 2–4. These are the numbers of the running campaign introduced in “A fictional campaign used throughout” (A one-day learning roadmap) and worked through in From prediction to bidding and budgets.

  • Opportunities. N=1{,}000{,}000 statistically identical bid opportunities (bid requests from publishers). Every auction win yields exactly one successful impression; display failures are ignored here (Real-time advertising systems relaxes this).
  • Contracts. The ad network sells to the advertiser on CPA terms: it charges $30 per billable attributed conversion, i.e. a conversion that the agreed attribution rules credit to the ad and that the contract makes payable. It buys impressions from publishers in first-price auctions: the winner pays its own bid, per impression.
  • Conversion probability. Each impression produces a billable attributed conversion with probability q=0.0005, the same for every opportunity and independent of the bid and of competition. The network's expected collections per impression are therefore v=30q=0.015 dollars.
  • Advertiser economics. Pre-advertising contribution per order is g=40 dollars: sales revenue minus goods, fulfillment and other per-order variable costs, before ad spend and fixed costs. The true causal lift is \Delta q=0.0002: for the same opportunity, showing the ad raises the probability of an order by 0.0002 compared with not showing it. Attributed conversions are not all caused by the ad, so \Delta q<q.
  • Competition. The competitive threshold Z is the highest competing bid for an opportunity, in dollars per impression. Assume Z\sim\text{Uniform}(0,L) with L=0.02, independently across opportunities. A bid b wins when b\ge Z, so the win rate is F(b)=b/L for 0\le b\le L.
  • Objective. Unless a question says otherwise, the network picks one uniform bid b for all opportunities to maximize expected media gross spread: expected collections from the advertiser minus expected payments to publishers. There is no budget, except in Question 4, which adds a media budget B=2{,}000 dollars on expected media cost over the N opportunities.
  • Units. “Spend”, “bill” and “collections” mean expected values. CPM is a price per thousand impressions; eCPM (effective CPM) expresses any expected amount per impression as an amount per thousand impressions. Convert dollars per impression to CPM by multiplying by 1,000.

Each question is worth 10 points. Spend 30 minutes answering on your own, then 20 minutes checking against the answers. Calculation questions are graded on the numbers and on whether you name the right party, units and assumptions. A score of at least 80, with no conceptual errors on Questions 2, 6 and 8, means you have a working framework for discussing and reviewing ad systems. This is this reader's own pass mark, not an industry certification.

Question 1: Product dimensions. An ad runs in a mobile game's rewarded video slot (a video the player chooses to watch in exchange for an in-game reward), and the impression is bought in a real-time auction. The publisher charges the buyer per impression. The advertiser has set a target CPA (tCPA) of $30, the average cost per conversion the bidding algorithm aims for (Products and cash flow). Can you conclude that the advertiser pays only when a conversion happens? Which two kinds of contract information are missing?
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 1. No. A target CPA is an optimization target, not a billing unit. An advertiser can be billed per impression or per click while the bidder steers toward an average of $30 per conversion. The two missing kinds of information are:

  1. The advertiser-side billing basis: is the advertiser billed per impression, click, install or conversion, and at what price?
  2. The definition of a billable conversion: which event counts, the attribution window, deduplication rules, and refund and chargeback rules.

The publisher-side terms in the question (an auction, priced per impression) say nothing about the advertiser side; the two are separate contracts. Each side's budget must also be confirmed separately and cannot be inferred from the target CPA. Scoring: 5 points for separating the optimization target from the billing basis, 5 points for clearly naming both kinds of missing information.

Question 2: Three values. Using the shared parameters above, compute three values, each as an eCPM: (a) the network's expected collections; (b) the advertiser's attributed contribution, i.e. attributed conversions valued at g each; (c) the advertiser's incremental contribution, i.e. caused orders valued at g each. Why can't the network simply replace its own receivables estimate with the advertiser's incremental contribution?
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 2.

\begin{aligned} \text{(a) network collections} &= 1000\cdot q\cdot 30 = 1000\times 0.015 = \$15\ \text{eCPM}\\ \text{(b) attributed contribution} &= 1000\cdot q\cdot g = 1000\times 0.0005\times 40 = \$20\ \text{eCPM}\\ \text{(c) incremental contribution} &= 1000\cdot \Delta q\cdot g = 1000\times 0.0002\times 40 = \$8\ \text{eCPM} \end{aligned}

The network's receivables are fixed by the contract: it collects $30 for every billable attributed conversion, whether or not the ad caused it. The advertiser's incremental contribution measures what the ads are really worth to the advertiser, and so its long-run willingness to keep paying. It does not change the probability that a given impression triggers a contractual payment, so substituting it for the receivables estimate would misprice every bid. Note what the numbers imply: the advertiser pays $15 per thousand impressions for $8 of incremental contribution, losing $7 per thousand. That is a churn risk the network should track as a separate metric or constraint, not by rewriting its receivables forecast. Scoring: 2 points for each of the three numbers, 4 points for distinguishing the two objectives.

Question 3: First-price bidding. With v=0.015, Z\sim\text{Uniform}(0,0.02) and no budget, derive the optimal uniform bid, the win rate, and the expected media spend and network collections over N=1{,}000{,}000 requests. Then give one condition under which the rule “bid half the value” fails.
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 3. Expected gross spread per request is the margin on a win times the win probability:

\begin{aligned} m(b) &= (v-b)\,\frac{b}{L} = \frac{(0.015-b)\,b}{0.02},\\ m'(b) &= \frac{v-2b}{L}=0 \;\Rightarrow\; b^{*}=\frac{v}{2}=0.0075 . \end{aligned}

m is concave and b^{*}\le L, so this is the maximum: a bid of $7.50 CPM. The win rate is 0.0075/0.02=37.5\%, giving 375,000 impressions; media spend is 375{,}000\times 0.0075=\$2{,}812.50, network collections 375{,}000\times 0.015=\$5{,}625, and gross spread $2,812.50.

“Bid half the value” is specific to a uniform threshold starting at zero, with no other constraints. It fails, for example, when the threshold distribution is not uniform; when a budget binds (Question 4); when there is a floor price (a seller's minimum accepted price) or there are fees on top of the bid; or when value is correlated with the threshold. Even with a uniform distribution it fails if the lower bound a is positive (the optimum becomes (v+a)/2) or if v/2>L (the bid is capped at L). Scoring: derivation 4 points, numbers 4 points, failure condition 2 points.

Question 4: Whose budget? Add a media budget of $2,000 that caps the network's expected media cost over the N opportunities. Find the optimal first-price uniform bid and the advertiser's expected bill. If the advertiser then says it also has a hard budget of $2,000 on its own bill, is that bid still acceptable? Bonus: suppose instead that the advertiser's expected bill is capped at $2,000, while the $2,000 media budget stays in place. Find the optimal uniform bid. Does it guarantee that the actual bill never exceeds $2,000?
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 4. Expected media cost is C(b)=N\cdot\frac{b}{L}\cdot b=Nb^2/L, so the budget requires

b\le\sqrt{\frac{BL}{N}}=\sqrt{\frac{2000\times 0.02}{1{,}000{,}000}}\approx 0.0063246 .

Gross spread increases in b up to v/2=0.0075, which lies above this limit, so the budget binds: b^{*}=\min\bigl(v/2,\ \sqrt{BL/N},\ L\bigr)\approx 0.0063246, i.e. $6.3246 CPM. The win rate is 31.62%, giving about 316,228 impressions and 158.11 expected attributed conversions. The advertiser's expected bill is about $4,743.42 and the gross spread about $2,743.42.

A $2,000 hard budget on the advertiser's bill rules this bid out: the expected bill alone is $4,743.42. The media budget limits what the network pays publishers, not what it bills the advertiser. A cap on the advertiser's bill has to be enforced separately on the CPA receivables side, including conversions still to come from impressions already bought. Scoring: calculation 6 points, identifying whose budget each constraint is 4 points.

Question 4 bonus. The expected bill is A(b)=N v\,b/L. Requiring A(b)\le 2000 gives

b\le\frac{2000\times 0.02}{1{,}000{,}000\times 0.015}\approx 0.0026667 .

Gross spread still increases in b over this range, and media cost at this bid is far below $2,000, so the optimum is b^{*}\approx 0.0026667 ($2.67 CPM). The win rate is 13.33%, about 133,333 impressions and 66.67 expected conversions; media cost is Nb^2/L\approx\$355.56 and gross spread about $1,644.44.

This satisfies only the expected-value constraint. Conversions are random: treating them as Poisson with mean 66.67, the standard deviation is about 8.2 conversions (about $245), and the bill exceeds $2,000 (67 or more conversions) with probability about 50%. Conversions also arrive with a lag after the impressions are bought. A hard cap therefore needs either an earlier stop that leaves room for conversions still in flight, or a contract term under which conversions beyond the cap are not billed and the network absorbs them. The bonus is not scored.

Question 5: Calibration and sampling. A conversion model is trained on downsampled data: every positive example is kept, and each negative is kept independently with probability 10%, regardless of its features (Attribution, experiments, and data). Training applies no weight correction, and the model is calibrated on the sampled distribution. For some feature vector it outputs 0.5. What is the true conversion probability? Separately: a new model has a higher AUC than the current one, but its predicted probabilities are about twice the observed conversion rates across the board. Can it go live as is?
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 5. With negative retention rate s=0.1 and sampled-data prediction p_s=0.5:

p=\frac{s\,p_s}{1-p_s+s\,p_s}=\frac{0.1\times 0.5}{1-0.5+0.1\times 0.5}=\frac{1}{11}\approx 0.0909 .

Equivalently, downsampling negatives multiplies the odds by 1/s, so the true odds are 0.1\times 1=0.1.

No, it cannot go live as is. AUC depends only on ranking and is unchanged by any monotone transformation of the scores, so it says nothing about calibration. Bids depend on the absolute probability: v=30q, so doubling q doubles the value the bidder thinks it is buying. In the shared example without a budget, the bid would rise from $7.50 to $15 CPM, where the true expected gross spread (0.015-b)\,b/L is zero. Before launch, check that the sampling correction was applied, recalibrate on matured labels, check calibration by segment (a uniform factor of 2 rarely holds everywhere), evaluate the monetary loss rather than ranking metrics, confirm the labels have matured, and then run a limited online experiment. Scoring: number 4 points, calibration and launch logic 6 points.

Question 6: Incremental profit. This is the experiment from Attribution, experiments, and data. An eligible population is randomly split into a treatment group (the campaign may deliver) and a control group (it may not), 100,000 users each. Over the same matured window, treatment has 1,200 purchases and control 1,000. The attribution system credits 500 of the treatment purchases to the ad. Ad spend is $5,000. Net revenue per purchase is $50 (sales revenue after refunds and other adjustments, before goods, fulfillment and other costs), and the pre-advertising contribution rate is 40% of net revenue. Assume randomization and event definitions are correct and that the groups do not affect each other. Compute attributed ROAS (attributed revenue ÷ ad spend), incremental ROAS (iROAS: incremental revenue ÷ ad spend) and incremental contribution profit (incremental contribution − ad spend). Then explain why you cannot just compare the treatment users who actually saw the ad with the control group.
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 6. The groups are the same size, so incremental purchases are 1200-1000=200.

\begin{aligned} \text{attributed ROAS} &= \frac{500\times 50}{5000}=5\\ \text{iROAS} &= \frac{200\times 50}{5000}=2\\ \text{incremental contribution profit} &= 200\times 50\times 0.4-5000=-\$1{,}000 \end{aligned}

Break-even requires an iROAS of 1/0.4=2.5, so a 5× attributed ROAS coexists with a loss. These are point estimates. If each user buys at most once and users are independent, the standard error of the purchase difference is \sqrt{1200(1-0.012)+1000(1-0.010)}\approx 46.6, so an approximate 95% interval for incremental purchases is 109 to 291. Each incremental purchase is worth \$50\times0.4=\$20 of contribution, so profit =20\cdot\text{lift}-5{,}000 lies between about −$2,830 and +$830 (the same calculation as in chapter 4). The lift is clearly positive, but this sample does not settle the sign of the profit.

Who actually sees the ad is decided after randomization, by targeting, auction wins and how active each user is, and all of these correlate with the propensity to buy. Comparing exposed treatment users with the whole control group compares two populations that are no longer alike, which reintroduces selection bias. Analyze the ITT (intent to treat: the effect by original random assignment) first. Estimating the effect on exposed users needs extra assumptions or designs, for example dividing the ITT by the exposure rate (assuming no effect on unexposed users) or ghost ads that record which control users would have been exposed. Scoring: numbers 6 points, causal and statistical caveats 4 points.

Question 7: Price feedback. In a first-price auction you bid $8 CPM, win, and pay $8 CPM. Can you use $8 as a supervised label for that auction's competitive threshold Z (From prediction to bidding and budgets)? In a second-price auction, where the winner pays Z, what bias results if you fit a model of Z only on the prices you paid in auctions you won?
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 7. No. In first price you pay your own bid, so a win tells you only that Z\le 8: a censored observation that bounds Z from above, not a label. A loss tells you Z>b. You learn more only if the exchange returns an extra signal, such as the minimum bid that would have won.

In second price, a win reveals Z exactly, but only when Z\le b. Training on wins alone drops every auction with Z>b, so the sample is truncated from above. The fitted distribution of Z is biased low: it underestimates the threshold, overestimates the win rate at any given bid, and understates the cost of winning. The bias is worst on contested, high-value inventory where you lose most often. The fix is to keep the losses as censored observations at b and use a censored likelihood (each win contributes the density at Z, each loss 1-F(b)) or a survival-analysis estimator. Scoring: 5 points per part.

Question 8: Ledger states. A bid is placed under Case A in “How delivery probability changes the bid” (Real-time advertising systems): the publisher is paid only after a successful impression, and the contract names the OpenRTB billing notice burl as the signal that confirms billing. Before the bid was sent, the budget system reserved the bid's maximum possible cost. After the bid, a win notice (nurl) arrives but no billing notice; then a duplicate of the same win notice arrives. Can the system book this as confirmed media spend? Can it release the whole reservation? How should it handle the billing notice when it later arrives twice?
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 8.

  • Confirm spend? No. Under this contract, winning the auction does not create a payment obligation; only a successful impression does, and burl is what confirms it. The duplicate win notice is deduplicated on the bid's business key (exchange, request, impression and bid IDs) and changes nothing.
  • Release the whole reservation? No. The impression may still render and the billing notice may arrive late, so the external obligation is still open. Releasing the reservation would let the same budget be spent twice. It may be released only on authoritative evidence that the obligation is gone: a confirmed loss or cancellation, a reconciliation result, or a binding contractual cutoff. A local timeout is not such evidence.
  • Duplicate billing notice. The first valid burl converts the actual billed amount from reserved to confirmed spend and releases any difference between the reservation cap and that amount. The duplicate is an idempotent no-op on the ledger and is logged for reconciliation. Late, missing or mismatched notices go to a pending-reconciliation state and are settled against the exchange's settlement reports. An amount above the reservation cap means the cost-cap premise has broken and goes to exception handling.

Scoring: distinguishing the states 4 points, managing the open obligation 3 points, idempotency and reconciliation 3 points.

Question 9: Offline versus online. A new conversion model beats the current one on offline log loss, but in an online A/B test the network's media gross spread (collections minus media cost, From prediction to bidding and budgets) falls. Give four distinct diagnostic hypotheses, and for two of them name the logs or metrics that would confirm or rule them out.
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 9. Candidate mechanisms include:

  1. Miscalibration where it matters: average log loss improves while calibration on high-bid traffic worsens, causing overbidding. In first price you win precisely where you overestimate.
  2. Price or traffic-mix shift: offline evaluation uses traffic selected by the old model; the new model bids differently and wins traffic and prices the evaluation set never covered.
  3. Label problems: label delay (immature conversions in the offline set) or leakage (features carrying post-impression information) inflate the offline score.
  4. Training–serving skew: mismatched feature and model versions or features missing online; higher latency causing more timeouts.
  5. Downstream interactions: bid shading, pacing or budget control tuned to the old score distribution, including budget-control oscillation.
  6. Measurement changes: different fees or attribution definitions between the arms.

Evidence for two of them. Calibration: predicted versus matured realized conversion rates, sliced by arm, publisher and predicted-score bucket. Overbidding and traffic selection: per-arm logs of bids, win rate, clearing price, traffic composition, cost per impression and collections per impression. Scoring: four distinct mechanisms 6 points in total, two pieces of evidence that would actually localize the problem 4 points in total.

Question 10: Evidence for a moat. MAX is AppLovin's mediation product, which runs publishers' auctions across demand sources; Adjust is its MMP (mobile measurement partner), which attributes conversions on behalf of advertisers; Axon is its prediction and bidding engine (AppLovin and Axon). Someone claims: “MAX sees all bids and Adjust has all the attribution data, so Axon necessarily trains on all of it, and a competitor that can replicate the technology only needs to subsidize data purchases to catch up.” Identify at least four missing links in this argument, and propose one validation metric better than total conversion volume.
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 10. Missing links:

  1. Data a system sees is not necessarily data it may train on: MAX receives bids in order to run auctions, and contracts and privacy rules govern other uses. MAX also sees only the auctions it mediates, not all bids.
  2. Data permitted for training does not necessarily enter the training pipeline.
  3. Owning a measurement company is not owning its customers' data: Adjust processes attribution data on advertisers' behalf.
  4. More samples are not automatically unbiased or transferable: data collected under one delivery policy and one supply mix is selection-biased.
  5. The same model does not mean the same supply access and execution conditions.
  6. Predicting short-term responses well is not delivering incremental profit to advertisers.
  7. Subsidies cannot show that advertisers stay once full pricing starts.

Better metrics: advertiser retention and spend after subsidies end; matured incremental contribution (from holdout tests) under the same budget constraint; or the marginal-return curve of additional budget. Scoring: four gaps 8 points, a suitable metric 2 points.
