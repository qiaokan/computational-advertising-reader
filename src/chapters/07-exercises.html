Ten questions, 100 points

CPA-contract parameters for Questions 2, 5 and 9. The running campaign from “A fictional campaign used throughout” (A one-day learning roadmap), worked through in the optional network-spread appendix. Questions 3–4 separately specify the main advertiser direct-buying contract.

  • Opportunities. N=1{,}000{,}000 statistically identical bid opportunities (bid requests from publishers). Every auction win yields exactly one successful impression; display failures are ignored here (Real-time advertising systems relaxes this).
  • Contracts. The ad network sells to the advertiser on CPA terms: it charges a=\$30 per billable attributed conversion, a conversion that the agreed attribution rules credit to the ad and that the contract makes payable. It buys impressions from publishers in first-price auctions: the winner pays its own bid, per impression.
  • Conversion probability. Each impression produces a billable attributed conversion with probability q=0.0005, the same for every opportunity and independent of the bid and of competition. The network's expected collections per impression are v=qa=\$0.015.
  • Advertiser economics. Pre-advertising contribution per order is g=\$40: sales revenue minus goods, fulfillment and other per-order variable costs, before ad spend and fixed costs. The true causal lift is \Delta q=0.0002: for the same opportunity, showing the ad raises the probability of an order by 0.0002 compared with not showing it. Here \Delta q<q is a property of the given numbers. The two probabilities measure different events and neither bounds the other in general; attribution can both credit nonincremental orders and miss incremental ones.
  • Competition. The competitive threshold Z is the highest competing bid for an opportunity, in dollars per impression, with Z\sim\text{Uniform}(0,L), L=\$0.02, independently across opportunities. A bid b wins when b\ge Z, so the win rate is F(b)=b/L for 0\le b\le L.
  • Objective. In the optional network-spread appendix, the network picks one uniform bid b for all opportunities to maximize expected media gross spread: expected collections from the advertiser minus expected payments to publishers. That appendix uses a network media budget, which is not the advertiser spend cap in Questions 3–4.
  • Units. “Spend”, “bill” and “collections” mean expected values. CPM is a price per thousand impressions; eCPM (effective CPM) expresses any expected amount per impression as an amount per thousand impressions. Dollars per impression × 1,000 = CPM.
Question 1: Product dimensions. An ad runs in a mobile game's rewarded video slot (a video the player chooses to watch in exchange for an in-game reward), and the impression is bought in a real-time auction. The publisher charges the buyer per impression. The advertiser has set a target CPA (tCPA) of $30, the average cost per conversion the bidding algorithm aims for (Products and cash flow). Can you conclude that the advertiser pays only when a conversion happens? Which two terms of the advertiser's contract would you need to see to settle it?
Reference answers and scoring points

Answer: no. A target CPA is an optimization goal for the bidder, not a billing unit. An advertiser can be billed per impression or per click while the bidder steers toward an average of $30 per conversion.

A full-marks answer contains:

  1. Target ≠ billing event. tCPA says what the algorithm aims for; it says nothing about what triggers a charge.
  2. Missing term 1, the advertiser-side billing basis and price: billed per impression, click, install or conversion, and at what rate.
  3. Missing term 2, the definition of a billable conversion: which event counts, the attribution window, deduplication, and refund and chargeback rules. Even under per-conversion billing, “a conversion happens” means “a conversion the contract makes payable”.
  4. The publisher side is a separate contract. Per-impression buying from the publisher says nothing about how the advertiser is billed. If the advertiser pays per conversion while the network pays per impression, the network carries the conversion risk. Each side's budget must be confirmed separately; neither follows from the tCPA.

Scoring: 5 points for separating the optimization target from the billing basis; 5 points for naming both missing terms.

Question 2: Three values. Using the shared parameters above, compute three values, each as an eCPM (dollars per thousand impressions): (a) the network's expected collections; (b) the advertiser's attributed contribution, i.e. attributed conversions valued at g each; (c) the advertiser's incremental contribution, i.e. caused orders valued at g each. Why can't the network simply replace its own receivables estimate with the advertiser's incremental contribution?
Reference answers and scoring points
Worked solution

Three values per thousand impressions

Given: q=0.0005 billable attributed conversions per impression, a=\$30 per billable conversion, g=\$40 contribution per order, \Delta q=0.0002 caused orders per impression.

  1. (a) Network collections: each impression bills a with probability q. \begin{aligned}1000\,q\,a &= 1000\times 0.0005\times 30\\ &= 1000\times\$0.015 = \$15\ \text{eCPM}\end{aligned}
  2. (b) Attributed contribution: the same attributed conversions, valued at g. \begin{aligned}1000\,q\,g &= 1000\times 0.0005\times 40\\ &= 1000\times\$0.020 = \$20\ \text{eCPM}\end{aligned}
  3. (c) Incremental contribution: only caused orders count. \begin{aligned}1000\,\Delta q\,g &= 1000\times 0.0002\times 40\\ &= 1000\times\$0.008 = \$8\ \text{eCPM}\end{aligned}

Result: (a) $15 eCPM, (b) $20 eCPM, (c) $8 eCPM.

Check: (b)/(a) = g/a = 40/30 = 4/3 ✓, and (c)/(b) = \Delta q/q = 0.4 ✓. The advertiser pays $15 for $8 of incremental contribution, a loss of $7 per thousand impressions; equivalently its true incremental CPA is qa/\Delta q = 0.015/0.0002 = \$75 per caused order against g=\$40, matching chapter 3.

Why not substitute (c) for (a). A full-marks answer makes these points:

  • Receivables are set by the contract. The network collects $30 for every billable attributed conversion, whether or not the ad caused it. Its expected revenue per impression is qa; bids priced off \Delta q\,g would misprice every auction (here they would value an impression at $8 instead of $15 eCPM).
  • Incremental contribution is the advertiser's objective. It measures what the ads are really worth to the advertiser and so its long-run willingness to keep paying.
  • The gap is a separate risk. A $7 loss per thousand impressions is a churn risk. Track it as its own metric or constraint (an incremental-CPA or iROAS guardrail), not by rewriting the receivables forecast.

Scoring: 2 points for each number; 4 points for distinguishing the two objectives.

Question 3: Advertiser first-price bidding. Use the direct/pass-through contract in Chapter 3, not the CPA billing contract in Question 2. The advertiser pays the exact auction cost with no fees. Every win displays. Let N=10^6, Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) with L=\$0.02 per impression, and advertiser incremental contribution w=\Delta qg=0.0002\times\$40=\$0.008 per impression, independent of competition and bid. The advertiser chooses one bid b to maximize incremental contribution after ad spend. Without a budget, find the bid, expected impressions, ad spend and objective value. Explain why this is not the $7.50 CPM network-spread answer.

First-price cost per win is b; win probability is F(b)=b/L. Define J(b) as advertiser incremental contribution after cost:

J(b)=\frac NL(wb-b^2),\qquad J'(b)=\frac NL(w-2b),\qquad J''(b)=-\frac{2N}{L}<0.

The stationary point is the global maximum: b=w/2=\$0.004 per impression, or $4 CPM, within [0,L]. Expected impressions are 10^6(0.004/0.02)=200{,}000; advertiser spend is 200{,}000\times0.004=\$800; incremental contribution before spend is 200{,}000\times0.008=\$1{,}600; after spend it is $800. The appendix's $7.50 CPM bid uses network receipts v=qa=\$0.015 under CPA billing and maximizes a different party's ledger. Substituting that value for w changes the economic question.

Scoring: objective and contract 4 points; bid and ledger 4 points; distinction from network spread 2 points.

Question 4: An advertiser's budget is a cap. Keep Question 3's direct-buying contract and value. What happens to the optimal first-price bid with a $2,000 expected ad-spend cap? What if the cap is $500? Then find the ideal second-price bid and advertiser outcome under the $2,000 cap. Do these expected-budget solutions guarantee a realized hard spending limit?

For first price, let B be the advertiser's expected-spend cap. The best feasible bid is b_1^*=\min\{w/2,\sqrt{BL/N},L\}.

  1. At $2,000 the budget is slack: the $4 CPM profit-maximizing bid costs only $800. Expected incremental contribution after spend stays $800; the bidder does not force another $1,200 of spend.
  2. At $500 the budget binds: b_1^*=\sqrt{500\times0.02/10^6}=\$0.00316227766 per impression ($3.1623 CPM). Expected impressions are 158,113.88; contribution before ad spend is 158{,}113.883008\times0.008=\$1{,}264.911064; after $500 spend the objective is $764.91.
  3. For ideal second price, b_2^*=\min\{w,\sqrt{2BL/N},L\}. At $2,000 it bids $8 CPM, wins 400,000 impressions in expectation, and spends 10^6(0.008)^2/(2\times0.02)=\$1{,}600. Incremental contribution is $3,200 before spend and $1,600 after spend. The budget is slack.

All amounts above use unrounded inputs and are expected values. A realized hard cap needs separate reservation and settlement controls (Chapter 5). A $30 CPA contract would create a different advertiser bill; it cannot be inserted into these direct-cost equations without changing the model.

Scoring: $2,000 first-price case 3 points; $500 case 3 points; second price 2 points; hard-budget distinction 2 points.

Question 5: Calibration and sampling. A conversion model is trained on downsampled data: every positive example is kept, and each negative is kept independently with probability 10%, regardless of its features (Attribution, experiments, and data). Training applies no weight correction, and the model is calibrated on the sampled distribution. For some feature vector it outputs 0.5. What is the true conversion probability? Separately: a new model has a higher AUC than the current one, but its predicted probabilities are about twice the observed conversion rates across the board. Can it go live as is?
Reference answers and scoring points
Worked solution

Undoing negative downsampling

Given: negative retention rate s=0.1, positives all kept, sampled-data prediction p_s=0.5 (calibrated on the sampled data). Want: true probability p for the same feature vector.

  1. For a fixed feature vector, sampling keeps a fraction 1 of positives and s of negatives, so it multiplies the odds by 1/s: \frac{p_s}{1-p_s}=\frac{p}{(1-p)\,s}.
  2. True odds: s\cdot\frac{p_s}{1-p_s}=0.1\times\frac{0.5}{0.5}=0.1.
  3. Back to a probability: p=\frac{0.1}{1+0.1}=\frac{1}{11}\approx 0.0909.

Result: p = 1/11 ≈ 0.0909 (9.09%).

Check: the closed form p=\frac{s\,p_s}{1-p_s+s\,p_s}=\frac{0.05}{0.5+0.05}=0.0909 ✓; at s=1 it returns p=p_s, as it must.

The higher-AUC model cannot go live as is. A full-marks answer contains:

  • AUC is blind to calibration. It depends only on ranking and is unchanged by any strictly increasing transformation of the scores.
  • Bids use absolute probabilities. The network’s expected receipts per impression are v=30q, so doubling q doubles the value the bidder thinks it buys. In the shared example without a budget, the bid rises from v/2 = $7.50 CPM to (2v)/2=\$0.015 per impression = $15 CPM, where the true spread (0.015-b)\,b/L is zero: the whole $2,812.50 of spread per million requests is lost.
  • Launch checklist. Confirm the sampling correction was applied; recalibrate on matured labels; check calibration by segment (a uniform factor of 2 rarely holds everywhere); evaluate monetary loss, not ranking metrics; then run a limited online experiment.

Second-order point: under a binding budget a pacing multiplier would absorb a perfectly uniform factor for one campaign (the appendix’s $6.3246 CPM network bid is set by its binding $2,000 media budget, not by v), but across campaigns and segments with different errors it misallocates spend. Scoring: number 4 points; calibration and launch logic 6 points.

Question 6: Incremental profit. This is the experiment from Attribution, experiments, and data. An eligible population is randomly split into a treatment group (the campaign may deliver) and a control group (it may not), 100,000 users each. Over the same matured window, treatment has 1,200 purchases and control 1,000. The attribution system credits 500 of the treatment purchases to the ad. Ad spend is $5,000. Net revenue per purchase is $50 (sales revenue after refunds and other adjustments, before goods, fulfillment and other costs), and the pre-advertising contribution rate is 40% of net revenue. Assume randomization and event definitions are correct and that the groups do not affect each other. Compute attributed ROAS (attributed revenue ÷ ad spend), incremental ROAS (iROAS: incremental revenue ÷ ad spend) and incremental contribution profit (incremental contribution − ad spend). Then explain why you cannot just compare the treatment users who actually saw the ad with the control group.
Reference answers and scoring points
Worked solution

Attributed versus incremental returns

Given: 100,000 users per arm; purchases 1,200 (treatment) and 1,000 (control); 500 attributed purchases; ad spend $5,000; net revenue $50 per purchase; contribution rate 40%.

  1. Incremental purchases (equal arms, so compare counts directly): 1{,}200-1{,}000=200.
  2. Attributed ROAS: \frac{500\times\$50}{\$5{,}000}=\frac{\$25{,}000}{\$5{,}000}=5.0
  3. iROAS: \frac{200\times\$50}{\$5{,}000}=\frac{\$10{,}000}{\$5{,}000}=2.0
  4. Incremental contribution profit: contribution per purchase \$50\times0.4=\$20, so \begin{aligned}&200\times\$20-\$5{,}000\\ &=\$4{,}000-\$5{,}000\\ &=-\$1{,}000.\end{aligned}
  5. Break-even: iROAS 1/0.4=2.5, i.e. \$5{,}000/\$20=250 incremental purchases.
  6. Uncertainty (assuming each user buys at most once and users are independent): \begin{aligned}\text{SE}^2&=1200(1-0.012)\\ &\quad+1000(1-0.010)\\ &=1185.6+990=2175.6,\\ \text{SE}&=\sqrt{2175.6}\approx 46.6.\end{aligned} 95% interval for the lift: 200\pm1.96\times46.6\approx[109,\,291]. Profit is 20\cdot\text{lift}-5{,}000, so it lies in about [-\$2{,}830,\,+\$830].

Result: attributed ROAS 5.0, iROAS 2.0, incremental contribution profit −$1,000 (95% interval about −$2,830 to +$830).

Check: attribution credits 500/200 = 2.5 times the measured lift, and attributed ROAS ÷ iROAS = 5.0/2.0 = 2.5 ✓. The interval matches chapter 4.

Why not compare exposed treatment users with control. Full-marks points:

  • Exposure is decided after randomization, by targeting, auction wins and how active each user is, all of which correlate with the propensity to buy.
  • Exposed treatment users and the whole control group are therefore no longer comparable populations: the comparison reintroduces selection bias, typically inflating the effect.
  • Analyze the ITT (intent to treat: the effect by original random assignment) first.
  • The effect on exposed users needs extra assumptions or designs: divide the ITT by the exposure rate (assuming no effect on unexposed users), or use ghost ads that record which control users would have been exposed.

Scoring: numbers 6 points; causal and statistical caveats 4 points.

Question 7: Price feedback. In a first-price auction you bid $8 CPM, win, and pay $8 CPM. Can you use $8 as a supervised label for that auction's competitive threshold Z (From prediction to bidding and budgets)? In a second-price auction, where the winner pays Z, what bias results if you fit a model of Z only on the prices you paid in auctions you won?
Reference answers and scoring points

First price: no. You pay your own bid, so the price carries no information about Z beyond the win itself. Full-marks points:

  • A win says only Z\le 8 (CPM): a censored observation bounding Z from above, not a label.
  • A loss says only Z>b.
  • You learn more only if the exchange returns an extra signal, such as the minimum bid that would have won.

Second price: downward bias. Full-marks points:

  • A win reveals Z exactly, but only when Z\le b; training on wins alone drops every auction with Z>b, truncating the sample from above.
  • The fitted Z is biased low: it underestimates the threshold and overestimates the win rate at every bid. Cost errors depend on the bid evaluated. Below the logged bid b, the predicted cost per win, E[Z\mid Z\le b'], is correct, but the inflated win rate overstates expected cost per request. Above b, the model has no data and understates the cost per win. The bias is worst on contested, high-value inventory, where you lose most often.
  • Fix: keep losses as censored observations at b and maximize the censored likelihood (each win contributes the density f(Z), each loss 1-F(b)), or use a survival-analysis estimator.
Illustration

How large the truncation bias is

Given (for illustration, not stated in the question): Z\sim\text{Uniform}(0,\$20\ \text{CPM}), the shared example's L, and you always bid $8 CPM in a second-price auction.

  1. Win rate: F(8)=8/20=40\%. The other 60% of auctions produce no price.
  2. Prices seen on wins: Z\mid Z\le 8\sim\text{Uniform}(0,8), mean $4 CPM. True mean: 20/2=\$10 CPM.
  3. A model fitted to those prices puts all its mass below $8 and predicts a 100% win rate at an $8 bid; the truth is 40%. Below $8 it gets the cost per win right (b'/2), but its win rate b'/8 is 2.5 times the true b'/20, so it overstates cost per request 2.5-fold. Above $8 it predicts $4 per win against a true b'/2.

Result: the wins-only estimate of mean Z is $4 CPM against a true $10 CPM.

Check: in general the wins-only mean is b/2, which reaches the true L/2 only at b=L, when you win everything and nothing is censored ✓.

Question 8: Ledger states. A bid is placed under display-based billing, where only a confirmed display incurs a charge (Case A) (Real-time advertising systems): the publisher is paid only after a successful impression, and the contract names the OpenRTB billing notice burl as the signal that confirms billing. Before the bid was sent, the budget system reserved the bid's maximum possible cost. After the bid, a win notice (nurl) arrives but no billing notice; then a duplicate of the same win notice arrives. Can the system book this as confirmed media spend? Can it release the whole reservation? How should it handle the billing notice when it later arrives twice?
Reference answers and scoring points
  • Confirm spend? No. Under this contract a win creates no payment obligation; only a successful impression does, and burl is what confirms it. The duplicate nurl is deduplicated on the bid's business key (exchange, request, impression and bid IDs) and changes nothing.
  • Release the whole reservation? No. The impression may still render and burl may arrive late, so the external obligation is still open. Releasing the reservation would let the same budget be spent twice. Release it only on authoritative evidence that the obligation is gone: a confirmed loss or cancellation, a reconciliation result, or a binding contractual cutoff. A local timeout is not such evidence.
  • Billing notice twice. The first valid burl moves the actual billed amount from reserved to confirmed spend and releases the difference between the reservation cap and that amount. The duplicate is an idempotent no-op on the ledger, logged for reconciliation. Late, missing or mismatched notices go to a pending-reconciliation state and are settled against the exchange's settlement reports. An amount above the cap means the cost-cap premise has broken: send it to exception handling.

The ledger, with cap c (the reservation) and actual billed amount p\le c, starting from confirmed spend C and open reservations R:

Only admission raises confirmed + reserved, and admission checks it against the budget; every later step keeps it flat or lowers it. That is what makes the hard budget safe. Scoring: distinguishing the states 4 points; managing the open obligation 3 points; idempotency and reconciliation 3 points.

Question 10: Evidence for a moat. MAX is AppLovin's mediation product, which runs publishers' auctions across demand sources; Adjust is its MMP (mobile measurement partner), which attributes conversions on behalf of advertisers; Axon is its prediction and bidding engine (AppLovin and Axon). Someone claims: “MAX sees all bids and Adjust has all the attribution data, so Axon necessarily trains on all of it, and a competitor that can replicate the technology only needs to subsidize data purchases to catch up.” Identify at least four missing links in this argument, and propose one validation metric better than total conversion volume.
Reference answers and scoring points

Missing links (any four, each stated as the specific step that fails):

  1. Seeing is not permission. MAX receives bids to run auctions; contracts and privacy rules govern any other use. MAX also sees only the auctions it mediates, not all bids.
  2. Permission is not use. Data allowed for training does not necessarily enter the training pipeline.
  3. Processing is not ownership. Adjust processes attribution data on advertisers' behalf; owning the MMP is not owning its customers' data.
  4. More data is not unbiased or transferable data. Samples collected under one delivery policy and supply mix are selection-biased for other policies.
  5. Same model, different access. Replicating the technology does not replicate supply access and execution conditions.
  6. Prediction is not incrementality. Predicting short-term responses well is not delivering incremental profit to advertisers.
  7. Subsidies prove nothing about retention. They cannot show that advertisers stay once full pricing starts; and some of the data is not for sale at any price.

Better metrics (any one, with why it beats volume): advertiser retention and spend after subsidies end; matured incremental contribution from holdout tests under the same budget constraint; or the marginal-return curve of additional budget. Each measures value delivered to the advertiser rather than activity. Scoring: four gaps 8 points; a suitable metric 2 points.
