Real-time advertising systems

In this chapter

  • Split a 120 ms tmax into network allowance, safety margin and per-stage deadlines, and see why per-stage P99s do not compose into an end-to-end P99.
  • Keep the hard-budget invariant C+R\le B through admit, win notice, billing notice, duplicate notice and timeout, and know which evidence may release a reservation.
  • Choose a deduplication key that turns at-least-once notices into one ledger posting per billable event, and recognize the two ways a wrong key fails.
  • Quantify what the display contract does to the bid (6.666667 vs 6.324555 CPM, $230.93 of spread), and what never releasing reservations costs ($2,743.42 of spread falls to $867.54).

Organize the system around three pipelines

An ad network (the intermediary that buys impressions from publishers and sells results to advertisers; see Chapter 1 and Chapter 2) runs three pipelines. The control pipeline manages contracts, campaigns (an advertiser's unit of budget, targeting and goals), creatives (the ad assets themselves), budgets and configuration releases. The online pipeline answers each bid request from an exchange before its deadline: it filters for eligibility, retrieves candidates, predicts responses and computes a bid. The event pipeline records delivery, billing, attribution, training data and reconciliation.

Terms used in the diagram: eligibility is the set of hard filters a campaign must pass before it is scored (targeting, available budget, approved creative, publisher and advertiser block lists, consent and regulatory rules). Retrieval cheaply narrows the eligible set to a few hundred candidates; ranking orders them by predicted value. Pacing is the online control of spend rate across the budget period (Chapter 3). The online snapshot is a versioned, read-only copy of configuration and features pushed to serving nodes. The diagram is a teaching architecture. It is not the known implementation of Axon or of any other company.

The hard part of an ad system is not picking a database for each box. It is getting the boxes to agree on a verifiable account of what happened and how much is owed to whom. A successful HTTP response proves neither that an impression was delivered nor that a conversion occurred, let alone that the advertiser benefited.

One impression spans about nine orders of magnitude of time, from milliseconds to months, and each stage is evidence of something different. The time scales below are illustrative; the integration contract sets the real ones.

  1. 0 ms
    Bid request. The exchange sends the request; the tmax window is measured at the exchange.
  2. ≤ 120 ms
    Bid response. Must reach the exchange inside tmax. The budget reservation is made before the response leaves.
  3. + ms to s
    Auction result. Win notice (nurl) or loss notice (lurl), if the exchange sends them. A win is not a delivered or billable impression.
  4. + seconds
    Render. The creative loads on the device and fires the impression pixel, or fails (probability 1-\rho below).
  5. + s to hours
    Billing notice. burl fires when the impression becomes billable under the exchange's policy; retries and late arrivals are normal. Reservation becomes confirmed spend.
  6. + days
    Clicks and conversions. Attributed inside the attribution window; create the CPA receivable. Refunds and chargebacks arrive later still.
  7. monthly
    Settlement. The exchange's statement, your log and the advertiser bill are reconciled; corrections are posted as compensating entries.

Request deadlines and per-stage computation

OpenRTB is the IAB Tech Lab's protocol family for real-time bidding: the exchange sends a bid request describing one or more impression opportunities (imp objects), and each bidder returns a bid response. The fields this chapter relies on have these OpenRTB 2.x meanings: OpenRTB 2.6 request and bid objects

  • tmax (request): the maximum time, in milliseconds, that the exchange allows for bids to be received, including internet latency.
  • price (bid): the bid price, quoted as CPM (dollars per thousand impressions; see Chapter 3) even though the transaction is for a single impression.
  • nurl (bid): the win notice URL, called if the bid wins. The spec states that a win does not necessarily mean a delivered, viewed or billable ad.
  • burl (bid): the billing notice URL, called when the winning bid becomes billable under the exchange's own business policy.
  • lurl (bid): the loss notice URL, called when the bid is known to have lost.

All three notice URLs are optional. Exchanges substitute macros into them, notably ${AUCTION_PRICE} (the clearing price, in the same currency and units as the bid, so also CPM; many exchanges encrypt it) and the request and impression ids (${AUCTION_ID}, ${AUCTION_IMP_ID}). ${AUCTION_BID_ID} echoes the response-level BidResponse.bidid, not the per-bid Bid.id; since the bidder writes the notice URLs itself, it should embed its own Bid.id in them. The deadlines and auction types in this chapter's examples are teaching assumptions, not industry facts.

Suppose a request carries tmax=120. This is a teaching value, not an industry service-level objective. Because the window includes the network round trip and the bidder cannot observe when the exchange sent the request, the bidder has well under 120 ms of compute: it subtracts estimated network time in both directions and a safety margin, then splits the remainder across feature reads, retrieval, inference, budget admission and response encoding. A model whose inference P99 (99th-percentile latency) is 20 ms does not guarantee an on-time end-to-end response. Candidate count, cache misses, fan-out and queueing set the tail, and per-stage P99s neither sum to nor bound the end-to-end P99.

Worked example

A 120 ms deadline, split into stage budgets

Given (all latencies illustrative): tmax = 120 ms; estimated network round-trip P99 of 30 ms for this exchange and region, measured as a round trip from the bidder's own logs (not the sum of two one-way P99s, which per the check below is not a bound); safety margin m = 10 ms; feature reads fan out to 20 shards in parallel.

  1. Network allowance: the round-trip P99. t_{\text{net}} = 30\ \text{ms}
  2. Compute deadline, measured from receipt at the bidder. \begin{aligned}D &= t_{\max} - t_{\text{net}} - m\\ &= 120 - 30 - 10 = 80\ \text{ms}\end{aligned}
  3. Split D into stage budgets: decode and eligibility 10, feature reads 15, retrieval 10, inference 30, budget admission 5, pricing and encoding 5, unallocated slack 5 (all in ms). 10+15+10+30+5+5+5 = 80
  4. Fan-out. If each of the 20 feature shards meets 15 ms at its own P99 and shards are independent, the share of requests with at least one slow shard is 1-0.99^{20} \approx 18.2\%. For the stage to meet 15 ms on 99% of requests, each shard must meet it at quantile 0.99^{1/20} \approx 0.99950, i.e. its P99.95, or the stage needs hedged reads or a default-feature fallback.

Result: 80 ms of compute after receipt, propagated as absolute deadlines (receipt time + offset) to each stage. A stage that misses its deadline takes its declared degradation path or the request no-bids.

Check: 30 + 10 + 80 = 120 ms. Stage P99s do not bound the end-to-end P99: let two stages each take 0 ms with probability 0.991 and 100 ms otherwise. Each has P99 = 0 ms, but \Pr(\text{sum}>0) = 1-0.991^2 \approx 1.79\% > 1\%, so the end-to-end P99 is 100 ms.

Every degradation path has an economic meaning. Filling missing features with defaults, switching to a smaller model and not bidding at all bias value estimates differently and carry different risks. If key configuration is stale, the budget has no available quota, or a creative's review status is unknown, bidding anyway can create real spend and breach contracts. Give each degradation path explicit preconditions, a maximum exposure in dollars and a logged reason code. Only then can you tell whether “higher availability” has merely moved losses into the ledger.

How a single request in the unified example is booked

Continue the unified example from Chapter 3: a first-price auction (the winner pays its own bid) and the budget-constrained bid of 6.324555 CPM, i.e. at most $0.006324555 of media cost for this one impression, with no fees for now. Assume the upstream contract defines a determinable billing rule and a maximum obligation per bid.

First the system fixes the bid's business identity, the tuple (exchange, BidRequest.id, Imp.id, Bid.id); the exchange is part of the key because request ids are unique only within one exchange, and Imp.id only within one request (it is often just 1). It also records the campaign, model, feature, configuration and experiment versions. Before the bid response leaves, the budget service must admit the bid and reserve its maximum possible spend.

Do not accumulate millions of amounts in floating point. Internally, use integers at an explicit scale or fixed-point decimals, and convert and round at protocol boundaries as the contract specifies (price is a float on the wire). Rounding direction matters. In micro-dollars (µ$, millionths of a dollar) the cap here is 6,324.555 µ$, which is not an integer: either use a finer scale (6,324,555 nano-dollars is exact) or round the reservation up to 6,325 µ$. Rounding down to 6,324 µ$ under-reserves by 0.555 µ$ on every bid and silently breaks the hard-cap invariant below.

The exchange may then call nurl. The spec says this means the bid won the auction; it does not imply that the ad was delivered, viewed or billable. A burl call means the impression became billable under the exchange's business policy (typically on delivery or on view). Whether each notice is sent at all, how late it may arrive and which party's record prevails in a dispute are set by the integration contract. Do not assume every exchange implements every optional field. OpenRTB 2.6 bid notification semantics

After a win, the network carries an open obligation for that bid. When a contractually recognized billing signal arrives, it books the actual billed amount (for example ${AUCTION_PRICE}/1000 for one impression) as spend and releases the difference between the reserved cap and that amount. This assumes the actual amount does not exceed the cap. If it does, the cost-cap premise is broken: send the case to exception and risk handling, and stop claiming the hard-budget guarantee. A duplicate callback must produce at most one ledger change. A missing impression pixel (the tracking request the rendered ad fires from the device) does not prove there is no external debt, so you cannot wait out a local TTL and spend the amount again. Late notices and settlement discrepancies stay in a pending-reconciliation state until there is enough evidence to discharge the obligation.

“At most one ledger change per billing event” needs a precise mechanism. Transport is at-least-once: exchanges retry notices and your own queues redeliver. Derive the deduplication key from business identity and event type, e.g. (exchange, auction id, impression id, bid id, billing), not from a transport message id. The uniqueness check and the ledger posting must commit atomically, either in one transaction or by keying the posting row on the dedup key under a uniqueness constraint. Checking a dedup cache and posting in a separate step loses the guarantee on a crash or a race. Dedup state must also live at least as long as the counterparty may redeliver, or a late duplicate posts twice.

Worked example

Three billing notices, three candidate dedup keys

Given: three burl deliveries arrive, each at 6.324555 CPM, posted as 6,325 µ$ (cap and posting both rounded up to whole µ$). D1 is from exchange X for auction 7f3c, impression 1, our bid 9a01, transport message m-101. D2 is X's retry of D1 with a new transport message m-205. D3 is from exchange Y, which happens to mint the same auction id 7f3c and impression id 1, for our bid 9a77, message m-311. Two real billable events exist: D1 (= D2) and D3.

  1. Key = transport message id. All three ids differ, so all three post: 3 \times 6{,}325 = 18{,}975 µ$ against a true 12,650 µ$. C is overstated by 6,325 µ$: a double charge that surfaces as a settlement dispute.
  2. Key = (auction id, impression id, event type). D1, D2 and D3 share (7f3c, 1, billing), so only D1 posts: 6,325 µ$. C is understated by 6,325 µ$: the payable to Y is missing and bid 9a77's reservation stays open with no billing to discharge it. The budget is still safe, because that cap remains in R. It becomes an overspend only if the stuck reservation is later released without authoritative grounds, e.g. by a timer.
  3. Key = (exchange, auction id, impression id, bid id, event type). D1 and D3 have distinct keys; D2 collides with D1 and its insert fails the uniqueness constraint, rolling back its transaction: 2 \times 6{,}325 = 12{,}650 µ$.
  4. A fourth delivery D4 carries D1's key but ${AUCTION_PRICE} = 5.900000. It is not a duplicate: same key, different payload. Store a payload hash with the posting; on a key conflict with a hash mismatch, post nothing and route D4 to exception handling.

Result: only the full business key posts exactly once per real billable event (2 postings, 12,650 µ$). A key that is too fine double-posts; a key that is too coarse silently drops real debt.

Check: postings under each key equal the number of distinct key values among D1–D3: 3, 1 and 2. Only the last equals the 2 real events. If every Bid.id you mint is globally unique, (Bid.id, event type) alone would also give 2; the other fields then serve as a consistency check on the notice.

Clicks and conversions travel through a separate event pipeline. In the default example the publisher is paid per impression and the advertiser pays per billable conversion (CPA, cost per action; Chapter 3), so the event types, attribution windows and payment timing differ. After the media ledger confirms spend, the network may wait days to learn whether that impression produced a collectible conversion, and later still may see a chargeback or refund that reverses it. Store media payables (owed to publishers and exchanges), advertiser receivables and training/attribution events separately; a single “conversion success” field cannot stand in for all three.

Final reconciliation compares three records: the protocol events you logged, the publisher's or exchange's settlement statement (its periodic invoice of what it says you owe) and the advertiser's bill. Idempotent consumers give you effectively-once application of each event you received. They say nothing about events the counterparty sent that never arrived, or events you recorded that the counterparty did not. There is no end-to-end exactly-once across an organizational boundary. What you can build is a replayable event log, reversal (compensating) entries instead of in-place edits, a defined authority for corrections and an explanation for every discrepancy.

How delivery probability changes the bid

This section keeps the first-price auction and the Chapter 3 parameters:

  • b: our bid in dollars per billable unit (per successful impression in Case A, per win in Case B); the CPM quote is 1000b.
  • v = qa = 0.0005 \times \$30 = \$0.015: expected network revenue per successful impression, where q is the attributed conversion probability per impression and a the CPA price per conversion.
  • Z \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0, L) with L = \$0.02 ($20 CPM): the competitive threshold, i.e. the highest competing bid. Our win probability is F(b) = \Pr(Z \le b) = b/L for 0 \le b \le L.
  • N = 1{,}000{,}000 requests and media budget B = \$2{,}000, imposed on expected spend: N\,C_A(b) \le B or N\,C_B(b) \le B, with C_A, C_B defined next.
  • \pi_A, \pi_B: expected collections minus media cost (gross spread) per request under the two contracts; C_A, C_B: expected media cost per request. The subscripts A and B only label contracts and are unrelated to the budget B.

Now drop Chapter 3's assumption that every win is displayed and billed. Let \rho = 0.9 be the probability that a win produces a successful impression (the ad actually renders and is counted under the contract; it can fail because the creative does not load or the user leaves first). Assume \rho is independent of the bid and of Z, and that attributed conversions and network revenue arise only after a successful impression. We compare two fictional contracts devised for teaching; neither is claimed to be the default in OpenRTB or in MAX (AppLovin's mediation product; see Chapter 6). The objective is still to maximize expected gross spread subject to the expected budget.

Case A pays the publisher only after a successful impression. Expected gross spread and media cost per request are:

\pi_A(b)=\rho(v-b)F(b),\qquad C_A(b)=\rho bF(b).

Because \rho scales both collections and cost, the unconstrained optimum is still b = v/2 = \$0.0075 ($7.50 CPM). Its expected spend is N\rho (v/2)^2/L = \$2{,}531.25 > \$2{,}000, so the budget binds. Solving N\rho b^2/L = B:

b=\sqrt{\frac{BL}{\rho N}}=\sqrt{\frac{2000\times 0.02}{0.9\times 10^{6}}}\approx \$0.006666667 \;(\$6.666667\ \text{CPM}).

Expected wins are Nb/L \approx 333{,}333.33, giving 300,000 successful impressions, 150 attributed conversions, $4,500 of network collections, $2,000 of media spend and a $2,500 gross spread. The bid is higher than the \rho = 1 bid of 6.324555 CPM because the network pays for only 90% of its wins, so the same budget supports a higher price.

Case B's contract creates a media payment obligation when the auction is won, so the network pays even if display later fails. This is a contract term; it does not license treating the protocol's nurl as billing evidence. Then:

\pi_B(b)=(\rho v-b)F(b),\qquad C_B(b)=bF(b).

The unconstrained optimum drops to b = \rho v/2 = \$0.00675 ($6.75 CPM), with expected spend N(\rho v/2)^2/L = \$2{,}278.125 > \$2{,}000, so the budget binds again. Cost is not scaled by \rho, so the budget-constrained bid is again \sqrt{BL/N} \approx \$0.006324555 (6.324555 CPM). Expected wins are about 316,227.77, successful impressions about 284,604.99, attributed conversions about 142.302495, network collections about $4,269.07 and gross spread about $2,269.07.

The $230.93 shortfall against Case A is entirely lost collections: Case B buys 15,395.01 fewer successful impressions at $0.015 each, with the same $2,000 of spend. The cause is that (1-\rho)B = \$200 of Case B's budget pays for wins that never display. The shortfall is not $200 because the two cases also bid differently. Successful impressions are \sqrt{\rho NB/L} in Case A and \rho\sqrt{NB/L} in Case B, a ratio of \sqrt{\rho} \approx 0.948683, so

\begin{aligned}N(\pi_A - \pi_B) &= v\sqrt{NB/L}\,\bigl(\sqrt{\rho}-\rho\bigr)\\ &= 0.015 \times 316{,}227.77 \times 0.048683\\ &\approx \$230.93.\end{aligned}

The contract therefore changes the bid and the returns, not just the event names in reports. If the probability of a successful impression varies with Z, the bid or the quality of the opportunity, you cannot pull a constant \rho out of the expectation; you must model the conditional display probability and the payment function explicitly. On the systems side, the reservation must cover the maximum payment obligation under the contract. In both cases that is b per bid: a hard-quota reservation cannot be cut by 10% just because the expected display probability is 90%.

Division of labor between expected-budget control and hard budgets

This section switches from expected cost per request to actual ledger quantities, all in dollars for one accounting period. Let C be confirmed cumulative media spend (not the per-request expectation C_A or C_B above), R the total of maximum possible payment obligations not yet discharged (open reservations), and B the hard media budget. R here is the reservation total, not the revenue function R(C) from Chapter 2. Let c_i be the cost cap of bid i (the most it can ever be charged, fees included) and p_i its actual billed amount. A hard budget means maintaining the invariant below on every path:

\begin{aligned} &\text{invariant:} && C+R\le B\\ &\text{admit bid } i \text{ only if } C+R+c_i\le B: && R \leftarrow R+c_i\\ &\text{bill bid } i \text{ at } p_i\le c_i: && C \leftarrow C+p_i,\; R \leftarrow R-c_i\\ &\text{authoritative loss or cancellation of } i: && R \leftarrow R-c_i \end{aligned}

Billing changes C+R by p_i - c_i \le 0 and a release lowers it, so only admission can raise it, and admission checks the bound. Since R \ge 0, C \le B follows. This holds under three conditions. Every bid has a reliable cap c_i. Each admission is an atomic check-and-reserve: either a serializable update of a single counter, or escrow, where B is split into per-node quotas whose sum is provably at most B, each node enforces its own quota, and moving quota between nodes is atomic (or the donor debits before the recipient credits). And each billing or release step applies its two updates atomically, exactly once per bid.

Worked example

One bid through the ledger at the last bid's worth of budget

Given: the Chapter 3 setting (\rho = 1, bid 6.324555 CPM); burl is the contractual billing signal, so a win alone books nothing, and lurl is authoritative for losses. Pitfall: releasing on a timer

Suppose event 5 had released j on its local timeout. Then R = 0, bid k is admitted (R = 6,325), and j's late burl posts 6,325 µ$ with no reservation behind it: C = 2,000,000,000 and C+R = 2,000,006,325 µ$ > B. If k also bills, confirmed spend overshoots the hard budget by 6,325 µ$. The amount is small here only because the timeout released one bid; a regional notice outage releases thousands.

The definitions of c_i and R must cover fees, spend not yet reported, bids whose local timeout expired but that the exchange may still have accepted, and late billing. Reserving expected cost (win probability × bid) instead of c_i frees capital, but then you have statistical overspend control, not a proof of zero overspend on every path. The overshoot tail depends on correlation between outcomes, and correlated outcomes are exactly what incidents produce. The expiry of a multi-region lease likewise does not discharge obligations already incurred with an external party.

Reserve the cap or reserve the expected cost?

Given: remaining headroom H = $10 (illustrative, near the end of the period); bid b = $0.006324555 per impression, win probability F(b) = b/L = 0.31622775; ρ = 1; every admitted bid stays in flight until the headroom is used up, then all resolve.

Keep safety separate from capital availability. Never releasing an undischarged reservation keeps the budget safe but locks up quota. A safe release needs one of: an authoritative final loss (for example a lurl call, where the contract makes it authoritative), a cancellation, a reconciliation result, or a binding contractual cutoff that extinguishes any remaining payment obligation. Such evidence can arrive early, so release need not wait for a deadline. An empirical upper bound on callback delay, or a local TTL, does not terminate a payment obligation. If no confirmable discharge mechanism exists, the system can either hold the reservation indefinitely or accept statistical overspend within an explicit risk limit; the latter is no longer a per-path hard-budget guarantee.

Prompt discharge makes full-cap reservation cheap. By Little's law, open reservations equal bid rate r times mean time to discharge, and losers and winners discharge on different clocks: r\,[(1-F)\,T_{\text{loss}} + F\,T_{\text{bill}}], where a loser waits for its loss notice and a winner for its billing notice. If the 1,000,000 requests arrive evenly over a day (r = 11.57 bids per second), with F = 0.316228, a mean loss-notice delay of 2 s and a mean billing delay of 60 s (both illustrative), about 235 reservations are open at once, locking about $1.49 of the $2,000 budget. At a 1-hour mean billing delay it is about 13,192 reservations and $83.44.

Without any release, the same rule starves delivery. On the exact nano-dollar scale each bid holds 6,324,555 n$, so the $2,000 budget admits \lfloor 2\times10^{12}/6{,}324{,}555 \rfloor = 316{,}227 bids before any reservation is released (316,205 at the rounded-up 6,325 µ$), about a third of the 1,000,000 requests. At the same bid and a 31.6% win rate, those yield about 100,000 wins, $632.45 of spend and an $867.54 gross spread, against $2,743.42 when losses are released promptly (Chapter 3, \rho = 1). In general, with no release, B/b bids win B/L impressions in expectation whatever the bid, so gross spread is (B/L)(v-b) for b \ge B/N. The best response is to bid lower and stretch the budget over all N requests: b = B/N = \$0.002 (2 CPM) gives N(b/L)(v-b) = \$1{,}300, still less than half of $2,743.42.

So release clearly lost bids promptly, shorten the time to confirm obligations, and allocate quota to regions or shards according to supply quality. Conservative reservation lowers overspend risk but causes under-delivery (spending less than planned). Measure that cost as lost gross spread, not as lock contention or budget-service QPS (queries per second).

A hard cap on the advertiser's CPA bill is a separate problem. Post-click conversions arrive with a delay and still create receivables, so shutting off new media buying cannot stop impressions already delivered from producing further billable events. Handle this with contract terms, conservative control of the remaining quota and explicit tracking of in-flight conversion obligations. Do not pass off the media ledger as the advertiser ledger.

Correctness boundaries for data and model releases

Decision logs must link at least: the bid opportunity's identity; candidate eligibility and the final action; the timestamp or version of the features that were available; predicted probabilities and valuations; bid and budget policy; experiment assignment; win, loss and rejection reasons; impression, click and conversion times; attribution rules; billing status; and later adjustments. Not every field can be kept forever, and access and retention policies must match data permissions.

Training samples must be reproducible at a specific version. Track the model, the calibrator (the post-hoc map from raw model score to probability), feature definitions, creatives and budget-control parameters together. Train only on matured labels, i.e. examples whose attribution window has closed, so that a missing conversion really is a negative. If one release changes both the model and the attribution window, you cannot tell why online results moved, even if they improved. Rolling back the model without rolling back features or the calibrator may not restore the old behavior either.

A shadow deployment (the new model scores live traffic, but its outputs drive no decisions) can expose latency and distribution anomalies. Because it does not affect which opportunities are bought, it cannot show that the model makes money online. After a canary rollout (the new model controls a small share of live traffic), check whether the traffic mix and price distribution changed, and evaluate under the same budget constraints with matured labels. A new policy buying different opportunities is part of its effect. You cannot take credit for its traffic selection while analyzing the two arms as if they had bought the same samples.

A dashboard that drives action

Good monitoring ties each failure to an economic consequence. On-time response rate and no-bid rate broken down by reason explain lost opportunities. The gap between wins and billable impressions explains delivery and integration risk. The amount and age of open obligations explain budget lock-up. Duplicate billing, settlement discrepancies and the overspend tail measure financial correctness. Per-segment calibration, matured CPA and ROAS (attributed revenue divided by ad spend; Chapter 2) and incremental contribution (Chapter 4) measure policy quality.

Duplicate billing, budget violations and changes to value definitions should each be able to trigger a stop-loss on its own.
