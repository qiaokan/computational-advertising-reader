An erratum in the RTB monograph’s second-price example

The monograph “Display Advertising with Real-Time Bidding (RTB) and Behavioural Targeting” by Wang, Zhang and Yuan (arXiv:1610.03013v2, 122 pages; arXiv date July 15, 2017, title page July 18) is a sound end-to-end account of RTB. One worked example in its Section 5.3.4 gets the budget equation wrong: it counts the win probability twice. This section re-derives the example, locates the slip and sizes the error. Equation and page numbers below refer to v2 and were checked against the PDF. arXiv metadata

Symbols

Notation follows Prediction and bidding. The monograph’s symbol, where different, is in brackets.

  • Z: the competitive threshold, the highest competing bid on one bid request. We win if our bid is at least Z [market price z].
  • L>0: the upper bound of Z, with Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) [l].
  • b\ge 0: our bid on one request. Z, L and b are dollars per impression (×1,000 for CPM).
  • F(b)=P(Z\le b): the win rate at bid b; f=F' [w(b), p_z].
  • r\in[0,1]: the request’s predicted click-through rate (pCTR), the probability that a won impression is clicked. Here r is a probability, not money. (In the SAM paper cited in Prediction and bidding, r is the reward per conversion; that chapter renames it a.)
  • b(r): the bidding function, mapping pCTR to a bid.
  • N: bid requests over the campaign’s life [T]. B: total media budget in dollars. B/N is the budget per request.
  • C(b)=E[Z\,\mathbf{1}\{Z\le b\}]: expected payment per request, counting zero on a loss, as in Prediction and bidding. \mathbf{1}\{\cdot\} is the indicator; E is over Z. The monograph’s cost function c(b) (Eq. 5.5) is the expected payment given a win, c(b)=E[Z\mid Z\le b], so C(b)=c(b)\,F(b).
  • \lambda>0: the Lagrange multiplier on the budget. The objective counts clicks, so \lambda is clicks per dollar and 1/\lambda is dollars per click.

Model and correct solution

The monograph’s special case: an ideal second-price auction (the winner pays Z; no floor, fees or bid increments); every win is one billable impression; r\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1), independent of Z; a risk-neutral buyer that maximizes expected clicks subject to expected spend =B. Nothing assumes the bid is linear in r; linearity falls out of the first-order condition.

Derivation

Budget-constrained click maximization under second price

Given: Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) in dollars per impression; pCTR r\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1), independent of Z; N requests; budget B dollars.

  1. Win rate and costs for 0\le b\le L. The per-request cost integrates the payment over winning thresholds only; it already contains the win probability.
    \begin{aligned} F(b)&=\frac{b}{L},\\ c(b)&=E[Z\mid Z\le b]=\frac{b}{2},\\ C(b)&=\int_0^b z\,\frac{dz}{L}=\frac{b^2}{2L}\\ &=c(b)\,F(b). \end{aligned}
    For b>L the bid always wins and C(b)=E[Z]=L/2, so bidding above L is equivalent to bidding L.
  2. The program (the monograph’s Eq. 5.11 with u(r)=r):
    \begin{aligned} &\max_{b(\cdot)}\; N\int_0^1 r\,F\bigl(b(r)\bigr)\,dr\\ &\text{s.t.}\quad N\int_0^1 C\bigl(b(r)\bigr)\,dr=B. \end{aligned}
  3. The Lagrangian separates across r. Maximize rF(b)-\lambda C(b) pointwise. Since C'(b)=b\,f(b), the derivative is (r-\lambda b)\,f(b): positive below r/\lambda, negative above. So
    b(r)=\min\!\left(\frac{r}{\lambda},\,L\right).
    This is the monograph’s Eq. 5.32 and the second-price rule b=v/\lambda of Prediction and bidding, with value v=r measured in clicks.
  4. The budget binds. With r/\lambda\le L for all r, expected clicks are N\int_0^1 r\cdot\frac{r}{\lambda L}\,dr=\frac{N}{3\lambda L}, decreasing in \lambda; so the optimum spends the whole budget and the equality constraint is right.
  5. Solve the budget equation, writing c^*=1/\lambda^* for the bid coefficient:
    \begin{aligned} N\int_0^1 \frac{(r c^*)^2}{2L}\,dr&=\frac{N (c^*)^2}{6L}=B\\ \Longrightarrow\quad c^*=\frac{1}{\lambda^*}&=\sqrt{\frac{6LB}{N}}. \end{aligned}
  6. Validity. The step-5 integral assumed no bid exceeds L, i.e. c^*\le L, which is 6LB/N\le L^2, i.e. B/N\le L/6. For L/6<B/N<L/2 the bid \min(rc,L) costs L/2-L^2/(3c) per request, so c=L^2/\bigl(3(L/2-B/N)\bigr); it equals L at B/N=L/6, so the two regimes join continuously. For B/N\ge L/2 the budget never binds: bidding L everywhere costs E[Z]=L/2 per request.

Result: b^*(r)=r\sqrt{6LB/N} for B/N\le L/6 (dollars per impression; the coefficient is dollars per click).

Check: (i) Units: L and B/N are both dollars, so \sqrt{6LB/N} is dollars, per click because r is clicks per impression. (ii) Marginal cost: spend S=N c^2/(6L) and clicks K=N c/(3L) give dS/dK=c, so at the optimum the last click costs exactly 1/\lambda^*, as a shadow price should. (iii) Numerical double integration of E[Z\,\mathbf{1}\{Z\le rc^*\}] over (r,Z), a Monte Carlo run, and a grid search over linear coefficients all reproduce the closed form and the optimum.

Where the monograph goes wrong

The monograph’s budget constraint (Eqs. 5.11 and 5.33, PDF pp. 56 and 58) is N\int c(b(r))\,F(b(r))\,dr=B. That is correct: Eq. 5.5 (p. 54) defines c as the conditional payment, so cF=C. But Eq. 5.35 (p. 58) evaluates c(b) as \int_0^b z/L\,dz=b^2/(2L), dropping Eq. 5.5’s denominator F(b). That is C(b), not c(b). Eq. 5.36 (p. 59) then multiplies by F(b)=b/L again, making the per-request cost b^3/(2L^2). With b=r/\lambda:

\begin{aligned} \int_0^1\frac{(r/\lambda)^3}{2L^2}\,dr&=\frac{1}{8\lambda^3L^2}=\frac{B}{N}\\ \Longrightarrow\quad \lambda_{\mathrm{paper}}&=\frac12\left(\frac{N}{BL^2}\right)^{1/3},\\ c_{\mathrm{paper}}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{paper}}}&=2\left(\frac{L^2B}{N}\right)^{1/3}. \end{aligned}

These are Eqs. 5.37 and 5.38. The linear form b=r/\lambda survives; only the coefficient is wrong. The first-price result in the same section, b(r)=r\sqrt{3LB/N} (Eq. 5.29, p. 58), uses the per-request cost b\,F(b)=b^2/L correctly, so the slip is confined to the second-price case. Pitfall: conditional vs per-request cost

“Expected cost” has two meanings: given a win, E[Z\mid Z\le b], and per request, E[Z\,\mathbf{1}\{Z\le b\}]. They differ by a factor F(b). A budget constraint needs the per-request one: the win rate enters exactly once. Units will not catch a second factor of F, which is dimensionless; scaling will. Under a uniform threshold, per-request second-price spend must grow as b^2; a formula that grows as b^3 has counted the win rate twice.

Size of the error

Bidding with c_{\mathrm{paper}}, the true expected spend per request is c_{\mathrm{paper}}^2/(6L) as long as c_{\mathrm{paper}}\le L. Divided by the budget per request:

\begin{aligned} \frac{c_{\mathrm{paper}}^2/(6L)}{B/N}&=\frac{4\,(L^2B/N)^{2/3}}{6L\,B/N}\\ &=\frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{LN}{B}\right)^{1/3}. \end{aligned}

The published coefficient keeps every bid at or below L exactly when 8L^2B/N\le L^3, i.e. B/N\le L/8. The factor decreases in B/N, so on that range it is at least \tfrac23\cdot 8^{1/3}=\tfrac43. On L/8<B/N\le L/6 some published bids exceed L; true spend is then L/2-L^2/(3c_{\mathrm{paper}}) per request, and the factor keeps falling to 3-6^{1/3}\approx1.183 at B/N=L/6. Wherever the correct closed form applies, the published one overspends by at least 18%, and by more as the budget tightens. This is not a boundary effect.

Worked example

The erratum at the running example’s numbers

Given: threshold bound L=\$0.02 per impression ($20 CPM); N=1{,}000{,}000 requests; media budget B=\$2{,}000; pCTR r\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1) as in the monograph’s special case. Values are carried unrounded and rounded only for display.

  1. Budget per request and validity: B/N=\$0.002. This is below L/6=\$0.00333, so the correct closed form applies, and below L/8=\$0.0025, so no published bid exceeds L either.
  2. Correct coefficient: \begin{aligned} c^*&=\sqrt{6LB/N}=\sqrt{6\times0.02\times0.002}\\ &=\sqrt{0.00024}=\$0.015492 \end{aligned} per click. A request with pCTR r gets a bid of r\times\$15.49 CPM.
  3. Published coefficient: \begin{aligned} c_{\mathrm{paper}}&=2(L^2B/N)^{1/3}\\ &=2\,(0.0004\times0.002)^{1/3}\\ &=2\times(8\times10^{-7})^{1/3}\\ &=2\times0.0092832=\$0.018566 \end{aligned} per click: a bid of r\times\$18.57 CPM, 19.8% higher.
  4. True expected spend at the published coefficient: \begin{aligned} N\,\frac{c_{\mathrm{paper}}^2}{6L}&=10^6\times\frac{4\times(8\times10^{-7})^{2/3}}{0.12}\\ &=10^6\times\frac{3.44710\times10^{-4}}{0.12}\\ &=\$2{,}872.58. \end{aligned}
  5. Clicks. Expected clicks are Nc/(3L): 258,199 at c^* and 309,439 at c_{\mathrm{paper}}. The extra 51,240 clicks cost $872.58, or $0.0170 each, above the optimum’s marginal cost of 1/\lambda^*=\$0.0155 per click.
  6. With a hard cap at $2,000 and requests arriving evenly, the published bidder runs out after 2000/2872.58=69.6\% of the flight, so it collects 69.6% of 309,439 clicks: exactly 2B/c_{\mathrm{paper}}=215{,}443, against the optimum’s 2B/c^*=258{,}199: 16.6% fewer clicks for the same money.

Result: the published coefficient spends $2,872.58 in expectation against a $2,000 budget (1.436×); under a hard cap it delivers 16.6% fewer clicks than the correct one.

Check: the overspend formula gives \tfrac23(LN/B)^{1/3}=\tfrac23\times10^{1/3}=\tfrac23\times2.15443=1.436290, and 1.436290\times\$2{,}000=\$2{,}872.58, matching step 4. The click shortfall is 1-c^*/c_{\mathrm{paper}}=1-0.015492/0.018566=16.6\%. Unitless case L=1, B/N=0.01 (below L/8): coefficients 0.244949 and 0.430887, overspend factor \tfrac23\times100^{1/3}=3.094.

The main text derives its budget results independently and does not reuse this example.

Where to read next

For the full RTB picture, read the RTB monograph’s Chapters 2 (how RTB works), 3 (auctions and the bid landscape), 5 (bidding strategies) and 7 (attribution). For how production click models define and calibrate probabilities, read Google’s 2013 click-prediction paper. To build an integration, work from OpenRTB and your counterparties’ contracts. To judge whether advertising creates value for the advertiser, read the randomized experiments. In every paper, write down the explicit assumptions; they matter more than the model names.

Glossary

This is the reader’s canonical glossary, in alphabetical order. Definitions give the sense used in this reader; individual contracts and platforms may define a term differently, and the contract wins.

Term Definition
A/B testA randomized experiment that assigns users (or requests) to treatment or control so that the difference in outcomes estimates a causal effect.
Ad exchangeA marketplace that receives bid requests from sellers, runs the auction among buyers and sets the clearing price.
Ad groupThe level below a campaign that carries finer targeting, bids or creative combinations; the hierarchy differs across platforms.
Ad loadHow many ads are shown per session or per unit of content; raising it trades user retention for short-term revenue.
Ad networkAn intermediary that aggregates publishers’ inventory and sells it to advertisers, often buying in one pricing unit (e.g. CPM) and selling in another (e.g. CPA) and keeping the spread.
Ad opportunityOne chance to show one ad to one user in one slot at one moment; also called an impression opportunity.
Ad requestAn app’s or page’s request for an ad to fill one slot; mediation or an SSP may fan one ad request out into many bid requests.
Ad serverThe system that selects which ad to show for a request, delivers it and logs the result.
AdAttributionKitApple’s privacy-preserving attribution framework (iOS 17.4 and later), successor to SKAdNetwork; it reports conversions in delayed, crowd-anonymized postbacks without user-level identifiers.
ads.txt / app-ads.txtA file the publisher hosts on its own domain listing the ad systems authorized to sell its inventory (app-ads.txt is the app variant).
AdvertiserThe buyer: the business that pays for impressions, clicks or actions in order to reach users.
ATTApp Tracking Transparency: Apple’s iOS permission prompt, required before an app tracks a user across other companies’ apps and websites or reads the IDFA.
AttributionRule-based assignment of credit for a conversion to earlier ad interactions (commonly last-touch); it is an accounting rule, not a causal estimate.
Attribution windowThe maximum delay between an ad interaction and a conversion for the conversion to be credited; click-through and view-through windows are set separately. Also called a lookback window.
AxonAppLovin’s ad prediction and bidding engine.
BidThe price a buyer offers for one opportunity; most interfaces quote it in CPM.
Bid requestThe message, usually in OpenRTB format, by which a seller invites buyers to bid on one ad opportunity before a response deadline.
Bid shadingBidding below one’s value in a first-price auction, trading win probability for a lower payment; at an interior optimum b=v-F(b)/f(b).
Billable eventThe event that triggers payment under a given contract: a win, a rendered impression, a click, an install or a qualifying action.
burlOpenRTB billing notice URL: the exchange calls it when the impression becomes billable under the exchange’s policy.
CalibrationThe property that predicted probabilities match observed frequencies (among requests with pCTR 2%, about 2% are clicked); bidding needs it because bids multiply value by probability.
CampaignAn advertiser’s unit of budget, objective, schedule and targeting.
Censoring (winning prices)Partial observation of the competitive threshold: on a win in a second-price auction it is observed (in first price, a win shows only that it was at most our bid); on a loss we know only that it exceeded our bid. Regression on wins alone is therefore biased.
Clearing priceThe price the auction winner actually pays.
Competitive thresholdThe highest competing bid for an opportunity (or the floor, if higher): the bid needed to win, written Z. The RTB monograph calls it the market price.
ContributionIn this reader, pre-advertising contribution per order: sales revenue minus cost of goods, fulfillment and other variable non-advertising costs.
ConversionThe advertiser’s target action after an ad, such as an install, purchase or registration.
CPACost per action: the price paid per agreed conversion; as a metric, spend divided by conversions.
CPCCost per click: the price paid per click; as a metric, spend divided by clicks.
CPICost per install: the price paid per attributed app install.
CPMCost per mille: the price per 1,000 impressions. A $6 CPM bid is $0.006 for one impression; auctions still sell one impression at a time.
CTR / pCTRClick-through rate: clicks divided by impressions. pCTR is a model’s predicted probability that one impression is clicked.
CVR / pCVRConversion rate; the denominator (per click, per impression or per install) must be stated. pCVR is the model’s predicted value.
Deal typesDirect deal (negotiated by hand, fixed price and volume); programmatic guaranteed (fixed price, reserved volume, automated execution); preferred deal (fixed price, first look, no volume commitment); private auction (invitation-only); open auction (any qualified buyer).
Delayed feedbackConversions arriving hours to days after the impression, so recent training examples carry incomplete labels: a negative may still convert.
Demand sourceAny buyer that can fill an ad request: an ad network, a DSP or a direct campaign.
DSPDemand-side platform: software that bids on impressions across exchanges on behalf of advertisers.
eCPMEffective CPM: revenue or value per 1,000 impressions, whatever the pricing unit (e.g. CPC × CTR × 1,000); it puts bids priced in different units on one scale.
Fill rateThe share of ad requests that receive an ad.
First-price auctionAn auction in which the winner pays its own bid.
Floor priceThe seller’s minimum acceptable price for an opportunity; bids below it lose. Also called a reserve price.
Ghost adsAn experiment design that logs, for control users, the impressions the campaign would have won, so the effect on users who would actually be exposed can be estimated without placebo ads.
Gross spreadA term defined in this reader: an ad network’s collections from advertisers minus its media procurement cost (M=A-C in Prediction and bidding); it is before the network’s other costs, so it is not profit.
Gross vs net revenueRevenue recognition: a platform that controls the service before it reaches the customer (a principal under IFRS 15 and ASC 606) books the full advertiser spend as revenue (gross); an agent books only its fee (net).
GSPGeneralized second-price auction for several ranked slots, as in search ads: each winner pays the minimum bid needed to keep its position, i.e. the next-ranked bid (quality-weighted where quality scores apply). Bidding one’s value is not a dominant strategy and in general not an equilibrium.
Guaranteed deliveryA contract that commits the seller to deliver a fixed volume of qualifying impressions, with a remedy for shortfall.
Hard capA spend limit that must hold on every realized path, not just in expectation; enforced by reserving each bid’s maximum possible cost before the bid is sent. Pacing and expected-budget constraints do not guarantee it.
HoldoutA randomly selected group withheld from ads (or from a campaign or feature) to serve as the control when measuring incrementality.
IABInteractive Advertising Bureau, the industry body whose Tech Lab publishes OpenRTB, ads.txt and sellers.json.
IDFAIdentifier for Advertisers: the per-device iOS advertising identifier, readable only after ATT consent.
ImpressionOne rendered display of an ad. A “successful impression” here means the display event the contract counts actually completed; a won auction can still fail to render.
IncrementalityThe change in an outcome caused by showing ads, measured against a randomized counterfactual; also called lift. Contrast with attribution.
InterstitialA full-screen ad shown at a natural break, such as between game levels.
IPSInverse propensity scoring: an off-policy estimator that reweights each logged reward by \pi(a\mid x)/\mu(a\mid x), the ratio of the new policy’s to the logging policy’s action probability.
iROASIncremental ROAS: revenue caused by the ads (treatment minus control) divided by ad spend.
ITTIntent-to-treat effect: the average effect of being assigned to treatment, whether or not the user was actually exposed.
LTVLifetime value: the value a user generates over a stated horizon. State whether it is revenue or contribution, the horizon, the discounting and whether acquisition cost is already subtracted.
lurlOpenRTB loss notice URL: the exchange calls it when the bid loses, optionally with a loss-reason code.
MAXAppLovin’s mediation platform for app publishers.
MediationSoftware in a publisher’s app that decides which of several demand sources fills each ad request, by a bidding auction, a waterfall or both.
MMPMobile measurement partner (e.g. Adjust, AppsFlyer): a third party whose SDK records installs and in-app events and attributes them to ad networks.
nurlOpenRTB win notice URL: the exchange calls it when the bid wins. On its own it does not mean the ad rendered or is billable.
OpenRTBThe IAB Tech Lab protocol for bid requests, bid responses and notices; this reader cites version 2.6.
PacingOnline control of the spend rate over the budget period, e.g. by adjusting a bid multiplier toward a target spend trajectory; it works alongside, and does not replace, a hard budget cap.
PostbackA server-to-server message that reports a conversion, and possibly its value, to the ad network or MMP.
ProgrammaticBuying and selling ad opportunities through automated systems, one opportunity at a time.
PSAPublic-service ad: a placebo ad shown to control users in older experiment designs; biased when an optimizer chooses who sees ads.
PublisherThe owner of the app or website whose ad slots are sold.
Retrieval and rankingThe stages of ad selection: retrieval picks a small candidate set worth scoring; ranking then estimates response probabilities and value for each candidate.
Rewarded videoA video ad the user opts into in exchange for an in-app reward.
ROASReturn on ad spend: attributed revenue divided by ad spend. It ignores cost of goods and does not measure incrementality.
RPMRevenue per 1,000 ad requests (also called request eCPM); if each filled request yields one impression, RPM = fill rate × impression eCPM.
RTBReal-time bidding: an auction run separately for each ad opportunity while the user’s page or app waits, within a deadline the exchange sets per request (OpenRTB tmax, commonly on the order of 100 ms).
SDKSoftware development kit: a library embedded in an app, e.g. by an ad network, mediation platform or MMP.
Second-price auctionAn auction in which the winner pays the highest competing bid, or the floor if higher. “Ideal second-price” in this reader ignores floors, bid increments and fees.
sellers.json / SupplyChain objectsellers.json is a file each SSP or exchange publishes listing the sellers it pays; the OpenRTB SupplyChain object lists every intermediary a bid request passed through.
SettlementThe final, contractually checked computation of who owes whom.
Shadow priceThe increase in the optimal objective per extra unit of a constrained resource, given by the constraint’s Lagrange multiplier; for a budget, extra value (or clicks) per extra budget dollar. Written \lambda or \mu in this reader.
SKAdNetworkApple’s earlier privacy-preserving install-attribution API (SKAN), succeeded by AdAttributionKit.
SSPSupply-side platform: software that sells a publisher’s inventory to many buyers and exchanges and manages floor prices.
Statistical arbitrageHere, buying impressions on CPM while selling outcomes on CPA: profitable in expectation, but exposed to conversion, attribution and payment risk.
Take rateThe amount the platform keeps after paying publishers, divided by the total advertiser spend that flows through it.
tCPATarget CPA: the average cost per action an advertiser asks the bidder to aim for; a bidding goal, not a billing price.
tmaxOpenRTB bid-request field: the maximum time, in milliseconds, the exchange allows for bids to arrive, including network latency.
TOT / CACETreatment effect on the treated / complier average causal effect: the effect on users actually exposed; with one-sided noncompliance and the exclusion restriction (assignment affects outcomes only through exposure), the ITT divided by the exposure rate in the treatment group.
tROASTarget ROAS: the return on ad spend an advertiser asks the bidder to aim for; a bidding goal, not a billing contract.
View-through conversionA conversion credited to an impression that was not clicked; a click-through conversion follows a click.
WaterfallA mediation method that calls demand sources one at a time in a configured priority order until one fills the request.
Win rateThe probability of winning at bid b, F(b)=P(Z\le b); as a metric, wins divided by bids.
