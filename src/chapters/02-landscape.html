The advertising landscape

In this chapter

  • Trace who pays whom in the three ways ads are bought: guaranteed deals, auctions inside a walled garden, and open programmatic auctions through DSPs, exchanges and SSPs (in apps, through mediation).
  • Say why search, social feeds, display, in-app, video and connected TV, and retail media price differently, and how brand buying differs from performance buying.
  • Place CPM, CPC, CPA and optimized bidding (oCPM, target CPA, Advantage+, Performance Max) on one ladder, and rank two ads by \text{eCPM}=1000\times\text{pCTR}\times\text{pCVR}\times\text{CPA bid}.
  • State what third-party cookies, Apple's ATT and Chrome's 2025 reversal on cookies changed about measurement.
  • Read the 2025 advertising revenue of Alphabet, Meta and Amazon and the US market by format, and know which parts of the field this reader does not cover.

The gist. Advertising sells users' attention. Advertisers pay; publishers own the screens; platforms in between choose which ad each user sees, mostly by running an auction for every ad slot. Most of the money goes to a few companies that own both the users and the auction, such as Google, Meta and Amazon. Formats differ in how much they reveal about what the user wants right now, and that sets their prices. Pricing has moved from paying per view toward bidding on results such as purchases, which forces the platform to predict those results for every impression. Privacy rules now limit how results can be observed. The rest of this reader goes deep on one slice: performance ads bought in auctions.

Advertisers pay for attention through three kinds of market

Money flows from advertisers to whoever controls users' screens, and the route it takes falls into one of three markets: a negotiated deal, an auction inside one company, or an auction across many companies. Five parties appear in every route:

  • The advertiser pays to reach users, from a global brand to a mobile game studio.
  • An agency plans and buys media for large advertisers for a fee; small advertisers buy directly in self-serve tools.
  • The publisher owns the content and the ad slots (its inventory): a news site, a mobile game, a streaming service.
  • A platform is the software, and usually the company, that decides which ad fills a slot and handles the money. Some platforms are their own publisher.
  • The user pays in attention, not cash; spend too much of it and users leave.
  1. Guaranteed (direct) deals. The advertiser or its agency agrees a fixed price and volume with a publisher in advance, for example one million impressions on a sports site's home page next month at a fixed CPM (price per 1,000 impressions). The publisher bears the risk of not delivering the volume. Software may execute the deal (programmatic guaranteed), but the price is negotiated, not auctioned.
  2. Auctions inside a walled garden. A walled garden is a company that owns the users, the ad slots, the auction and the measurement: Google (Search, YouTube), Meta (Facebook, Instagram), Amazon, and ByteDance (TikTok, and Douyin in China, where its ad platform is called Ocean Engine). The company auctions every impression, and outsiders cannot see user-level data. Most of the money is here (below).
  3. Open programmatic. For the rest of the internet, independent websites and apps, no single company owns both sides. Each impression passes through a chain of specialised intermediaries and is usually sold by real-time bidding (RTB): a separate auction for each impression, run within a fraction of a second while the page or app loads. Programmatic means any automated buying; RTB is its auction form.

The open-programmatic chain has its own vocabulary. A DSP (demand-side platform) is the buyer's software: it receives bid requests, predicts what each impression is worth to its advertisers, and bids within their budgets. An SSP (supply-side platform) is the seller's software: it offers each impression to many buyers, applies the publisher's floor prices and rules, and collects payment. An ad exchange is the marketplace that runs the auction between them; today SSP and exchange are usually one product. Between open auctions and guaranteed deals sit private marketplaces (PMPs): auctions open only to invited buyers, often with a floor price. Each hop takes a fee, so the publisher receives less than the advertiser spends.

Mobile apps add one more layer. An app usually embeds the software development kits (SDKs) of several ad networks, and mediation software in the app decides which network fills each ad slot. Under a waterfall, mediation calls networks one at a time in a fixed priority order set from their past average prices, and the first one that accepts fills the slot. Under in-app bidding, all networks bid on the same impression at once and the highest bid wins, so a network that values this particular user highly can win even if its average price is low; the industry has moved toward bidding. AppLovin's MAX, Unity's LevelPlay and Google's AdMob are the main mediation products; chapter 8 walks through one MAX auction.

The running campaign of chapter 1 sits in lane 3a: an ad network buys app impressions in auctions and bills the advertiser per purchase. Walled gardens run the same machinery internally, so most of what later chapters derive applies to them too.

Formats price differently because they reveal different amounts of intent

A format is worth more per impression when the context tells the platform what the user wants right now, and when the platform can see whether the ad led to a sale. A search query states a need; a feed only hints at one; a banner on a news article says little.

Search is the oldest auction market and the largest single revenue line. Advertisers bid on keywords; each query triggers an auction among the ads whose keywords match it. In the late 1990s GoTo.com (later Overture) ranked ads by bid and charged each winner its own bid per click. Google's AdWords auction, introduced in 2002, changed two things: it ranked ads by bid times predicted click-through rate, so an ad that users click more often can outrank a higher bid, and each winner paid only what it needed to keep its position. That payment rule is the generalized second-price (GSP) auction. Edelman, Ostrovsky and Schwarz (2007) showed that, unlike a true second-price auction, it does not make bidding one's true value optimal. Google's ranking score today, Ad Rank, combines the bid with a quality estimate built from expected click-through rate, ad relevance and landing-page experience; the advertiser-facing summary of that estimate is the Quality Score. Search remains Google's revenue base: in 2025 “Search & other” brought in $224.5 billion of Google's $294.7 billion advertising revenue, 76%.

Retail media sells ads on a store's own shelves, and its edge is the closed loop. On Amazon, a Sponsored Products ad is a product listing promoted in search results or on product pages, bought by keyword auction and billed per click. Because the ad, the click and the purchase all happen inside the retailer, it observes the sale directly, without cookies or a third-party measurement company. Its conversion labels are therefore complete and timely, which is hard to get elsewhere (chapters 5 and 6). The IAB's broader term is commerce media.

Connected TV (CTV) is streaming video watched on a television set (smart TV, streaming stick or console). It carries traditional TV budgets and commands high CPMs because the ad fills a large screen with sound on, but viewers rarely click, so tying a CTV ad to a later purchase needs statistical matching rather than a click trail. In mobile apps the comparable high-attention unit is rewarded video: the user opts in to watch a full ad for an in-game reward, and app-install campaigns buy it heavily.

Brand and performance buy different things

Brand advertising buys exposure to the right people; performance advertising buys measurable actions. A brand campaign is planned in reach (the number of distinct people who see the ad at least once) and frequency (the average number of times each of them sees it), often with a frequency cap such as three per week. It is bought per impression, often through guaranteed deals, and judged by awareness surveys or later sales studies. A performance campaign is judged by actions it can count, such as installs or purchases, and buys whatever impressions produce them at an acceptable cost, almost always by auction. This reader is about performance buying; brand planning and guaranteed-delivery allocation are among the gaps listed at the end of this chapter.

Pricing moved the bid toward results, while the auction still sells impressions

Over three decades the event advertisers bid on moved down the funnel (the sequence impression, click, conversion), but a slot is still sold one impression at a time, so the platform must convert every bid into a price per impression using predicted rates. The main pricing products, in roughly historical order:

  • CPM (cost per mille): pay per 1,000 impressions. The original banner price, still standard for brand, video and most open-web supply.
  • CPC (cost per click): pay per click. It made search advertising work, because a click on a search ad signals interest directly.
  • CPA (cost per action): pay per agreed action, such as an install (then often called CPI) or a purchase. Common with affiliate and mobile ad networks, and the contract of the running campaign; it needs conversions the platform can verify (chapter 3).
  • Optimized bidding: the advertiser states a target per action and the platform bids per impression on its behalf with its own models, while billing stays per impression or per click. TikTok and the Chinese platforms call these products oCPM and oCPC (optimized CPM or CPC, together oCPX). Google's Smart Bidding pursues a target CPA (average cost per conversion) or a target ROAS (return on ad spend: attributed revenue divided by spend). The newest step automates more than the bid. Google's Performance Max (2021) is one goal-based campaign that buys across all Google inventory: Search, YouTube, Display, Discover, Gmail and Maps. Meta's Advantage+ campaigns, renamed Advantage+ sales, app and leads campaigns in 2025, likewise let Meta's models choose audience, placement and creative. Several Chinese oCPX platforms also offer cost guarantees: if realized cost per conversion overshoots the target by more than a stated tolerance, the platform refunds part of the excess.

One idea organizes all of these. Every product fixes four points on the path from impression to business result: where the auction ranks, where the advertiser is billed, where the advertiser bids, and what the advertiser is judged on. The ranked point is almost always the impression. Under oCPM, for example, the advertiser is billed per impression, bids per purchase, and is judged on, say, revenue in the first seven days after install. The rates between the points (click-through rate, conversion rate) are unknown, and the product decides who estimates each one and who pays when the estimate is wrong. Chapter 3's four points works that out product by product; this chapter needs only the conversion from a bid to an auction score.

To rank bids made on different events, the platform restates each as an eCPM (effective CPM): the expected payment per 1,000 impressions. With pCTR the platform's predicted click-through rate (clicks per impression) and pCVR its predicted conversion rate (conversions per click),

\begin{aligned}\text{eCPM}_{\text{CPC}}&=1000\times\text{pCTR}\times\text{CPC bid},\\ \text{eCPM}_{\text{CPA}}&=1000\times\text{pCTR}\times\text{pCVR}\times\text{CPA bid}.\end{aligned}

The running campaign's $30 bid per purchase, with pCTR = 0.01 and pCVR = 0.05, ranks at 1000\times0.01\times0.05\times\$30=\$15, the eCPM of chapter 1. Google's Ad Rank and Meta's “total value” add quality terms to this score, but the core is the same product of bid and predicted rates.

Worked example

The higher bid can lose

Given: one ad slot and two CPC advertisers. A bids $0.30 per click with pCTR = 3%; B bids $0.40 per click with pCTR = 2%. The slot goes to the higher eCPM; there is no floor price.

  1. A's eCPM: 1000\times0.03\times\$0.30=\$9.
  2. B's eCPM: 1000\times0.02\times\$0.40=\$8.
  3. A wins with the lower bid. Under GSP pricing A pays the lowest CPC that still matches B's eCPM: \frac{\$8}{1000\times0.03}=\$0.2\overline{6}\approx\$0.27\text{ per click}.

Result: A wins at an eCPM of $9 against $8, and pays about $0.27 per click, less than its $0.30 bid.

Check: at that price the platform expects 1000\times0.03\times\$0.2\overline{6}=\$8 per 1,000 impressions, exactly B's eCPM, as a second-price rule should give. Ranking by bid alone would hand the slot to B, whose clicks are worth $8 per 1,000 impressions to the platform against A's $9 at the same bids.

The formula makes the platform's prediction a price-setting variable. If A's pCTR were overestimated, A would win impressions it should not and the platform would collect less than it expected; which party absorbs that error depends on the product (chapter 3). Getting these rates right, and calibrated on the impressions actually won, is the subject of chapter 5.

Measurement decides what can be optimized, and privacy rules now limit it

A platform can only optimize toward events it can observe and link to an ad, and since 2020 browsers and Apple's iOS have removed much of the cross-company tracking that made that linking easy. Start with two kinds of measurement. Attribution credits a conversion to an earlier ad by a rule, such as “the last ad clicked within seven days”; it is bookkeeping and can credit sales that would have happened anyway. Incrementality asks how many conversions the ad caused, which needs a randomized experiment with a control group. Optimization and billing run on attribution; whether the advertiser should keep paying depends on incrementality. Chapter 6 develops this difference.

Linking an ad on one company's property to a purchase on another's relied on shared identifiers. A third-party cookie is a small identifier stored in the browser by a domain other than the site being visited, typically an ad-tech company embedded on many sites, so the same browser can be recognized across all of them. On phones the equivalent is the mobile advertising ID: Apple's IDFA and Google's advertising ID on Android, a per-device identifier that any app could read. Four steps reshaped this:

  1. 2020
    Safari blocks all third-party cookies by default (March 2020); Firefox already blocked known trackers' cookies by default. On those browsers, cross-site conversion measurement through third-party cookies stopped working.
  2. 2021
    Apple's App Tracking Transparency (ATT), from iOS 14.5, requires an app to ask the user's permission before tracking them across other companies' apps and websites or reading the IDFA; without permission the IDFA reads as all zeros. Install attribution on iOS moved to Apple's own privacy-preserving reports, SKAdNetwork and its successor AdAttributionKit, which send delayed, aggregated postbacks with no user identifier (chapter 6 has the timing).
  3. 2025
    Chrome plans, then abandons, removing third-party cookies. Google announced in 2020 that Chrome would phase them out and built replacement “Privacy Sandbox” interfaces for targeting and measurement. After several delays it dropped the removal in 2024, said in April 2025 that Chrome would keep its current approach of letting users choose, and on 17 October 2025 announced it would retire most Privacy Sandbox technologies, including Topics, Protected Audience and Attribution Reporting, on Chrome and Android, citing low adoption.
  4. 2026
    Status at the time of writing (November 2026): Chrome still allows third-party cookies unless the user blocks them, and the retired Privacy Sandbox interfaces are being removed. Safari and Firefox still block them. Consent laws such as the EU's GDPR apply regardless of browser defaults.

Platforms filled the gap with modelled conversions: where a conversion cannot be linked to an ad, the platform estimates the link from similar users it can observe and reports observed and modelled conversions together. Google, for example, reports both in its standard Conversions column. Two consequences follow. Training labels are partly the output of other models, and are missing more often for users who declined tracking, so they are not a random sample (chapter 5). And walled gardens, which see clicks and often purchases on their own properties, lost less signal than the open web, one reason money keeps concentrating in them.

The auction-and-prediction machinery in this reader is therefore not a niche: search, social feeds and retail media, where intent is visible or predictable from a company's own data, take most of the money. The open web and the app ecosystem compete for the rest against companies that see the whole loop from impression to purchase; that is where AppLovin (chapter 8) competes.
