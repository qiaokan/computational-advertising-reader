Prediction and the data it learns from

In this chapter

  • State exactly what the conversion model predicts, and why a bid needs that probability to be right in absolute terms: doubling every prediction leaves AUC unchanged and doubles every bid.
  • See why the ad that wins an auction is overpredicted even by an unbiased model: with ten equal ads and 20% prediction noise, the winner's prediction averages 31% too high.
  • Recognize three ways the training logs mislead: the delivery policy chooses which outcomes you see, negative downsampling inflates probabilities (a 4.76% output is really 0.05%, a 95× overvaluation), and late conversions make young data look worse (a 10.2% gap from label age alone).
  • Learn the competing-price distribution from auction feedback in which a loss reveals only that the price was above the bid.

The gist. A bid is a predicted probability times a price, so the prediction must be right in absolute terms, not merely in the right order. Two forces push it wrong. The auction picks the ad with the highest prediction, and the highest of several noisy guesses is usually too high. And the model learns from logs the system produced itself: it sees outcomes only for ads it chose to show, engineers discard most non-converting examples to save compute, and conversions arrive days late. Each distortion has a known correction, and each fails silently if skipped. The bidder also needs the distribution of competing prices, which auction logs reveal only partly: a loss says the price was higher than the bid, not by how much.

The model predicts the billable event per displayed impression

The conversion model's target is the probability that one successfully displayed impression produces the event the contract pays for. Let x be the information available when the bid is placed (user, context, ad slot and ad). Define three indicators, each 1 if the event happens and 0 otherwise: S, the impression is displayed successfully; K, the user clicks; Y, a qualifying conversion occurs within the observation window (the period after the ad in which a conversion counts, for example 7 days). The running campaign's CPA contract (Chapter 1) bills post-click attributed purchases, so a billable event needs K=1 and Y=1:

q(x)=P(K=1,Y=1\mid x,S=1)=P(K=1\mid x,S=1)\cdot P(Y=1\mid K=1,x,S=1).

This is the chain rule; it does not assume clicks and conversions are independent. CTR (click-through rate) is clicks per impression and CVR (conversion rate) is conversions per click; pCTR and pCVR are a model's predictions of them (Chapter 2). Always state the denominator: the second factor is per click, q(x) is per displayed impression. If a model already predicts conversions per impression, do not multiply by CTR again. If the contract also pays for view-through conversions (a conversion after an impression with no click), add the qualifying K=0 branch.

Before the auction we do not yet know whether we will win or whether the ad will render. With win probability F(b) at bid b (Chapter 4) and display success rate \rho=P(S=1\mid\text{win}), one bid request yields F(b)\,\rho\,q(x) expected billable conversions, assuming the response does not depend on the bid itself and failed displays never convert. A network paid a per billable conversion values a displayed impression at v(x)=q(x)\,a; with the running values q=0.0005 and a=\$30, v=\$0.015, i.e. $15 CPM. The advertiser's value is different: w(x)=\Delta q(x)\,g, the causal lift in purchase probability times contribution per order. A model of q predicts attributed response; it says nothing about \Delta q, which needs experiments (Chapter 6).

Bidding needs calibrated probabilities, not just good ranking

A model that ranks impressions correctly can still bid wrongly, because the bid uses the probability's value, not its rank. Take the running example. A model that outputs 0.0010 wherever the truth is 0.0005, doubling every prediction, orders impressions exactly as the true model does. A value bidder using it bids 0.0010\times\$30=\$0.030, $30 CPM instead of $15: twice what the impression is worth.

Two standard metrics separate these properties. Call a sample a positive if the event occurred and a negative otherwise. AUC (area under the ROC curve) is the probability that a random positive scores above a random negative, ties counting one half. It depends only on the order of the scores, so the doubled model has the same AUC. A model is calibrated if, among samples predicted at probability p, the event frequency is p once labels have matured (the observation window has closed and late conversions are in). Log loss, the average of -\bigl[y\ln p+(1-y)\ln(1-p)\bigr] over a fixed evaluation set, penalizes both bad ranking and bad calibration, so it does punish the doubled model. Check calibration on slices too (expensive publishers, new users, one country): good average calibration can hide large errors that cancel. Accept a model only when ranking, calibration, stability across slices and business outcomes all hold up. Google's click-prediction paper treats calibration as a system problem in this sense. McMahan et al. 2013

Retrieval, ranking and the auction each filter the candidates

Prediction runs as a funnel, and an ad dropped early can never win later. Candidate retrieval cheaply narrows the eligible ads to the few worth scoring; ranking estimates response and value for that small set; value estimates, budgets, eligibility rules and the auction then pick the winner. Improving ranking AUC does nothing for a high-value ad that retrieval never passed on. Meta's Andromeda is one published example of staged retrieval followed by ranking. Meta 2024 Andromeda

Features describe the advertiser and campaign (an advertiser's unit of budget, targeting and goal), the creative (the image, video or text shown), the slot, the user or context, and past interactions. Each feature must be available at decision time, permitted, and identical in meaning in training and serving. A new creative has no feedback history, so the model must lean on content and context features, and an exploration budget must buy it impressions; otherwise past winners keep collecting labels and new candidates never get the chance to prove themselves.

Optional: training a conversion model when only clicks reveal conversions (ESMM)

A pCVR model trained only on clicked impressions is applied to all impressions; the clicked ones are a selected subset. This is called sample selection bias. ESMM (Entire Space Multi-Task Model) trains on all impressions using the chain impression → click → conversion: it is supervised on click labels (pCTR) and on click-and-convert labels (pCTCVR = pCTR × pCVR, the chain rule above), so its pCVR tower is fitted over all impressions without dividing one estimate by another. It does not fix selection created by the serving policy, delayed labels or causal identification, which are the subjects of the rest of this chapter. Ma et al. 2018, ESMM

Auction winners are overpredicted even by an unbiased model

The ad that wins is the one whose prediction is highest, and the highest of several noisy predictions is biased upward even when each prediction is unbiased. The reason is one line: the maximum is at least as large as any single prediction, so its expectation is at least the expectation of any one of them, which is the true value; with noise, it is strictly larger.

Take 10 ads with the same true value V. Each prediction is V(1+0.2\,\varepsilon_i) with independent standard normal \varepsilon_i: unbiased, with 20% noise. The winner's prediction is V(1+0.2\,M), where M=\max_i\varepsilon_i. Since P(M\le m)=\Phi(m)^{10}, with \Phi the standard normal CDF and \varphi its density, M has density 10\,\varphi(m)\,\Phi(m)^9, and

\mathbb{E}[M]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} m\cdot 10\,\varphi(m)\,\Phi(m)^{9}\,dm\approx1.5388 .

The integral has no closed form; 1.5388 is computed numerically (a simulation of 10 normal draws, repeated, gives the same value to three decimals). The winner's predicted value therefore averages V(1+0.2\times1.5388)\approx1.31\,V, about 31% above its true value. If the winner pays in proportion to its prediction, its realized cost per conversion runs about 31% over target. Two remedies are standard: calibrate on won impressions rather than on all scored candidates, and shrink predictions toward a prior, more strongly where they are noisier (new ads, little data). This is a main reason platforms that sell cost-capped products see overruns; which party absorbs them depends on the contract (Chapter 3).

Equal true values are the worst case: when one ad is truly much better, it usually wins on merit and noise matters less.

The delivery policy decides which outcomes you see

Ad logs are not a passive sample of the world: every outcome in them was selected by the system's own past decisions. Eligibility rules (targeting, budget, frequency caps, content policy) decide which ads may compete; retrieval decides which get scored; bidding decides which opportunities are bought; rendering and privacy rules decide which outcomes are observed. A CVR model fitted on won impressions estimates P(Y=1 \mid x, \text{shown by the old policy}), not the rate on traffic the old policy never bought. No model can recover labels for actions that were never taken.

A new policy can be evaluated from old logs only if the old policy sometimes took the new policy's actions at random and logged how likely each action was. Then each logged outcome is weighted by the ratio of the two policies' probabilities for the action taken, a method called inverse propensity scoring (IPS). For example, if the old policy showed ad B on one comparable request in ten and B's purchase rate there is 2%, then per logged request the weighted B outcome averages 0.1\times10\times0.02=0.02, exactly B's purchase rate: the weight of 10 undoes the 1-in-10 selection. The price is variance. A choice logged with probability 0.05 gets weight 20, so a handful of rare outcomes can dominate the estimate. This is why the exploration that creates the randomness costs real money, and why it must be logged exactly.

Optional: why IPS is unbiased, what its variance is, and a six-row stress test

Notation (local to this block). x_i is the context of logged opportunity i; a_i the action taken (which ad, what bid; not the CPA a); r_i the observed reward; \mu(a\mid x) the logging policy's probability of action a (not a budget multiplier); \pi(a\mid x) the policy to evaluate; \bar r(x,a)=\mathbb{E}[r\mid x,a]. The target is V(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_x\bigl[\sum_a\pi(a\mid x)\,\bar r(x,a)\bigr] and the estimator is

\hat V_{\text{IPS}}(\pi)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\pi(a_i\mid x_i)}{\mu(a_i\mid x_i)}\,r_i .

Assumptions. (1) Support: \mu(a\mid x)>0 wherever \pi(a\mid x)>0. (2) The logged \mu(a_i\mid x_i) is the probability actually used, not a reconstruction. (3) The reward is defined and observed the same way under both policies (same conversion event, window and postback). (4) The new policy does not change the contexts or the environment; in an auction this fails when its bids move clearing prices or competitors' behavior. The (x_i,a_i,r_i) are independent draws from the logging process.

Unbiasedness. Fix a context x. The logging policy picks action a with probability \mu(a\mid x), so one term's conditional expectation is

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\tfrac{\pi(a\mid x)}{\mu(a\mid x)}\,r\Bigm| x\Bigr]=\sum_{a:\,\mu(a\mid x)>0}\mu(a\mid x)\,\frac{\pi(a\mid x)}{\mu(a\mid x)}\,\bar r(x,a)=\sum_{a}\pi(a\mid x)\,\bar r(x,a).

The logging probability cancels. The last step uses support: every action with \pi>0 is in the first sum. Averaging over x gives V(\pi); each of the n terms has that expectation, so their mean does too.

Variance. For a deterministic \pi choosing \pi(x), a term is r/\mu(\pi(x)\mid x) with probability \mu(\pi(x)\mid x) and 0 otherwise, so its second moment given x is \mu\cdot\mathbb{E}[r^2\mid x,\pi(x)]/\mu^2=\mathbb{E}[r^2\mid x,\pi(x)]/\mu: it grows like 1/\mu. Clipping the weight at some M, i.e. using \min\{M,\pi/\mu\}, cuts variance at the cost of a downward bias when r\ge 0. Offline replay reweights outcomes you logged; it cannot manufacture outcomes you never logged. Swaminathan and Joachims, counterfactual risk minimization

  2. Estimate: the weighted rewards sum to 0+0+1.25+0+0+20=21.25, so \hat V_{\text{IPS}}=\frac{21.25}{6}=3.54.

Negative downsampling inflates predicted probabilities

Training pipelines usually keep every positive but only a fraction of negatives, and a model trained on that sample predicts the sampled rate, which is far above the true rate until corrected. Let p be the true event probability for a fixed feature value, s (0<s\le 1) the probability that a negative is kept, independently of everything else, and p_s the event rate among kept samples, which is what an unweighted model learns.

Worked example

Correcting a downsampled conversion model before bidding

Given: 1,000,000 displayed impressions at the running example's true rate q=0.0005; all positives kept; negatives kept with s=0.01; no training weights; CPA a=\$30.

  1. Counts kept: 1{,}000{,}000\times0.0005=500 conversions, all kept; 999{,}500\times0.01=9{,}995 of the non-conversions.
  2. Sampled rate: p_s=500/(500+9{,}995)=500/10{,}495=0.0476. The model trained on this sample outputs 4.76%.
  3. General form: per original sample, the kept positive mass is p and the kept negative mass is s(1-p), so p_s=p/\bigl(p+s(1-p)\bigr). Multiply out, p_s\,p+p_s\,s-p_s\,s\,p=p, collect the p terms, p\,(1-p_s+s\,p_s)=s\,p_s, and divide:
    p=\frac{s\,p_s}{1-p_s+s\,p_s},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \frac{p}{1-p}=s\cdot\frac{p_s}{1-p_s}.
  4. Correct the output: p=\dfrac{0.01\times0.0476}{1-0.0476+0.01\times0.0476}=0.0005 (using the unrounded p_s=500/10{,}495, it is exactly 0.0005).
  5. Value: corrected, 0.0005\times\$30=\$0.015 per impression ($15 CPM). Uncorrected, 0.0476\times\$30=\$1.43 per impression ($1,428 CPM).

Result: the true probability is 0.05%; skipping the correction overvalues the impression 0.0476/0.0005\approx95\times.

Check: from the general form, p_s/p=1/\bigl(p+s(1-p)\bigr)=1/(0.0005+0.01\times0.9995)=1/0.010495=95.3, close to 1/s=100 because p is small next to s.

In logit terms the correction is a constant shift by \ln s. Apply it exactly once. If training instead weighted each kept negative by 1/s, the model already targets the original population and needs no correction; check calibration on representative held-out data to know which case you are in. If the retention rate depends on features, use each sample's own retention probability; a single constant no longer works.

Delayed labels make young data look worse

A conversion can arrive days after the impression, so an impression that has not converted yet is not a negative, and data read too early understates conversion rates. Waiting for every label to mature costs the model freshness, so a pipeline must choose an observation window, a maturity rule (train or compare only on cohorts whose labels are, say, 95% final), a model of the delay, and a backfill policy for labels that arrive late. Delayed feedback and elapsed-time sampling

Assume, for illustration, that the delay from impression to conversion is exponential with mean 3 days. The share of eventual conversions visible at label age t days is then H(t)=1-e^{-t/3} (H is the delay CDF, not the competing-bid CDF F). Labels are 95% final only when e^{-t/3}=0.05, at t=3\ln 20\approx 9.0 days. Two experiment arms with the same true CVR of 1.0%, read when their impressions are 7 and 5 days old, show 0.903% and 0.811%: the younger arm looks 10.2% worse with no real difference. Nor is a young unconverted impression a negative. If its eventual conversion probability is p, then "converts eventually and not yet by age t" has probability p\,(1-H(t)), and "not yet converted" has probability 1-p\,H(t). Dividing (Bayes' rule),

P(\text{converts eventually}\mid\text{not yet at age }t)=\frac{p\,(1-H(t))}{1-p\,H(t)} .

For p=1\% at t=1 day, H(1)=0.283 and the probability is 0.72%, still 72% of the base rate.

Pitfall: time leakage

Each training example may contain only information available at decision time. Account state after the purchase, counters already incremented by this purchase and attribution outcomes decided later must not leak into features. A point-in-time join needs event time, processing time and feature-pipeline version tracked separately. A model that wins offline through leakage does not degrade slightly online; it loses the very inputs its predictions depended on.

Learning competing prices from censored auction feedback

The bidder in Chapter 4 assumed the distribution of the competitive threshold Z (the highest competing bid) was known; in practice it must be learned from our own auction records, and those records show prices only partly. A price is censored when a record reveals only an interval for it. In an ideal second-price auction (no reserve price) a win reveals Z exactly, because we pay it; a loss reveals only Z>b. Treating the win prices as an ordinary regression sample therefore learns a selected distribution: at a fixed bid, every threshold above the bid is missing.

The running example shows how badly. With Z uniform between 0 and L=\$0.02 per impression ($20 CPM) and a constant bid of $6 CPM, we win 30% of requests (Chapter 4). The win prices are uniform on $0 to $6, averaging $3 CPM, while the true average threshold is $10 CPM. The losses, which a price regression throws away, carry the missing information.

The fix is a likelihood (the probability or density the model assigns to the observed records) in which each record contributes the probability of exactly what it reveals. Let F(z\mid x) and f(z\mid x) be the conditional CDF and density of Z. A second-price win reveals the value Z, so it contributes the density f(Z\mid x); a loss reveals Z>b, so it contributes P(Z>b\mid x)=1-F(b\mid x). For independent requests the probabilities multiply, so the log-likelihood \ell is a sum over won and lost requests, each with its own Z, b and x:

\ell_{\text{second price}}= \sum_{\mathrm{win}}\ln f(Z\mid x) +\sum_{\mathrm{loss}}\ln\bigl(1-F(b\mid x)\bigr).

In a first-price auction our payment is our own bid, so a win reveals only Z\le b. Every record is then censored, and

\ell_{\text{first price}}= \sum_{\mathrm{win}}\ln F(b\mid x) +\sum_{\mathrm{loss}}\ln\bigl(1-F(b\mid x)\bigr).

This is the likelihood of a binary win/loss outcome. Without a parametric form for F it pins down F only at the bids actually placed, so learning the whole curve needs variation in bids. Never label your own winning first-price bid as the competitors' price. Some exchanges return extra signals, such as the minimum bid that would have won; check the actual interface.

For the uniform example, maximizing either likelihood gives \hat L=b/(\text{win rate})=\$6/0.30=\$20 CPM, the true value, using only the win and loss counts.

Optional: deriving the censored estimate for the uniform example

Assume Z\sim\text{Uniform}(0,L) with L>b, a constant bid b, and n_w wins and n_l losses among independent requests. Then f(z)=1/L and 1-F(b)=1-b/L, so the second-price log-likelihood is

\ell(L)=-n_w\ln L+n_l\ln\Bigl(1-\frac{b}{L}\Bigr),\qquad \frac{d\ell}{dL}=-\frac{n_w}{L}+\frac{n_l\,b}{L(L-b)} .

Multiplying the derivative by L(L-b)>0 gives -n_w(L-b)+n_l b, which is positive below L=b(n_w+n_l)/n_w and negative above it, so that is the unique maximum: \hat L=b/(\text{win rate}). With 3,000 wins in 10,000 requests at b=\$6 CPM, \hat L=6\times10{,}000/3{,}000=\$20 CPM. The win prices themselves drop out: given a win, Z is uniform on (0,b) whatever L is, so here the losses carry all the information about L. The first-price likelihood, n_w\ln(b/L)+n_l\ln(1-b/L), has the same derivative and the same maximizer.

When the likelihood is valid. Wins and losses must be decided purely by the price comparison, and, once the logged x includes everything the bidder used, the bid must carry no further information about the threshold: P(Z\le z\mid x,b)=F(z\mid x) (non-informative censoring). Timeouts, creative rejections, budget filtering and users leaving the page are not "the price exceeded the bid" and must not be coded as such losses. RTB monograph, Chapter 3
