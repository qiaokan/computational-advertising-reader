AppLovin and Axon

In this chapter

  • Say what Axon, AppLovin Ads and MAX each are, and trace one MAX auction from ad request to billing, including what the publisher, the winning bidder and AppLovin each earn.
  • Separate what every MAX bidder learns from what only the auctioneer sees, and both from what AppLovin says Axon is allowed to train on.
  • Explain how an advertiser's two settings, a ROAS goal and a daily budget, become one bid per impression, and read which of the two is binding.
  • Explain how Axon can predict a user's day-7 revenue before the install, from revenue postbacks and pre-install signals, and why that is harder than predicting clicks.
  • Explain how a prediction advantage turns into retained profit, and why a sharper model can raise profit with no change in win rate.
  • Name the evidence that would distinguish a real moat from metrics that merely look good, and read AppLovin's net revenue for what it can and cannot tell you.

The gist. AppLovin sits on both sides of one market. MAX runs the auction inside publishers' apps; AppLovin Ads, powered by the Axon model, is one of the bidders in that auction, and it earns nearly all of the company's revenue. Running the auction shows AppLovin every bid, but seeing data is not permission to train on it, and the company says Axon does not use rivals' bids. A better model makes money by moving spend toward impressions that convert, even when the number of wins stays fixed in the uniform-threshold example. So a real moat shows up in advertisers' incremental returns on comparable supply, not in win rates, publisher prices or revenue growth.

What the products are

AppLovin sells three things that are easy to blur: a prediction engine, the ad product it powers, and the auction where that product bids. Publishers here are app developers; ad networks reach their inventory through an SDK (a library compiled into the app). Mediation software in the app picks the demand source for each ad request, either by calling networks in a fixed order (a waterfall) or by real-time bids (in-app bidding). A DSP (demand-side platform) bids for advertisers. MAX is AppLovin's mediation product, which the company calls its in-app bidding solution. Axon is AppLovin's prediction and bidding engine (the filings say "recommendation engine"). The advertiser product it powers is Axon Ads Manager in the 2025 Form 10-K and AppLovin Ads in the Q3 2026 Form 10-Q; this chapter says AppLovin Ads.

Public fact. AppLovin describes Axon as the AI advertising engine that powers AppLovin Ads. AppLovin bids on each impression according to the value Axon assigns to it for a given advertiser, aiming at that advertiser's return-on-ad-spend (ROAS) goal. The 2025 10-K and the Q3 2026 10-Q both say this product generates substantially all of AppLovin's revenue; the 10-K adds that revenue from other services "was not material". Axon official description, AppLovin 2025 10-K, AppLovin Q3 2026 10-Q

Public fact. MAX's partner documentation describes a "unified first-price auction" in which the highest bidder wins, and its OpenRTB specification says bidders are expected "to pay the CPM amount they bid in full". DSPs bid through AppLovin Exchange over OpenRTB 2.5; ad networks bid through tokens from their own SDKs. Non-bidding networks enter at a CPM set by the publisher or by auto-CPM from their recent reported CPMs. The documented sequence for one ad request: MAX auction flow, AppLovin OpenRTB specification, bid responses and notices, auto-CPM

  1. Request
    Ad request. The app asks for an ad. MAX collects bid tokens from the network SDKs it initialized at launch and sends the request to the MAX ad server.
  2. ≤ tmax
    Bids. The auction server sends bid requests to SDK bidders and to DSPs in AppLovin Exchange. Only bids returned within the timeout tmax count.
  3. Resolve
    Unified auction. Bids are merged with the publisher's waterfall networks at their CPM prices; the highest CPM wins. Invalid bids (below the floor, missing or zero) get a loss notice now.
  4. Load
    Win and loss notices. The winner's ad is loaded. If the load fails, the winner gets a loss notice. Other bidders get their loss notice after a winning ad loads.
  5. Render
    Billing. When the ad renders (video starts, or a graphic is displayed), MAX calls the winner's billing URL, the signal on which DSPs should count impressions and spend.

Public fact. The notices carry prices. ${AUCTION_PRICE}, the final price, is substituted into both the win URL and the loss URL, "so every bidder (win or loss) sees the final paid price". The winner also receives ${AUCTION_MINIMUM_BID_TO_WIN}, defined as the second-highest bid. A loss notice carries an OpenRTB loss-reason code. These are MAX's semantics; other exchanges define their notices differently (Chapter 7). MAX notice macros

Worked example · hypothetical numbers

One MAX auction: who earns what

Given, for one ad request (prices in dollars CPM unless marked per impression): publisher floor $3.00; waterfall network W entered at $5.50. Bids: AppLovin Ads $7.50, SDK bidder X $6.80, DSP Y $6.00, DSP Z $2.00. AppLovin Ads bids for an advertiser who pays a=\$30 per conversion, with conversion probability q=0.0005 per impression, so value v=qa=\$0.015 per impression; $7.50 CPM is an illustrative bid, chosen using the half-value rule from the Chapter 4 appendix. Let \varphi=5\% be the MAX fee on winning auction spend; MAX's real fee rate is not disclosed. For this stylized CPA contract, assume the fee is charged to a third-party bidder on top of the publisher payment.

  1. Filter. Z's $2.00 is below the $3.00 floor, so Z gets a loss notice during the auction.
  2. Rank by CPM: $7.50 (AppLovin Ads) > $6.80 (X) > $6.00 (Y) > $5.50 (W). AppLovin Ads wins and, under first price, pays its bid: b=\$7.50\ \text{CPM}=\$7.50/1000=\$0.0075\ \text{per impression}.
  3. Notices. The winner learns the second-highest bid, $6.80, so it paid $7.50 − $6.80 = $0.70 CPM more than needed to win. X and Y each receive AUCTION_PRICE = $7.50 in their loss notices: the price they had to beat.
  4. Render and bill. The billing URL fires at render; the publisher is credited $0.0075 for this impression.
  5. AppLovin's revenue when its own bidder wins: the advertiser charge minus the publisher payment, per impression and in expectation over the conversion: v-b=\$0.015-\$0.0075=\$0.0075.
  6. Counterfactual: X wins at the same $7.50. AppLovin earns only the MAX fee: \varphi\,b=0.05\times\$0.0075=\$0.000375\ \text{per impression}.

Result: the publisher earns $7.50 per 1,000 such impressions whoever wins; AppLovin expects $7.50 per 1,000 when its own bidder wins and $0.375 when a third party wins at the same price, a factor of 20 under the assumed prices and fee.

Check: (v-b)/(\varphi b)=0.0075/0.000375=20. The winning price ($7.50) is at least the second bid ($6.80), as a highest-bid-wins auction requires; among comparable eligible real-time bids, a reported winning price below a losing bidder’s own valid bid would warrant reconciliation against publisher settlement records. The notice itself is generated by the auctioneer.

Under these inputs the auctioneer earns more when its own bidder wins; at a fixed publisher payment b that holds only if v-b>\varphi b. The example shows a possible incentive, not auction behavior. Inference. When AppLovin Ads buys through MAX in an action-based arrangement, it pays the publisher per impression but is paid per action, so it bears the conversion-rate risk between the two, the same segment a CPA network bears (risk map, Chapter 3).

Public fact. The 10-K states that AppLovin is an agent, not a principal, in its advertising arrangements: it does not control the inventory before it passes to the advertiser. Under US GAAP an agent reports revenue net, so reported revenue is advertiser payments minus amounts paid or payable to publishers. Revenue is recognized when an impression is delivered (impression-based arrangements) or when a specified action such as a click or install occurs (action-based arrangements). Reported revenue was $5,480.7 million in 2025 (up 70.0% from $3,224.1 million in 2024) and $2,184.8 million in Q3 2026 (up 55.5% from $1,405.0 million in Q3 2025); both exclude the divested Apps business. Revenue recognition note, AppLovin 2025 10-K, AppLovin Q3 2026 10-Q

Inference. Because revenue is net, reported revenue reveals neither total advertiser spend nor the take rate (the share of advertiser spend the platform keeps; Chapter 3). In the worked example the advertiser pays $15 per 1,000 impressions in expectation and AppLovin books $7.50; a filing shows only the latter. MAX is "free for publishers" per its website, but the same FAQ says bidding networks pay AppLovin a fee on the ad revenue they generate, and the filings describe MAX revenue as a percentage of client or winning auction spend. Since revenue outside AppLovin Ads is immaterial, MAX's value to AppLovin lies mainly in the supply and auction position it provides, not in its fees. A ROAS goal and a budget are constraints; Axon turns them into one bid per impression

An AppLovin advertiser never types a bid. Its two main settings are the two constraints of its problem: spend at most the budget each day, and earn at least the ROAS goal in revenue per dollar spent. Axon then bids on every impression so as to buy as much predicted revenue as those two constraints allow. The per-impression work sits in Axon's predictions; the two settings only fix how predicted revenue is turned into a price, and how much of it to buy.

Public fact. AppLovin's campaign API takes the following:

  • A goal type: purchase-revenue ROAS, ad-revenue ROAS or blended ROAS, as well as cost-per-install and cost-per-event goals.
  • A goal value, set once for all countries or per country.
  • A ROAS day target, day 7 or day 28: the revenue window counted from install.
  • A required daily budget, set once for all countries or per country.

The goal type is fixed when the campaign is created; the goal value and the budget can be changed later. Two bidding strategies are listed:

  • target_goal_with_cpi_billing: AppLovin “aims to control the cost per result around your target goal” and charges per install.
  • auto_bidding_with_cpm_billing: it “aims to fully use your budget while maximizing results at the lowest possible cost per result” and charges per impression.

AppLovin campaign management API. The documentation does not publish the bidding algorithm. What follows is the standard model of bidding under a value goal and a budget (Aggarwal, Badanidiyuru and Mehta, 2019), not AppLovin's disclosed method.

Model. For each impression i, Axon predicts its value, the expected day-7 revenue it will produce:

v_i=P(\text{install}\mid i)\times\mathbb E[\text{D7 revenue}\mid\text{install},\,i].

With ROAS goal R (0.4 for 40%) and a pacing factor \lambda between 0 and 1, it bids

b_i=\lambda\,\frac{v_i}{R}.
  • The goal sets the exchange rate. Paying v_i/R for v_i of expected revenue earns exactly R, so v_i/R is the most the impression is worth at the goal. MAX's auction is first-price (the winner pays its bid; see above), so bidding v_i/R everywhere yields a campaign ROAS of exactly R in expectation, provided the predictions are calibrated.
  • The budget sets \lambda. If bidding v_i/R would spend more than the budget, the pacing controller lowers \lambda until expected spend equals the budget, and ROAS comes out above the goal. If it would not, \lambda=1: the goal binds and the campaign spends less than its budget.
  • The predictions pick the impressions. One multiplier scales every bid, so a user predicted to bring twice the revenue gets twice the bid. This is why two settings suffice; chapter 4 shows why one multiplier is the right answer for a budget.
Worked example

Which constraint binds

Given, for one country and one day: N=1{,}000{,}000 alike opportunities, each with a 0.5% install probability and $2 expected D7 revenue per install, so v=0.005\times\$2=\$0.01 per impression ($10 CPM). The highest competing bid is Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,\$0.05), as in chapter 4's model with a $50 CPM ceiling. ROAS goal 40%.

  1. Bid at \lambda=1: \$0.01/0.4=\$0.025 per impression, $25 CPM. It wins with probability 0.025/0.05=0.5: 500,000 impressions.
  2. Spend 500{,}000\times\$0.025=\$12{,}500; D7 revenue 500{,}000\times\$0.01=\$5{,}000; ROAS 5{,}000/12{,}500=40\%; 2,500 installs at $5 each.
  3. Budget $20,000: it does not bind. The campaign spends $12,500 and leaves $7,500 unspent.
  4. Budget $5,000: it binds. Expected spend is Nb^2/L, so the controller lowers the bid to b=\sqrt{5{,}000\times0.05/10^6}=\$0.0158\ (\$15.81\text{ CPM}), which is \lambda=15.81/25=0.632. It wins 316,228 impressions for $5,000 and earns $3,162 of D7 revenue, a ROAS of 63.2%, with 1,581 installs at $3.16.

Result: with a slack budget the goal binds, ROAS lands on 40% and $7,500 goes unspent; with a $5,000 budget the budget binds, bids fall to 63% of the goal-implied bid, and ROAS rises to 63%.

Check: when every impression has value v and costs its bid b, ROAS is v/b: 0.01/0.025=40\% and 0.01/0.0158=63.2\% ✓. Spend 316{,}228\times\$0.0158114=\$5{,}000 ✓.

Reading the table as an advertiser:

  • Spend below budget means the goal is the lever. Raising the budget changes nothing. Lowering the goal buys volume at a worse rate.
  • The marginal return is far below the average. Moving from a 40% to a 30% goal adds $7,500 of spend and $1,325 of D7 revenue, a marginal ROAS of 17.7% even though the campaign reports 31.6% (chapter 3 explains why).
  • Spend at budget with ROAS above the goal means the budget is the lever. Raising the budget buys volume until ROAS falls to the goal.
  • Set the goal from lifetime economics. The goal counts only seven (or 28) days of revenue, so derive it from the long-run return you need. If D7 revenue is typically a quarter of first-year revenue and you need a first-year ROAS of 120%, set the D7 goal at 0.25\times120\%=30\%. Revenue is not profit: break-even on contribution needs a return of 1/m, where m is the contribution margin (chapter 3).
  • The ROAS is attributed, not caused. It counts revenue the attribution rules credit to AppLovin; only a holdout test shows how much the ads caused (chapter 6).
  • Billing decides who bears which error. With CPI billing the advertiser pays per install, so AppLovin bears errors in the predicted install rate, as the platform bears click-rate errors under CPC (chapter 3). Errors in predicted revenue per install stay with the advertiser and show up as missed ROAS. With CPM billing the advertiser bears both.
Optional: why one multiplier is optimal under both constraints

Take chapter 4's first-price model with impressions of different values: the highest competing bid Z_i\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L), so a bid b_i\le L wins with probability b_i/L and pays b_i. The advertiser maximizes expected revenue \sum_i (b_i/L)\,v_i subject to a budget, \sum_i (b_i/L)\,b_i\le B, and a ROAS goal, \sum_i (b_i/L)\,v_i\ge R\sum_i (b_i/L)\,b_i. Revenue is the objective because the advertiser has already expressed what profit it needs through R.

  1. Attach a multiplier \gamma\ge0 to the ROAS constraint and \mu\ge0 to the budget. The problem separates into one term per impression: \max_{b_i}\ \frac{b_i}{L}\Big[(1+\gamma)\,v_i-(\gamma R+\mu)\,b_i\Big].
  2. The bracket times b_i is a downward parabola, maximized at b_i=\frac{1+\gamma}{2(\gamma R+\mu)}\,v_i=\kappa\,v_i. The factor \kappa is the same for every impression: one multiplier.
  3. With bids \kappa v_i, every won impression pays \kappa per dollar of value, so campaign ROAS is 1/\kappa. The goal requires \kappa\le1/R, and the budget requires \kappa\le\kappa_B, the multiplier at which expected spend equals B.
  4. Revenue rises with \kappa, so the best choice is \kappa=\min\{1/R,\ \kappa_B\}, which is the main text's \lambda/R with \lambda=\min\{1,\ R\,\kappa_B\}.

Check: in the worked example 1/R=2.5. A $5,000 budget allows a bid of $0.0158, so \kappa_B=0.0158/0.01=1.58; then \kappa=1.58 and \lambda=0.4\times1.58=0.632 ✓. Two caveats: the bid is exactly proportional to value only because competition is uniform, and real first-price bidders shade by the shape of the competing-bid distribution; in a second-price auction one multiplier is exactly optimal without that assumption (Aggarwal, Badanidiyuru and Mehta, 2019).

Axon can predict revenue, not just clicks, because revenue labels come back to it

A model can predict any outcome it receives labels for. Clicks are simply the label an ad network observes by itself; for app campaigns, installs and revenue come back too, because the advertiser's measurement partner sends them. The value v_i in the bid above is therefore a prediction like any other, only with rarer, later and noisier labels.

Public fact. A campaign in AppLovin's API names its measurement partner (the tracking_method field, for example Adjust or AppsFlyer). Such a partner (an MMP; chapter 6) sends the ad network a postback for each install it credits to that network and for the later in-app events the advertiser chooses to forward, typically including purchases with their value. Inference. If the advertiser's own app also shows ads through MAX, AppLovin sees the ad revenue each of its users generates directly.

Each install then becomes one training example, labelled with the revenue its user generates in the 7 days after install. The label is final only a week later, which is the delayed-label problem of chapter 5. Because most users never pay and a few pay a lot, the value is usually modelled in two steps:

v_i=P(\text{install}\mid i)\times P(\text{pays within 7 days}\mid\text{install},\,i)\times\mathbb E[\text{amount}\mid\text{payer},\,i].

With a 0.5% install rate, 4% of installs paying within the week and $50 per payer, v=0.005\times0.04\times\$50=\$0.01 per impression, the value used in the example above.

At bid time no install has happened yet, so the model can use only what exists before the impression. AppLovin's published list of Axon's inputs (below) maps onto three kinds of signal:

  • Device and network: device model and price tier, operating system, country, carrier or connection type. Spending differs sharply between a new flagship phone and a budget Android.
  • Context: the app showing the ad, the placement (rewarded or interstitial) and the time of day.
  • Engagement history: where a device identifier is available, how the device responded to past ads, such as which apps it installed through AppLovin and what those installs later earned according to postbacks from other advertisers' campaigns. This lets one game's labels inform the value of a user to another game. Whether every postback may be pooled across advertisers is a question of contracts and permissions, taken up in the next section.

Revenue is much harder to predict than clicks:

PropertyClick predictionD7 revenue prediction
How often the label is positiveAbout 1 click per 100 impressionsAbout 1 payer per 5,000 impressions (0.5% × 4%)
Label delaySeconds7 days
Label shapeYes or noMostly zero with a heavy tail; one large spender can be most of a cohort's revenue
Needs a device identifierNoLargely, to link a device's history; on iOS, Apple's ATT prompt removes it for most users

Three consequences follow. Noisier estimates make the winner's curse stronger, because the auction keeps the users whose value was overestimated most. On iOS, without the identifier, the network gets Apple's coarse, delayed SKAN postbacks instead of per-user revenue (chapter 6), so predictions lean on device and context and become blurrier. And scale matters most here: a network with more installs and more revenue postbacks has more payers from which to learn the tail, which is the plausible core of a prediction advantage.

Keep three sets apart: what every bidder learns, what only the auctioneer sees, and what its own bidder may train on.

What every MAX bidder learns (public fact)

The bid request for each auction it is invited to. For evaluated bids: win or loss, a loss-reason code, and the final price AUCTION_PRICE. If it wins, the second-highest bid. A billing call when its ad renders.

What only the auctioneer sees

All bids submitted for an auction, and which invited bidders did not bid; waterfall CPM prices, floors and other publisher settings; load and render outcomes for all networks.

On a first-price win the final price is the bidder's own bid, so the minimum-to-win field is what reveals the competition; a valid outbid loss notice reveals the price to beat (how bidders learn from such feedback: Chapter 5).

How a prediction advantage becomes retained profit

Better information can never hurt a single bidder in a fixed market; it pays by changing which impressions the bidder buys and at what price, and how much of the gain the bidder keeps depends on the market. Let \Pi(\mathcal I) be the set of feasible policies (same objective, actions and budget constraints) that can be computed from information \mathcal I. Then

\mathcal I_{\text{old}}\subseteq\mathcal I_{\text{new}}\;\Rightarrow\;\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{old}})\subseteq\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{new}})\;\Rightarrow\;\max_{\pi\in\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{new}})}\mathbb E[u(\pi)]\ \ge\ \max_{\pi\in\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{old}})}\mathbb E[u(\pi)].

The proof: the better-informed bidder can ignore the extra information and replay the old policy. The premises are that competitors and the clearing-price distribution stay fixed, the information is free, available at decision time and refines the old, and the policy optimizes on accurate values. A higher offline AUC (ranking quality on held-out logs) establishes none of these.

In the uniform-threshold model, let \hat v_{\rm old} and \hat v_{\rm new} be calibrated estimates of the impression’s dollar value. Assume the same mean prediction and that both models’ bids lie in the linear part of the win curve. The change in expected profit is N[\operatorname{Var}(\hat v_{\rm new})-\operatorname{Var}(\hat v_{\rm old})]/(4L), with the same expected win count. In the example below, profit rises from $2,812.50 to $3,825 at 375,000 expected wins. In a separate example with fixed true value, multiplying the forecast by a factor r changes profit by a factor 1-(r-1)^2 and spend by a factor r^2 while bids stay within the threshold range. That example holds the forecast error fixed. Chapter 5 shows how selecting the highest of several noisy scores can also lead to overprediction.

For the refinement formula, let s=\hat v be a calibrated value estimate, so the conditional expected true value at score s is s. The threshold is independent of the value and score. At bid s/2, conditional expected profit is (s-s/2)(s/(2L))=s^2/(4L). Across N opportunities, expected profit is N[\operatorname{Var}(s)+(\mathbb E[s])^2]/(4L). The mean term cancels when old and new predictions have the same mean. Expected wins are N\mathbb E[s]/(2L), so they stay fixed because the win probability is linear over this range.

Worked example · hypothetical numbers

A sharper model: same win count, more profit

Given: N=1{,}000{,}000 requests; highest competing bid Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) with L=\$0.02 per impression, unaffected by our bids; a=\$30 per conversion; first price; no budget cap. Half the requests have q_H=0.0008, half q_L=0.0002 (mean 0.0005). The old model cannot tell them apart and predicts 0.0005 everywhere; the new model sees the segment. Both bid b=\hat v/2, the uniform-threshold optimum (Chapter 4 appendix), where \hat v=\hat q\,a is the model's value estimate per impression.

  1. Values per impression: v_H=0.0008\times\$30=\$0.024, v_L=0.0002\times\$30=\$0.006; pooled \bar v=\$0.015.
  2. Old model: bid $0.0075 ($7.50 CPM) everywhere; win rate F=0.0075/0.02=37.5\%; 375,000 wins, 187,500 per segment. Spend 375{,}000\times\$0.0075=\$2{,}812.50. Conversions 187{,}500\times0.0008+187{,}500\times0.0002=150+37.5=187.5, worth 187.5\times\$30=\$5{,}625. Profit $2,812.50.
  3. New model, high segment: bid v_H/2=\$0.012 ($12 CPM), win rate 60%, 300,000 wins, spend $3,600, 240 conversions worth $7,200.
  4. New model, low segment: bid v_L/2=\$0.003 ($3 CPM), win rate 15%, 75,000 wins, spend $225, 15 conversions worth $450.
  5. New totals: 375,000 wins, spend $3,825, 255 conversions worth $7,650, profit $3,825.
  6. In general, at b=v/2 profit per request is (v-b)\,b/L=v^2/(4L), so the gain from refinement is N\,\frac{\mathbb E[v^2]-\bar v^2}{4L}=N\,\frac{\operatorname{Var}(v)}{4L}=10^6\times\frac{0.009^2}{0.08}=\$1{,}012.50.

Result: win count unchanged at 375,000; profit +36% ($2,812.50 → $3,825); this bidder’s spend also +36%; its average payment $7.50 → $10.20 CPM.

Check: $3,825 − $2,812.50 = $1,012.50, matching step 6. Cost per conversion is b/q=a/2=\$15 in every segment under both models: $2,812.50 / 187.5 = $3,825 / 255 = $15.

Win rate did not move, so it cannot be the metric that detects a better model. The gain came from moving wins out of the low segment into the high one.

A miscalibrated model: more volume, less margin

Given: the same market with a single segment, true q=0.0005, so v=\$0.015. The model predicts \hat q=r\,q with r=1.2: every probability is 20% too high. Multiplying all scores by a constant leaves ranking and AUC unchanged.

  1. Bid: b=r\,v/2=1.2\times\$0.015/2=\$0.009 ($9 CPM).
  2. Win rate: F=0.009/0.02=45\%, so 450,000 wins.
  3. Spend 450{,}000\times\$0.009=\$4{,}050; true value 450{,}000\times0.0005\times\$30=\$6{,}750; profit $2,700.
  4. In general, with b=rv/2 and r=1+\varepsilon, profit per request is (v-b)\,b/L and spend is b^2/L: \begin{aligned}\text{profit}&=N\,\frac{v^2}{4L}\,r(2-r)=N\,\frac{v^2}{4L}\,(1-\varepsilon^2),\\ \text{spend}&=N\,\frac{v^2}{4L}\,r^2=N\,\frac{v^2}{4L}\,(1+\varepsilon)^2,\end{aligned} with Nv^2/(4L)=\$2{,}812.50.

Result: a 20% overprediction raises wins by 20% and this bidder’s spend by 44%, while the bidder's media spread falls 4%.

Check: \$2{,}812.50\times(1-0.2^2)=\$2{,}700 and \$2{,}812.50\times1.2^2=\$4{,}050. Underpredicting by the same amount (r=0.8) costs the same $112.50 of profit but cuts spend to $1,800.

Total publisher revenue. Assume every request clears, and when our bidder loses, the rival pays its own bid Z. With a fixed bid b and Z uniform from 0 to L, the publisher earns b with probability b/L. The rival wins otherwise. Averaging both payments gives b\frac bL+\int_b^L\frac zL\,dz=\frac{b^2}{L}+\frac{L^2-b^2}{2L}=\frac L2+\frac{b^2}{2L}. The first term of the left-hand side is our bidder’s spend per request. The integral accounts for the rival’s spend on the requests our bidder loses. Across a million requests, baseline publisher revenue is \$10{,}000+\$2{,}812.50/2=\$11{,}406.25. With the refined model it is \$10{,}000+\$3{,}825/2=\$11{,}912.50, up 4.4%. With the overstated predictions it is \$10{,}000+\$4{,}050/2=\$12{,}025, up 5.4%. These are illustrative market totals. Actual exchange fees depend on which winning spend is fee-bearing and on the contract.

Inference. How much of a model's gain AppLovin keeps is shaped by market structure as well as model performance. Bidding more for better opportunities raises publisher revenue; competitors and publishers' floor prices adjust; advertisers push for better pricing. The split depends on whether the information is exclusive and stable, whether customers have alternatives, and how the platform charges.

Evidence that separates a moat from good-looking metrics

A moat exists if, on the same supply surface (the inventory one can bid on), with comparable data permissions and budgets, new advertisers and extra budget keep producing incremental contribution for advertisers. Compare incremental contribution per dollar with competing bidders on matched inventory, and check whether that advantage persists. Correct for survivorship bias: a showcase of surviving high-return customers proves nothing about the rest.

Three explanations for growth need different evidence. A better model shows up as higher performance on matched supply at the same budget; privileged data, in which signals each bidder was entitled to use; self-preferencing, in loss notices that contradict the documented auction rules (any bidder can check those) and, for an own bidder that might see rivals' bids before bidding, in an audit of bid-time data access, which loss notices cannot reveal. An advertiser holdout measures one campaign's causal return; separating causes needs a comparison that varies the proposed mechanism.

Three metrics look good without proving any of this. Attributed revenue is what the attribution rule credits to the ad; incremental revenue would not have happened without it (Chapter 6). D7 ROAS is attributed revenue in the seven days after acquisition divided by acquisition spend (defined in Chapter 3).

  1. Higher pCTR with flat ROAS. If predicted click-through rate (pCTR) and the clearing CPM both rise while D7 ROAS stays flat, the model may simply be buying more clickable traffic at a higher price. Decompose the change into price and traffic mix first.
  2. Higher publisher eCPM. Publisher eCPM (revenue per thousand impressions) can rise while advertisers gain nothing: in the miscalibration example under the same market assumptions, aggregate publisher revenue rises about 5.4% while the bidder’s profit falls.
  3. Subsidized growth. Inference (generic, not a claim about AppLovin's practices). Credits or discounts buy room for trial and error, not unbiased labels. Labels from subsidized customers, cheap traffic or retargeting (ads to users who already engaged) may not transfer once full pricing applies. Check retention after subsidies end and calibration by segment.

Takeaways

  • Axon is the model, AppLovin Ads the product that earns nearly all revenue, MAX the first-price auction where it bids. Every bidder whose bid was evaluated receives the final price in its notice (whether rejected bids, e.g. below the floor, also receive it is not documented). Revenue is reported net, so advertiser spend and take rate are not disclosed.
  • The auctioneer earns far more when its own bidder wins than from its fee on a rival's win. Neutrality is the claim to test. Loss-notice prices can flag inconsistencies among eligible real-time bids for reconciliation, but not an own bidder that sees rivals' bids before bidding and bids just above them; that needs an audit of bid-time data access.
  • With uniform thresholds and interior bids, refinement can raise profit at a constant expected win count; miscalibration moves spend to first order and profit only to second order. Neither win rate nor publisher eCPM identifies a better model.
