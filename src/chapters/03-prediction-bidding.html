From prediction to bidding and budgets

Follow the money through three ledgers

Take the position of an ad network: a company that buys opportunities to show ads from publishers (owners of apps and websites with ad slots) and resells them to advertisers (businesses that want their ads shown). Three amounts are easy to conflate: what the network collects from advertisers, what it pays publishers, and what the advertiser earns from the sales the ads produce. Two published examples pin down the first two; then we add the advertiser's ledger. The billing units below are defined in Chapter 2 and restated here at first use.

Step 1 · Who pays how much per 1,000 impressions?

An impression is one display of an ad. CPM (cost per mille) quotes a price per 1,000 impressions, so $2 CPM means $0.002 per impression. In Epom's example the network buys publisher inventory (the publisher's supply of ad impressions) at $2 CPM and sells it to advertisers at $5 CPM, keeping a $3 CPM spread. Both sides are billed per impression. Source: Epom, “Ad arbitrage” section

For every 1,000 impressions the network receives $5 and pays the publisher $2. The remaining $3 is the media spread, before servers, sales staff and other operating costs. The example says nothing about whether the advertiser's $5 bought enough business.

Step 2 · What if the advertiser pays only per conversion?

A conversion is an action the advertiser values, such as a purchase or an app install. CPC (cost per click) and CPA (cost per action) contracts bill the advertiser only when that click or action occurs, not per impression. Kevel's documentation uses 9,000 impressions, 18 billable events and $5 per event. Kevel allows the billable event to be a click or another action; we take the CPA reading and treat each event as a conversion that the contract recognizes. Source: Kevel, CPC/CPA worked example under “What Is eCPM?”

eCPM (effective CPM) restates revenue earned under any contract as revenue per 1,000 impressions:

QuestionCalculationAnswer
How much does the advertiser pay in total?18 conversions × $5$90
How much is collected per 1,000 impressions?$90 ÷ 9,000 × 1,000$10 eCPM

The contract is still $5 per conversion. The $10 eCPM converts revenue already earned into a per-1,000-impression figure so it can be compared with the price paid for impressions. It does not turn the contract into a CPM contract, and it does not guarantee that the next 9,000 impressions will earn the same.

Step 3 · Collect per conversion, pay per impression

The following combined example is constructed for this reader; neither source describes this transaction. Keep Kevel's 9,000 impressions, 18 conversions and $5 CPA, and assume the network bought those impressions at Epom's $2 CPM.

Whose ledgerCalculationAmount
Advertiser's ad bill18 conversions × $5 CPAPays $90
Publisher's ad revenue9,000 impressions ÷ 1,000 × $2 CPMReceives $18
Network's media spread$90 collected − $18 paid to the publisherKeeps $72

This is “buy per impression, sell per conversion.” The network pays for every impression it buys but is paid only when a contractual conversion occurs. If the same $18 of impressions yields only 2 conversions, the network collects $10 and loses $8. Conversion prediction tells the network which impressions are worth buying; it cannot make conversions happen.

Step 4 · The network kept $72. Did the advertiser make money?

We cannot tell yet. We know only that the advertiser spent $90. We do not know what each extra order is worth to the advertiser, or how many of the 18 recorded conversions the ad actually caused.

Two terms separate these questions. A conversion is attributed to an ad when an agreed rule (for example, “purchase within 7 days of a click”) credits it to that ad; attribution decides what gets billed. A conversion is incremental if it would not have happened without the ad; incrementality decides what the advertiser actually gained. Teaching assumption: each incremental order leaves $8 of contribution, meaning revenue minus cost of goods, fulfillment and other variable costs, before ad spend. The bill and media cost stay as above:

Assumed scenarioAdvertiser's calculationIncremental contribution after ad spend
All 18 are incremental18 × $8 − $90+$54
Only 9 are incremental9 × $8 − $90−$18

In both scenarios the network bills $90 for 18 contractual conversions, pays the publisher $18 and keeps $72, yet the advertiser's outcomes have opposite signs. The attributed conversions used for billing are not the incremental conversions. Measuring the latter requires causal methods, covered in Chapter 4.

The three ledgers are now separate. The publisher counts revenue from selling impressions; the network counts collections minus media cost; the advertiser counts incremental contribution minus the ad bill. That sets up the bidding question: for impressions not yet bought, how much should the network bid, and how many should it buy?

1. Two contracts before any algorithm

There are three parties: a merchant selling goods, an app that shows ads, and an ad network between them. “Platform” below means this ad network. Other intermediaries (exchanges and supply-side platforms, see Chapter 2) and their fees are left out so the money flows stay visible.

RoleWhat it doesHow money is settled
AdvertiserSells goods and wants ads to bring more ordersPays the platform $30 for each order the contract recognizes

Ad network / platformBuys opportunities from the publisher and delivers the advertiser's adsCollects from the advertiser, pays the publisher, and keeps the difference, positive or negative

Contract A: advertiser → platform, billed per order. In this example a conversion means placing an order and nothing else. A billable attributed conversion is an order that the attribution rule credits to one of the platform's impressions and that passes the contract's checks. We assume each such order is billed exactly once, in full.

This is a CPA contract with settlement price a=\$30 per order. That is a fixed payment price. It differs from a target CPA (tCPA), where the system tries to keep the average acquisition cost near a target (Chapter 2). An order being attributed to an ad does not prove the ad caused it; step 5 quantifies the gap.

Contract B: platform → publisher, impressions bought one auction at a time. An ad opportunity is one slot at one moment in which an ad could be shown. A bid request is the message in which the publisher (directly or through an exchange) invites bids on that opportunity. Each request is auctioned separately within milliseconds; that is what real-time bidding (RTB) means. To win is to be selected by the auction. A successful impression is the ad actually completing the display event defined in the contract. An ad can fail to load after a win, so the two events are kept separate.

Bids are quoted in CPM: $6 CPM means $6 ÷ 1,000 = $0.006 per impression. Each auction still sells a single opportunity; a CPM quote does not buy 1,000 impressions at once. The default is a first-price auction: the winner pays its own bid. By default every win also yields a successful impression.

The publisher is paid under the procurement contract (B); the advertiser is billed separately under the order contract (A).

Why does a $6 CPM bid win 30% of opportunities?

Two questions are separate: what price must we beat in one auction? and how often does a fixed bid beat it across many auctions? The first defines the competitive threshold; the second needs a probability model.

1. One auction: our bid against the highest competing bid

Assume every opportunity has at least one competing bid, and let Z be the highest of them. A floor price (reserve price) is the seller's minimum acceptable price; this model has none. Z is then the competitive threshold. We choose our bid b. If competitors bid $3, $5 and $9 CPM, then Z = $9 CPM: a $6 CPM bid loses and a $10 CPM bid wins. Our own bid is not part of the maximum that defines Z.

Z=\max\{\text{competing bids}\},\qquad \text{win}\iff b\ge Z.

Ties go to us (b\ge Z wins). Under the continuous distribution below an exact tie has probability zero, so the convention affects no result; with discrete price increments, real tie-breaking rules can matter. The threshold decides whether we win, not necessarily what we pay: in the default first-price auction the winner pays its own bid.

2. Many auctions: treat the unknown threshold as random

Different opportunities draw different competitors and bids, so Z varies across auctions, and the platform does not know the next value in advance. The teaching model assumes Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) (\sim reads “is distributed as”): price intervals of equal length are equally likely. We further assume every opportunity's threshold has this same distribution and that our own bidding does not move it (we are a price taker). The assumption concerns the highest competing bid, not each competitor's bid.

On the $0–20 CPM range, each $2-wide interval has probability 2/20 = 10%; the intervals $0–2 and $12–14 are equally likely.

3. We win exactly when the threshold falls below our bid

For a fixed b we win exactly when Z\in[0,b], an interval of length b inside a range of length L. Let F be the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of Z, so F(b) is the win probability at bid b:

F(b)=P(Z\le b)=\frac{b-0}{L-0}=\frac{b}{L},\qquad 0\le b\le L.

The denominator is the whole price range, not our bid. A $6 CPM bid wins against thresholds in $0–6 (length 6) and loses against thresholds in $6–20 (length 14): probabilities 6/20 = 30% and 14/20 = 70%.

$0$5$10$15$20 CPM

Win: thresholds $0–6.0 CPM → 6 / 20 = 30%. Lose: thresholds above $6.0 CPM → 70%.

This explorer lets you choose a bid to see its win probability. It is separate from the later calculator, which chooses a bid to maximize platform spread under a budget.

Outside the range, F(b)=0 for b<0 and F(b)=1 for b\ge L. Bidding above L buys no extra win probability and, under first-price, only raises the payment, so such bids are dominated.

4. CPM and per-event prices give the same probability

Every price in a comparison must use the same unit. Internally b, Z and L are in dollars per billable event; a CPM quote is 1,000 times that. The subscript CPM marks a quote, not a different quantity:

\begin{aligned} b_{\text{CPM}}&=1000\,b,\qquad L_{\text{CPM}}=1000\,L,\\ \frac{b}{L}&=\frac{\$0.006}{\$0.02}=\frac{\$6\ \text{CPM}}{\$20\ \text{CPM}}=0.30. \end{aligned}

The factor 1,000 cancels. Dividing a $6 CPM quote by $0.02 per event mixes units and gives 300, which is not a probability.

The publisher contract fixes what a billable event is: a win under win-based billing, a successful impression under display-based billing. At the default \rho=1 the two counts coincide. Switching the billing basis changes media cost; it does not change the win event Z\le b.

5. From win probability to expected wins

Let W be the number of opportunities won. With the same bid and the same marginal threshold distribution on every opportunity, linearity of expectation gives

\mathbb E[W]=N\,F(b)=1{,}000{,}000\times0.30=300{,}000\ \text{wins}.

Linearity needs no independence. Dependence across opportunities (for example, one competitor bidding on correlated traffic) changes only how widely the realized count fluctuates around 300,000. With \rho=1, these wins are also 300,000 expected impressions.

Optional: the density derivation, and what changes outside this model

The uniform threshold has density f(z)=1/L on [0,L] and 0 elsewhere, where z denotes a possible value of Z. The total area is L\cdot(1/L)=1. Integrating the density over the winning interval recovers the CDF:

F(b)=\int_0^b f(z)\,dz=\int_0^b\frac{1}{L}\,dz=\frac{b}{L},\qquad 0\le b\le L.

In a non-uniform market the win probability is still F(b)=P(Z\le b), but not b/L: if 80% of thresholds lie below $6 CPM, a $6 CPM bid wins 80%, not 30%. Floor prices, discrete bids, heterogeneous opportunity types and competitors that react to our bids each need a different model. Note also that the maximum of n independent \mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) competing bids has CDF (b/L)^n, so uniform individual bids do not produce a uniform threshold.

One more derived quantity is needed: the platform's expected revenue per successful impression, v. It follows from Contract A and is not a separate input:

v=q\,a=0.0005\times\$30=\$0.015\ \text{per impression}.

“Expected” means the average over many repetitions under the same conditions; orders come in whole numbers, but their expected count can be fractional. $0.015 per impression is $15 per 1,000 impressions, the platform's revenue eCPM. This is only a change of units; the contract still bills per order.

One simplification holds throughout: q is the same for every opportunity and does not depend on Z or b. If q and Z were correlated (for example, because competitors also bid more for likely buyers), a higher bid would buy a different mix of users and a single q would no longer apply.

4. Why the calculator bids $6.3246 CPM

Now the optimization problem: the platform chooses one bid, used on every opportunity, to maximize expected media gross spread, subject to expected media cost of at most $2,000. The platform is risk-neutral: it maximizes the expectation and ignores variance. “Optimal” is relative to this objective; the advertiser's profit is not in it. Because all opportunities are identical and the problem is concave (shown below), a single common bid is optimal; varying bids across identical opportunities cannot help.

First compare a few bids. All amounts are expected values:

Bid (CPM)Media costAdvertiser billPlatform gross spreadWithin budget?

Write step 3 as a function of b. With \rho=1, wins equal successful impressions; the formulas hold for 0\le b\le L:

\begin{aligned} D(b)&=N\,\frac{b}{L}, & A(b)&=v\,D(b),\\ C(b)&=b\,D(b)=\frac{Nb^2}{L}, & M(b)&=A(b)-C(b)=\frac{N}{L}\bigl(vb-b^2\bigr). \end{aligned}

A higher bid raises both the win rate and the price per win, so cost grows with b^2. Ignoring the budget, set the derivative to zero:

M'(b)=\frac{N}{L}\,(v-2b)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ b_0=\frac{v}{2}=\$0.0075\ \text{per impression}=\$7.50\ \text{CPM}.

b_0 is the budget-free optimum. Since M''(b)=-2N/L<0, M is strictly concave and b_0 is its unique global maximum on [0,L]; the upper boundary does not bind because b_0=0.0075<L=0.02. But b_0 costs $2,812.50, above the $2,000 budget. A feasible bid must satisfy

C(b)=\frac{Nb^2}{L}\le B\iff b\le b_B=\sqrt{\frac{BL}{N}}=\sqrt{\frac{2000\times0.02}{10^6}}=\$0.006324555\ldots\ \text{per impression}.

M increases on [0,b_0], so the best feasible bid is the largest one that is not past the peak, within budget, and not above L. Call it b^*:

b^*=\min\Bigl\{\frac{v}{2},\ \sqrt{\frac{BL}{N}},\ L\Bigr\}=\min\{0.0075,\ 0.006324555\ldots,\ 0.02\}\ \to\ \$6.3246\ \text{CPM (displayed)}.

The Lagrangian gives the same answer and introduces the multiplier used in the main text. Maximize M(b)-\mu\,\bigl(C(b)-B\bigr) with \mu\ge0 and complementary slackness \mu\,\bigl(C(b^*)-B\bigr)=0. The first-order condition \frac{N}{L}\bigl(v-2(1+\mu)b\bigr)=0 gives b=v/\bigl(2(1+\mu)\bigr). When the budget binds, b=b_B, so

1+\mu=\frac{v}{2b_B}=\frac{0.015}{0.012649\ldots}=1.18585\ldots,\qquad \mu\approx0.186.

\mu is the shadow price of the budget: one more budget dollar adds about $0.186 of expected spread. Check: the optimal spread is M^*(B)=M(b_B), with db_B/dB=b_B/(2B) and N/L=B/b_B^2, so dM^*/dB=\frac{N}{L}(v-2b_B)\frac{b_B}{2B}=\frac{v}{2b_B}-1=\mu.

In the calculator, “budget binding” means the budget, not the spread peak, sets the bid: the platform would bid $7.50 CPM but can afford only about $6.3246. It does not mean the advertiser's bill is $2,000. The budget constrains expected spend, so a realized day can still overspend; per-request stop rules (hard caps) are outside this example.

Conversely, raise the budget to $6,000. Then b_B=\sqrt{6000\times0.02/10^6}\approx\$10.95 CPM exceeds $7.50 CPM, so the platform still bids $7.50 CPM and spends only $2,812.50. The objective is spread; exhausting the budget is not a goal. The budget stops binding at B=Nb_0^2/L=\$2{,}812.50.

The advertiser's loss is not a platform miscalculation. The platform is paid per attributed order, while the advertiser needs incremental contribution to cover the whole bill, and the contract does not align the two. The example shows the gap can exist; it does not claim an informed advertiser would accept this price for long.

The total bill divided by incremental orders is the true incremental CPA, the ad spend per ad-caused order: $4,743.416… ÷ 63.245553… = $75 (equivalently qa/\Delta q=0.015/0.0002), well above the $40 contribution per order. Its denominator differs from that of the contractual $30 per attributed order.

The advertiser breaks even (incremental contribution profit of zero) when contribution per impression covers the bill per impression:

\Delta q\,g\ge q\,a\iff\Delta q\ge\frac{0.0005\times30}{40}=0.000375=3.75\ \text{per }10{,}000.

Move the \Delta q slider from 2 to 3.75 per 10,000 and the advertiser breaks even, while the platform's bid and collections stay put: the platform's objective uses q and a, not \Delta q. Note also that \Delta q and q measure different things and neither bounds the other; nothing here requires \Delta q<q.

Extension A: why does the formula change under “ideal second-price”?

An ideal second-price auction charges the winner the competitive threshold Z instead of its own bid b; floor prices, bid increments and fees are ignored. If you bid $6 CPM and the highest competing bid is $4 CPM, first-price charges $6 CPM and ideal second-price charges $4 CPM. You win either way; only the payment differs.

For a fixed b the win rate is still b/L. Conditional on winning, Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,b), so the expected payment per win is \mathbb E[Z\mid Z\le b]=b/2. With the default \rho=1:

\begin{aligned} C(b)&=N\cdot\frac{b}{L}\cdot\frac{b}{2}=\frac{Nb^2}{2L},\\ M(b)&=\frac{N}{L}\Bigl(vb-\frac{b^2}{2}\Bigr),\qquad M'(b)=\frac{N}{L}\,(v-b),\qquad M''(b)=-\frac{N}{L}<0. \end{aligned}

Ignoring the budget, the best bid is b_0=v: bid your value, the familiar truthful-bidding result for second-price auctions. Then apply the budget and the price range:

b^*=\min\Bigl\{v,\ \sqrt{\frac{2BL}{N}},\ L\Bigr\}=\min\{0.015,\ 0.0089443\ldots,\ 0.02\}\ \to\ \$8.9443\ \text{CPM}.

With the same $2,000 budget, the win rate is about 44.72%, the advertiser bill $6,708.20, the platform gross spread $4,708.20 and the advertiser incremental contribution profit −$3,130.50. The Lagrangian condition is now b=v/(1+\mu), so the budget's shadow price rises to \mu=v/b^*-1\approx0.677. More impressions mean more attributed orders billed; with these loss-making parameters the advertiser loses more.

The comparison holds the threshold distribution and the other inputs fixed, not the advertiser bill. In reality a change of auction rule also changes competitors' bids, so this is not a prediction of market-level outcomes.

Extension B: are failed displays billed? Compute a 50% success rate first, then the full formula

\rho is the probability that a win becomes a successful impression. At \rho = 50%, on average 50 of every 100 wins produce an impression. Revenue comes only from orders after successful impressions; media cost depends on the publisher contract.

Fix a first-price bid of $6 CPM without re-optimizing. It still wins 300,000 times, but only 150,000 impressions succeed, so collections are 150,000 × 0.0005 × $30 = $2,250.

Publisher contractMedia costPlatform gross spread
Display-based: billed only for successful impressions150,000 × $0.006 = $900$2,250 − $900 = $1,350
Win-based: billed on every win300,000 × $0.006 = $1,800$2,250 − $1,800 = $450

Under win-based billing, failed displays also cost money, so the re-optimized bid is lower. Both contracts are teaching settings for this calculator.

To cover all four combinations with one formula, introduce two pieces of notation; they are not new parameters to estimate:

  • \alpha (alpha), the media billing coefficient: the probability that a win is billed. \alpha=\rho under display-based billing; \alpha=1 under win-based billing.
  • k, the auction payment coefficient: k=1 for first-price and k=2 for ideal second-price, so the expected price per billed event is b/k. It is not a fee rate, and it is unrelated to the click indicator K used later in the chapter.

Assume display success is independent of Z, so conditioning on a successful display does not change the expected payment per win. Then

\begin{aligned} D&=N\,\frac{b}{L}\,\rho, & C&=N\,\frac{b}{L}\,\alpha\,\frac{b}{k}=\frac{N\alpha b^2}{kL},\\ A&=D\,q\,a=D\,v, & M&=\frac{N}{L}\Bigl(\rho vb-\frac{\alpha b^2}{k}\Bigr). \end{aligned}

Setting M'(b)=\frac{N}{L}\bigl(\rho v-2\alpha b/k\bigr)=0 gives the budget-free bid b_0=k\rho v/(2\alpha), a maximum because M''(b)=-2N\alpha/(kL)<0. Solving C\le B gives the budget cap. Together they form the calculator's complete formula:

\begin{aligned} b^*&=\min\Bigl\{\frac{k\rho v}{2\alpha},\ \sqrt{\frac{kBL}{N\alpha}},\ L\Bigr\},\\ \text{advertiser incremental contribution profit}&=D\,(\Delta q\,g-q\,a). \end{aligned}

The \rho slider covers 50%–100%, so \alpha>0. At \rho = 50% with the $2,000 budget and first-price, re-optimizing gives: under display-based billing (\alpha=\rho, so \rho cancels in b_0), b^* = $7.50 CPM and media cost $1,406.25; under win-based billing (\alpha=1), b^* = $3.75 CPM and media cost $703.13 (exactly $703.125). Neither budget binds. These are re-optimized bids, a different exercise from the fixed-$6 comparison above.

Define the event the model predicts

Let x be the information available when the bid is placed (user, context, ad slot and ad). Define three indicators: S=1 if the impression succeeds; K=1 if the user clicks; Y=1 if a qualifying conversion occurs within the agreed observation window (the period after the ad in which a conversion counts, for example 7 days); each is 0 otherwise. In this chapter's example the contract bills post-click attributed purchases, so a billable event requires K=1 and Y=1. The billable conversion probability per successful impression is

q(x)=P(K=1,Y=1\mid x,S=1)=P(K=1\mid x,S=1)\cdot P(Y=1\mid K=1,x,S=1).

This is the chain rule; it does not assume clicks and conversions are independent. If the contract also counts view-through conversions (conversions after an impression without a click), add the qualifying K=0 branch. CTR (click-through rate) is clicks per impression, and pCTR is a model's predicted CTR. CVR (conversion rate) and pCVR are the observed and predicted conversion rates (Chapter 2). Always state the denominator: P(Y=1\mid K=1,x,S=1) is per click, while q(x) is per successful impression. If a model already predicts conversions per impression, do not multiply by CTR again.

When q(x) feeds the bidding model below, we also assume that, given x and a successful impression, response probabilities do not depend on the bid b or the competitive threshold Z (defined in the next sections). Failed displays produce no conversions. The expected number of billable conversions from one bid request, before we know whether we win, is therefore F(b)\,\rho\,q(x), where F(b) is the win probability and \rho=P(S=1\mid\text{win}) the display success rate. q(x) itself is not a per-request probability.

For a network paid per billable conversion at settlement price a, v(x)=q(x)\,a is the expected revenue per successful impression. With this chapter's teaching values q=0.0005 and a=\$30, v=\$0.015. For the advertiser, the relevant value is expected contribution per conversion; for incremental value, one must also ask what would have happened had this ad not been shown. Response prediction, value prediction and incrementality (uplift) prediction have different targets.

Model metrics. Positives are samples in which the target event occurred; negatives are samples in which it did not. AUC (area under the ROC curve) is the probability that a randomly drawn positive scores higher than a randomly drawn negative, with ties counting one half. It measures ranking, not whether the probability values are right. A model is calibrated if, among samples predicted at probability p, the observed frequency is p once their labels have matured, i.e., once the observation window has closed and late conversions are counted.

Log loss scores probabilistic predictions. For a label y\in\{0,1\} and predicted probability p\in(0,1), the per-sample loss is -\bigl[y\ln p+(1-y)\ln(1-p)\bigr]; a lower average over a fixed evaluation set is better. The score depends on the evaluation sample, the label definition and any sample weights, and it does not substitute for delivery profit.

What each stage of the model pipeline does

Candidate retrieval narrows the eligible ads to the few worth scoring; ranking then estimates response and value for that smaller set; finally, value estimates, budgets, eligibility rules and the auction rules produce the decision. If retrieval drops a high-value ad, no downstream model can recover it, so improving ranking AUC alone may never touch the system's main bottleneck. Meta's Andromeda, described publicly in 2024, shows staged retrieval followed by ranking. It is one industrial example, not a claim about every platform or about Axon's current architecture (Chapter 6). Meta 2024 Andromeda

Ad features typically describe the advertiser and campaign (an advertiser's unit of budget, targeting and goal), the creative (the actual image, video or text shown), the ad slot, the user or context, past interactions and time. What matters is not the feature list but whether each feature is available at decision time, permitted for use, stably covered, and identical in meaning in training and serving. New ads and creatives have no feedback history, so the model must rely on transferable content and context features, and a controlled exploration budget must buy them impressions. Otherwise past winners keep accumulating labels and new candidates never get the chance to prove themselves.

For post-click conversion, ESMM (Entire Space Multi-Task Model) trains on all impressions using the chain impression → click → conversion: it learns pCTR and pCTCVR = pCTR × pCVR jointly and obtains pCVR as their ratio. This addresses sample selection bias: a CVR model trained only on clicked samples is applied to all impressions. ESMM does not by itself fix selection bias created by the serving policy (data logged under one policy used to learn or evaluate another), delayed feedback (conversions that arrive after the label is read), or causal identification. Original ESMM paper

Accept a model only after checking ranking, calibration, stability across segments and business outcomes together. AUC measures one property of ranking, but bidding needs absolute probabilities, because value is a probability times a price. Doubling every predicted probability (while staying at or below 1) leaves ranking and AUC unchanged; under linear value bidding with a fixed coefficient and no cap, it also doubles every bid. Good overall calibration does not imply calibration on slices such as expensive publishers, new users or one country. Google's 2013 industrial paper already treated sampling, calibration and confidence as system problems; a larger model does not make them disappear. First-price and second-price auctions cost different amounts

The following simplified decision model is original to this reader; by default every win yields a successful impression. Let Z\ge0 be the competitive threshold, the highest competing bid, which we must meet or beat to win; b\ge0 our bid; and v\ge0 the expected monetary value of winning and being shown. We win if and only if b\ge Z; ties have probability zero in this continuous model. For fixed x, F(b\mid x)=P(Z\le b\mid x) is the conditional CDF of the threshold, i.e., the win rate at bid b, and f(z\mid x)=\partial F(z\mid x)/\partial z is its density. From here on we fix x and drop it, writing F(b) and f(b); in integrals z denotes a value of Z and dF(z)=f(z)\,dz.

Bids, values and payments are in dollars per won-and-displayed opportunity; multiply by 1,000 for a CPM interface. Assumptions: the bidder is risk-neutral, maximizing expected net profit; given x, v does not depend on Z; one bid does not change later responses; there are no fees, ties or dynamic feedback; and there is no budget yet. Let \pi_1(b) and \pi_2(b) be the expected net profit per request under first-price and second-price, counting zero revenue and zero cost on a loss; primes denote derivatives in b. Formulas containing F/f apply only to interior candidates with f(b)>0; the allowed bid range, its boundaries and the global optimum must still be checked.

In a first-price auction the winner pays its own bid. Expected net profit per request is

\pi_1(b)=(v-b)F(b),\qquad \pi_1'(b)=(v-b)f(b)-F(b).

An interior optimum therefore satisfies

b=v-\frac{F(b)}{f(b)}.

This is an implicit equation, not a fixed discount. The gap F(b)/f(b) is the bid shading: bidding below value trades a lower payment per win against fewer wins. The second-order condition is \pi_1''(b)=-2f(b)+(v-b)f'(b)<0 at the candidate. A convenient sufficient condition for a unique global maximum is that F/f is nondecreasing (F log-concave): then \pi_1'(b)=f(b)\bigl[(v-b)-F(b)/f(b)\bigr] changes sign once, from positive to negative. The optimum always lies in [0,v], because any b>v earns \pi_1(b)\le0\le\pi_1(0). For uniform thresholds F(b)/f(b)=b, so b=v/2.

In an ideal second-price auction the winner pays the threshold. Expected net profit per request is

\pi_2(b)=\int_0^b(v-z)\,dF(z),\qquad \pi_2'(b)=(v-b)f(b).

\pi_2'\ge0 for b<v and \pi_2'\le0 for b>v, so under the assumptions above b=v is optimal (truthful bidding) whenever bidding v is allowed. If the threshold has no probability mass near v, nearby bids do equally well and the optimum is not unique. This does not mean every ad system should bid truthfully. GSP (generalized second-price auction) sells several ranked slots with different position values, and mechanisms with quality scores (multipliers that weight bids by predicted ad quality) must be modeled under their own rules. Real transactions also involve floor prices, fees, discrete bid increments and internal auctions (an intermediary first runs its own auction among its advertisers, then bids externally with the winner). Google Ad Exchange announced its move to first-price auctions in 2019, which is enough to show that second-price textbooks are not an operating manual for current markets. Google first-price migration announcement

The budget-free optimum in the shared example

Let the threshold be Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) with L=\$0.02 per impression, independent of response probabilities and identically distributed across requests. For 0\le b\le L the win rate is F(b)=b/L. With network value v=\$0.015 per impression (q=0.0005, a=\$30), first-price net profit per request is (v-b)\,b/L, maximized at b=v/2=\$0.0075, a bid of $7.50 CPM.

Over 1,000,000 requests, this gives 375,000 expected wins and 375,000 × 0.0005 = 187.5 attributed conversions. The network collects $5,625 from the advertiser, pays the publisher $2,812.50, and keeps a gross spread of $2,812.50. Gross spread is before infrastructure, channel fees, operations, bad debt and cost of capital; it is not final profit.

The advertiser's ledger is more revealing. Assume, as in the optional derivation, a contribution of g=\$40 per order and a true incremental conversion probability of \Delta q=0.0002 per impression (the order probability with the ad minus without it). Counting every attributed conversion as worth $40 gives 187.5 × $40 − $5,625 = $1,875, which looks profitable. Counting only incremental orders gives 375,000 × 0.0002 = 75 orders and 75 × $40 − $5,625 = −$2,625. The intermediary can make money while its customer loses money. If the gap persists, it eventually erodes the customer's budgets, renewals and the intermediary's pricing power.

Write the two budget objectives separately

Index the bid requests in the planning period by i. For request i, b_i is the bid, v_i the expected value of winning and being shown, F_i the threshold CDF, and C_i(b_i) the expected media cost of the request, counting zero spend on a loss. B is the total media budget for the period. Sums run over all requests; “s.t.” means “subject to.” Note that C_i(b) is a per-request cost, whereas C in the optional calculator is the total over requests.

Per request, expected cost is C(b)=b\,F(b) under first-price and C(b)=\int_0^b z\,dF(z) under ideal second-price. To maximize total value within the budget:

\max_{b_i}\sum_i v_iF_i(b_i) \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_i C_i(b_i)\le B.

Attach a multiplier \lambda\ge0 to the budget constraint. The Lagrangian separates across requests, with per-request term v\,F(b)-\lambda\,C(b) (subscript dropped). \lambda is measured in value dollars per budget dollar. Under the usual regularity conditions it is the shadow price of the budget: the increase in optimal total value per extra budget dollar. Using C'(b)=F(b)+b\,f(b) for first-price and C'(b)=b\,f(b) for second-price, the interior first-order conditions with \lambda>0 and f(b)>0 are

\text{Second-price:}\quad b=\frac{v}{\lambda}, \qquad \text{First-price:}\quad b=\frac{v}{\lambda}-\frac{F(b)}{f(b)}.

To maximize net profit under the same budget instead, solve \max_{b_i}\sum_i\bigl[v_iF_i(b_i)-C_i(b_i)\bigr] s.t. \sum_i C_i(b_i)\le B. The per-request term becomes v\,F(b)-(1+\mu)\,C(b), where \mu\ge0 is the budget multiplier for this objective: the 1 stands for the cost itself and \mu for the extra cost of budget scarcity. Under the same interior conditions:

\text{Second-price:}\quad b=\frac{v}{1+\mu}, \qquad \text{First-price:}\quad b=\frac{v}{1+\mu}-\frac{F(b)}{f(b)}.

Complementary slackness, \mu\bigl(\sum_i C_i(b_i)-B\bigr)=0, pins down \mu. A slack budget gives \mu=0, which recovers the budget-free profit optimum; otherwise \mu is set so that expected spend equals B. In the uniform example with B = $2,000, \mu\approx0.186 under first-price and \mu\approx0.677 under second-price (derived in the optional section).

The difference between the objectives is economic, not notational. If both objectives exhaust the budget at the same bids, comparing the conditions gives \lambda=1+\mu\ge1. But a total-value bidder can end up with \lambda<1; its second-price bid v/\lambda then exceeds v, so it knowingly buys impressions that cost more than they are worth, because its objective counts value, not profit. With a slack budget the total-value objective has no reason to economize at all: it buys every opportunity with positive value, and “set \lambda=0 in the division formula” is not a valid step. Budget constraints, ROAS constraints (a floor on return on ad spend; see Chapter 2) and CPA constraints (a cap on average cost per action) are also different problems. Transplanting multipliers between papers often produces strategies that look alike but mean different things.

All of this optimizes expectations under a known threshold distribution. If the distribution is unknown, it must be learned; if only win/loss outcomes are observed, the problem changes again (see the last section of this chapter). Recent research on first-price bidding with budgets distinguishes these feedback regimes, and its guarantees do not automatically transfer to production markets. How a $2,000 media budget changes the bid

Return to the homogeneous teaching parameters: N = 1,000,000 requests; threshold upper bound L=\$0.02 per impression; network value v=\$0.015 per successful impression; media budget B = $2,000; billable conversion probability q=0.0005 per impression; settlement price a=\$30 per order; every win is a successful impression. The objective is expected collections minus media cost.

The first-price budget-free optimum would spend $2,812.50, above the $2,000 limit. When the budget binds, Nb^2/L=B, so b=\sqrt{BL/N}=\$0.006324555. For this homogeneous group, the two objectives yield the same bid. More generally, suppose both objectives' optima exhaust the same budget B on the same feasible set. On the boundary \sum_i C_i=B, net profit equals total value minus the constant B, so both objectives select the same point on that boundary, even with heterogeneous opportunities. We make no claim about other tied optima off the boundary. This is not a general equivalence: a net-profit objective may deliberately underspend while a total-value objective keeps buying, and any change to the feasible set or the constraints requires solving again. The budget here also constrains expected spend only; realized requests and payments are random, and enforcing a hard cap on every realized path is a separate system responsibility.

Budget pacing is online control, not even spending

Pacing controls the rate of spend over time. The simplest illustrative update is

\mu_{t+1}=\max\{0,\ \mu_t+\eta\,(c_t-s_t)\}.

Here t indexes control periods (for example, minutes). \mu_t\ge0 is the budget multiplier in period t, not the bid; the bid is derived from it, for example b=v/(1+\mu_t) under ideal second-price, or b=v/(1+\mu_t)-F(b)/f(b) under first-price, which is v/\bigl(2(1+\mu_t)\bigr) for uniform thresholds. c_t is actual spend and s_t target spend in period t. The step size \eta>0, in units of 1/dollar, converts a spend error into a multiplier change, and \max\{0,\cdot\} keeps the multiplier nonnegative. Overspending raises \mu and lowers bids; underspending does the reverse. The update is projected subgradient descent on the Lagrangian dual: by the envelope theorem the dual objective's derivative in \mu is budget minus spend, so a step against it moves \mu by +\eta\,(c_t-s_t). It sets the direction of control, not stability: delayed billing data, traffic cycles and interacting control loops can make it oscillate. Feedback control in RTB advertising

The target trajectory s_t need not be flat over the day. If evening opportunities are worth more, hold budget back for the evening; if high-value supply is scarce, an unspent budget is not necessarily a fault. Distinguish under-delivery caused by a system failure while profitable opportunities exist from stopping because no opportunity meets the economic constraints. Forcing full spend through bid multipliers quietly turns value maximization into budget-consumption maximization.

Allocating across many campaigns adds more dimensions: which advertiser gets a given opportunity, how much budget each campaign has left, whether there are guaranteed-delivery obligations (contracts that promise a fixed number of impressions), and whether the platform earns a service fee, a per-impression price or a per-conversion price. Why price feedback cannot be used directly as a regression label

In ideal second-price, let Z be the competitive threshold, b the logged bid and x the decision-time information, with conditional CDF F(z\mid x) and density f(z\mid x). Assume a win reveals Z (it is the price paid), while a loss reveals only Z>b. Winning prices are selected by the policy: they are exactly the thresholds that fell below our bids. A regression fitted directly to winning prices therefore misses the high-price tail and underestimates the threshold. Let \ell be the log-likelihood (natural log) and “win” and “loss” the sets of won and lost requests; each term uses that request's own Z, b and x. For conditionally independent requests the censored log-likelihood is

\ell= \sum_{\mathrm{win}}\log f(Z\mid x) +\sum_{\mathrm{loss}}\log\bigl(1-F(b\mid x)\bigr).

Each won request contributes the density at its observed threshold; each lost request contributes the probability that the threshold exceeded its bid. This is valid only if wins and losses are determined purely by the price comparison, and if, once the price-relevant policy history used for bidding is included in the logged x, the bid carries no further information about the threshold: P(Z\le z\mid x,b)=F(z\mid x) (non-informative censoring). If the policy used unlogged, price-relevant information, the observation model must be extended. Timeouts, creative rejections, budget filtering and users leaving the page are not “the market price exceeded the bid” and must not be coded as such losses. The price-distribution chapter of the RTB monograph is a good entry point to the censoring problem. RTB monograph, Chapter 3

In first-price, our payment b is not the competitive threshold; on a win it is only an upper bound on it. With win/loss feedback alone, a win tells us Z\le b and a loss tells us Z>b, so every observation is censored and the log-likelihood becomes

\ell= \sum_{\mathrm{win}}\log F(b\mid x) +\sum_{\mathrm{loss}}\log\bigl(1-F(b\mid x)\bigr).

This is a binary-response likelihood for the win probability. Without a parametric model it identifies F only at the bids actually placed, so learning the whole distribution requires variation in b. It is never valid to label your own winning bid as the competitors' price. Some exchanges return extra signals (for example, the minimum bid that would have won), so check the actual interface.
