Products and cash flow

In this chapter

  • Describe any ad product by five separate choices: context, creative, how it is sold, what is billed and what is optimized.
  • Locate the four points of a pricing product (ranked, billed, bid, judged) and say who estimates and who bears each segment between them under CPM, CPC, CPA, oCPC and oCPM.
  • Explain why CPA billing needs conversions the platform can verify, and why platforms selling oCPM or oCPC offer cost guarantees.
  • Trace $100 of advertiser spend through net and gross revenue recognition: the same $20 gross profit shows as an 80% or a 20% gross margin.
  • Decide whether to scale a budget by comparing the return on the additional spend with the break-even return, one over the contribution margin.

The gist. An ad product is a contract. It names the event the advertiser pays for and the result the advertiser wants, and these are usually different events. Between the impression the auction sells and the purchase the advertiser wants lie conversion rates that nobody knows exactly. Whoever has to pay when a rate turns out lower than expected carries that risk, and choosing a pricing product (per impression, per click, per purchase, or an “optimized” variant) is choosing who that is. The second half follows the money into the books: a platform's margin, an advertiser's average return and the return on its next dollar are three different numbers, and only the last one says whether to spend more.

An ad product is five separate choices

Search, feed, real-time bidding, CPC and performance advertising are not categories of one kind; each answers a different question. Search and feed are user contexts. Real-time bidding (RTB: a separate auction for each impression while the page or app loads) is a way of selling. CPC (cost per click) is a billing event. Performance advertising is an objective: the advertiser pays for, and judges results by, measurable actions such as installs or purchases, unlike brand advertising, which buys reach. Forcing these into one taxonomy produces the wrong objective function downstream. The landscape surveys the values each dimension takes; this chapter needs only the dimensions.

The contract must define every event it pays for or optimizes

“Optimize for purchases” is not yet a contract: each event and value needs an operational definition before a model can target it or an invoice can count it. Does a purchase mean an order placed, a payment completed, or an order still valid after the refund period? Is its value merchandise revenue, a commission or contribution profit? Changing one definition changes training labels, delivery and reports with no change to the model. A campaign setup is therefore an executable contract: conversion events, budget and flight dates (start and end of delivery), audiences, creatives, attribution rules, value postbacks (server-to-server messages that report each conversion and its value to the platform) and billing terms.

Definitions used later. ROAS (return on ad spend) is revenue credited to ads by the attribution rules divided by ad spend, often quoted as a percentage (300% means 3.0); iROAS (incremental ROAS) uses instead the revenue that would not have occurred without the ads. D7 ROAS counts a cohort's attributed revenue in the window [t_0,\ t_0+168\text{ h}), where t_0 is acquisition (for apps, the install), and is valid only once the window has closed for every user. LTV (lifetime value) must state its horizon, revenue or contribution, refunds, discounting, and whether acquisition cost is already subtracted; treating revenue LTV as profit overstates what you can afford to bid.

Who bears which risk: the four points

Every pricing product fixes four points on the path from impression to business result, and who bears which risk follows from where they sit. Between the points lie conversion rates nobody knows exactly, and the party that estimates a rate is not always the party that pays when the estimate is wrong.

Units: CPM (cost per mille) is a price per 1,000 impressions, CPC per click, CPI per install, CPA (cost per action) per contractually defined action such as a purchase. CTR is clicks per impression and CVR conversions per click; the platform's predictions of them are pCTR and pCVR. eCPM restates any per-event price per 1,000 impressions, \text{eCPM}=1000\times\text{amount}/\text{impressions}. In oCPM and oCPC (together oCPX) the advertiser states a target price per conversion, the platform turns it into a bid with its model, and billing stays per impression or per click.

The four points, in funnel order:

  1. Ranked: the event the auction sells and compares bids on, almost always the impression. Every bid is converted to eCPM before ranking.
  2. Billed: the event on the invoice.
  3. Bid: the event the advertiser attaches its price to. A performance advertiser values purchases; even under manual CPM or CPC it sets its bid by multiplying its price per purchase by one conversion rate of its own, so its bid point is effectively the purchase.
  4. Judged: what the advertiser's business is measured on, such as purchases, D7 ROAS or LTV.

Three rates connect them, rank→bill, bill→bid and bid→judged, and each estimate of them has an error. Three rules follow:

  • Rank→bill is the seller's risk. The platform must estimate this rate to rank in eCPM at all. If it bills at a later event, it gives away the impression and is paid only if the click or purchase follows, so it bears this error.
  • Bill→bid is the payer's risk, whoever estimates it. This rate converts a price per purchase into a price per billed event. Under CPM and CPC the advertiser estimates it implicitly, with one constant; under oCPX the platform estimates it with a model, impression by impression. Either way the advertiser pays per billed event, so the error moves its cost per purchase.
  • Bid→judged stays with the advertiser under every product, including the gap between credited and caused purchases from chapter 1.

Worked example

One campaign under five products

Given: the running example's forecast p_{\text{click}}=0.01, p_{\text{conv}}=0.05, so q=0.0005; the advertiser's price per purchase a=\$30. Every bid is set from the forecast.

  1. Bids, each ranked at an eCPM of $15. CPM: 1000\times0.0005\times\$30=\$15 CPM. CPC and oCPC: 0.05\times\$30=\$1.50 per click, ranked at 1000\times0.01\times\$1.50=\$15. CPA: $30 per purchase, ranked at 1000\times0.0005\times\$30=\$15. oCPM: 1000\times0.01\times0.05\times\$30=\$15 CPM. Under CPC the advertiser chose the 0.05; under oCPC and oCPM the platform's pCTR and pCVR supplied the rates.
  2. Scenario A, true CTR 0.005, CVR as forecast: per 1,000 impressions, 5 clicks and 5\times0.05=0.25 purchases. Bills: CPM and oCPM $15; CPC and oCPC 5\times\$1.50=\$7.50; CPA 0.25\times\$30=\$7.50.
  3. Scenario B, true CVR 0.025, CTR as forecast: 10 clicks and 10\times0.025=0.25 purchases. Bills: CPM, oCPM, CPC and oCPC $15 (10\times\$1.50 for the click products); CPA $7.50.
  4. Cost per purchase, bill ÷ 0.25: a $15 bill gives $60, a $7.50 bill $30.

Result: cost per purchase doubles to $60 exactly where the advertiser bears the mis-estimated rate: CPM and oCPM in both scenarios, CPC and oCPC in scenario B. Under CPA it stays $30; the platform absorbs the shortfall.

Check against the table: the CTR error (A) is rank→bill for CPC, oCPC and CPA, but bill→bid for CPM and oCPM. The CVR error (B) is rank→bill only for CPA, and bill→bid for the other four.

Equal expected bills are a modelling convenience: a party that bears a segment charges for it, so CPC and CPA prices carry a risk premium over the CPM equivalent.

CPA needs a billing event the platform can verify

Under CPA the conversion count sets the bill, so the platform must verify conversions itself or through a trusted third party. For apps that party is usually a mobile measurement partner (MMP, such as Adjust or AppsFlyer), which records installs and in-app events and attributes them to ads. To see why, suppose the advertiser reports only a fraction k of its conversions, 0<k<1.

  • Under CPA, under-reporting pays. The bill per impression becomes a\,k\,q, smaller by the factor k at once. Allocation follows the platform's \text{eCPM}=1000\,a\,\text{pCTR}\,\text{pCVR}; if pCVR is pooled across advertisers or updates with a lag, it does not fall, and the advertiser keeps the same impressions for less. With k=1/2 it pays $7.50 instead of $15 per 1,000 impressions while still ranked at $15.
  • Under oCPM, reports only affect allocation. The bill is the auction price per impression. Under-reporting scales pCVR by k, so the eCPM falls by k unless the target rises from a to a/k: \begin{aligned}1000\,a\,\text{pCTR}\,(k\,\text{pCVR})&=k\cdot\text{eCPM},\\ 1000\,\tfrac{a}{k}\,\text{pCTR}\,(k\,\text{pCVR})&=\text{eCPM}.\end{aligned} The restored eCPM wins the same impressions at the same price under either auction rule, so the bill is unchanged and under-reporting buys nothing.

The CPA gain lasts only while allocation ignores the reports. With an instant, advertiser-specific model, restoring allocation needs the price a/k, and the bill is back to (a/k)\,k\,q=a\,q.

Why oCPX platforms offer cost guarantees

Under oCPX the platform makes the bill→bid estimate but the advertiser pays for its errors, so several platforms selling oCPX promise to compensate cost per conversion that exceeds the target by more than a stated tolerance. In scenario B an oCPC advertiser with a $30 target paid $60 per purchase. Much of such overrun comes from selection, not bias: the auction buys the impressions where the estimate is highest, so even an unbiased model overpredicts on the impressions it wins (the winner's curse, chapter 5). Realized cost per conversion runs above target on average, and the platform must correct its estimates on won traffic or pay out.

Dual bids limit variance while the model learns

A new campaign's conversion model has little data, so its bids are noisy. A dual bid states two prices: one for a frequent shallow event, such as an install, whose rate the platform already estimates well, and one for the rare deep event, such as a purchase. The platform buys an impression only if both targets look met, so the shallow bid caps how far a noisy deep-event estimate can push the impression bid. The cost is volume: the rule also rejects traffic that misses the shallow target but would meet the deep one, so once deep-event data accumulate, a single deep-event bid usually buys more.

Platform margin, advertiser return and scaling are different questions

A platform can hold an 80% gross margin while its advertiser loses money on every additional dollar. Below, one $100 budget is entered in two income statements, and then we ask what scaling does to each side. Amounts are for one period, “spend” is what the advertiser is billed, and refunds, credits and taxes are ignored.

1. What is the denominator of an 80% gross margin?

Four accounting terms first.

  • Take rate: the amount the platform keeps after paying the publisher, divided by total advertiser spend.
  • Gross vs net revenue recognition: a platform that recognizes revenue gross books the full advertiser spend as revenue and the publisher payout as a cost; one that recognizes revenue net books only the amount it keeps.
  • Cost of revenue: costs directly incurred to deliver the service, such as serving, data and payment processing. Under gross recognition it also includes the publisher payout, called TAC (traffic acquisition cost).
  • Gross profit = recognized revenue − cost of revenue. Gross margin = gross profit ÷ recognized revenue.
Worked example

$100 of spend under net and gross recognition

Given, for one period: advertiser spend S=\$100; publisher payout P=\$75; the platform's direct service costs D=\$5, all classified as cost of revenue. Compare two platforms with identical cash flows, one that qualifies for net recognition and one that qualifies for gross recognition.

  1. Take rate, a cash-flow ratio identical for both: (S-P)/S=(\$100-\$75)/\$100=25\%.
  2. Net recognition: revenue =S-P=\$25; cost of revenue =D=\$5; gross profit =\$25-\$5=\$20; gross margin =\$20/\$25=80\%. The $75 payout appears in neither line.
  3. Gross recognition: revenue =S=\$100; cost of revenue =P+D=\$75+\$5=\$80 (the $75 is TAC); gross profit =\$100-\$80=\$20; gross margin =\$20/\$100=20\%.
  4. Gross profit per dollar of advertiser spend, under both: \$20/\$100=20\%.

Result: same cash, same $20 gross profit; reported gross margin is 80% under net recognition and 20% under gross.

Check: the two margins share the numerator and their denominators are S-P and S, so gross margin (gross) = take rate × gross margin (net) =0.25\times0.80=0.20.

The 80% does not mean the platform keeps 80% of the ad budget, nor that 80% is profit; R&D, sales, taxes and the cost of capital still come out. Before comparing two ad companies' gross margins, align their revenue recognition. Here 80% versus 20% reflects no difference in efficiency at all.

Which treatment applies is set by accounting standards, not chosen by the platform.

Optional: who may recognize revenue gross

Calling oneself an “agent” in a contract does not decide it. Under IFRS 15 and its US counterpart ASC 606, a platform that controls the ad placement before it passes to the advertiser is a principal and recognizes the gross amount; otherwise it is an agent and recognizes only its net fee. Indicators of control are primary responsibility for delivery, inventory risk (for example, buying impressions before any advertiser order) and discretion over price. 2. Why does the advertiser's profit fall while its sales rise?

Switch to the advertiser's ledger. Its revenue is what consumers pay it for goods; it has nothing to do with the $25 the platform recognizes. Let m be the advertiser's contribution margin: the fraction of each incremental sales dollar left after goods, fulfillment and other variable costs, before ad spend and fixed costs. Take m=40\%, constant across volume. “Profit” below means incremental contribution profit after ad spend, incremental sales × m − ad spend, and each column's revenue is assumed to be truly incremental.

The first $100 generates $120 of contribution and earns $20 after paying for itself. The additional $50 generates only $30 of contribution and loses $20 on its own. Sales rise from $300 to $375 while profit falls from $20 to zero.

3. Average iROAS hides the loss on the additional budget

Let C be ad spend and R the incremental revenue it generates. Contribution profit is mR-C, which for C>0 and m>0 is non-negative exactly when R/C\ge 1/m. So the break-even iROAS is 1/m: 2.5 at m=40\%. The decision to add spend \Delta C, which brings additional incremental revenue \Delta R, applies the same test to the increment: \Delta R/\Delta C, the marginal iROAS, must reach 1/m.

Worked example

Should the advertiser add the extra $50?

Given: m=0.4; incremental revenue R=\$300 at C=\$100 and R=\$375 at C=\$150, same window and revenue definition.

  1. Break-even iROAS: 1/m=1/0.4=2.5.
  2. Marginal iROAS of the increment: \Delta R/\Delta C=(\$375-\$300)/(\$150-\$100)=\$75/\$50=1.5.
  3. Profit change from the increment: \Delta\Pi=m\,\Delta R-\Delta C=0.4\times\$75-\$50=-\$20.
  4. Average iROAS of the whole budget: \$375/\$150=2.5=1/m, so contribution profit at $150 is 0.4\times\$375-\$150=\$0.

Result: do not add the $50. It lowers contribution profit by $20, although the cumulative iROAS of 2.5 still meets the break-even threshold.

Check: profit at $100 is 0.4\times\$300-\$100=\$20 and at $150 is $0, a change of −$20. Equivalently, the increment needed \Delta R\ge2.5\times\$50=\$125 and delivered $75.

The cumulative 2.5 sits at break-even only because the early high-return spend subsidizes the late low-return spend. The trading analogue: a scaled-up strategy's marginal capacity may not cover its costs while the book's average return hides the loss.

Optional: where is the optimal budget? A fitted response curve

Write incremental revenue as a function of spend, R(C), with R(0)=0, and hold m fixed. Contribution profit is \Pi(C)=m\,R(C)-C, so a finite increase \Delta C pays iff m\,[R(C+\Delta C)-R(C)]>\Delta C, and an infinitesimal one iff m\,R'(C)>1. Let R be differentiable and concave, with no other constraint binding. If m\,R'(0)\le1, not advertising is optimal. If m\,R'(0)>1 and m\,R' eventually falls below 1, the optimum is interior and satisfies m\,R'(C^*)=1.

The three points R(0)=0, R(100)=300, R(150)=375 prove that the $150 plan is worse than the $100 plan, but not that $100 is optimal. Concavity gives a bracket: the chord slopes 3 on [0,100] and 1.5 on [100,150] give R'(0)\ge3>2.5 and R'(150)\le1.5<2.5, so 0<C^*<150. Since R'(100) can lie anywhere in [1.5,3], the data cannot say whether C^* is above or below $100. Pinning it down requires a functional form.

Worked example

An assumed concave curve through the three points

Given: assume R(C)=\alpha C-\beta C^2 (dollars), the simplest concave form with R(0)=0, fitted through R(100)=\$300 and R(150)=\$375; m=0.4. This is an illustration, not an estimate.

  1. Fit: 100\alpha-10{,}000\beta=300 and 150\alpha-22{,}500\beta=375. Subtracting 1.5 times the first from the second gives -7{,}500\beta=-75, so \beta=0.01 and \alpha=4: R(C)=4C-0.01C^2, increasing for C<\$200.
  2. Marginal and average iROAS: R'(C)=4-0.02C; R(C)/C=4-0.01C. The marginal falls twice as fast as the average.
  3. Optimum: m\,R'(C^*)=1\Rightarrow 4-0.02C^*=2.5\Rightarrow C^*=\$75. Then R(75)=\$300-\$56.25=\$243.75 and \Pi(75)=0.4\times\$243.75-\$75=\$22.50.
  4. Average iROAS reaches 1/m where 4-0.01C=2.5, i.e. at C=\$150, exactly where \Pi=0.

Result: under this curve the optimal budget is $75, with profit $22.50; the $100 plan earns $20 and the $150 plan $0. The average iROAS at the optimum is 3.25, so a “healthy” average says nothing about whether you are at the optimum.

Check: for a quadratic the chord slope equals the derivative at the interval midpoint, so the chord on [100,150] is R'(125)=4-2.5=1.5, matching the previous example; and C^*=75 lies inside the bracket (0,150).

4. Platform gross profit can grow while customer profit falls

Now combine the two ledgers. Assume the platform always keeps 25% of the budget, its direct service costs are always 5% of the budget, and it recognizes revenue net.

MetricAd budget $100Ad budget $150
Platform net revenue$25$37.50
Platform direct service costs$5$7.50
Platform gross profit$20$30
Platform gross margin80%80%
Advertiser contribution profit$20$0

Scaling up raises platform gross profit by $10 and cuts the advertiser's profit by $20. Customer retention and competition constrain a platform from exploiting this, but platform revenue growth, a high gross margin and customer profit growth do not automatically move together. An objective such as “maximize sales subject to cumulative iROAS ≥ 2.5” picks the $150 plan, which meets the constraint exactly and has higher sales; maximizing advertiser profit picks the $100 plan. An algorithm can execute its objective flawlessly and still produce a commercially bad outcome.

5. The strongest assumption: revenue must be truly incremental

“The platform attributes the order to the ad” and “the order would not have happened without the ad” are different claims. Suppose attributed revenue is A=\$300 but the revenue the ads caused is only R=\$200. Then the first $100 of spend already loses money: 0.4\times\$200-\$100=-\$20. In general, write \kappa=R/A for caused ÷ attributed revenue. Profit is mR-C=m\kappa A-C, so for m,\kappa>0 attributed ROAS must reach

\frac{A}{C}\ge\frac{1}{m\kappa}=\frac{1}{0.4\times2/3}=3.75,

above the reported 3.0. The ratio \kappa comes from a randomized experiment (chapter 6), not from the attribution report, and it can exceed 1 when attribution misses caused sales. To judge a platform such as Axon (chapter 8), ask: under the same measurement definition, margin and budget, how much does it raise the customer's incremental contribution profit, and how much of that survives as the budget grows? The platform's gross margin cannot answer it.
