From prediction to bidding and budgets

Let money flow through the three ledgers

First, put yourself in the ad network's position: you buy display opportunities from publishers and then charge advertisers. The money you collect, the money you pay, and the money the advertiser earns from selling goods are three different things. Below, two external examples first settle the first two, and then the advertiser's ledger is added.

Step 1 · For every 1,000 impressions, who pays how much?

Epom's example is straightforward: the ad network buys inventory from the publisher at $2 CPM and sells it to advertisers at $5 CPM, keeping a $3 spread. Here both buyer and seller are charged per impression. Source: Epom, the “Ad arbitrage” section of the article

The network's account first receives $5, then pays the publisher $2; the remaining $3 is the procurement spread, before deducting servers, sales, and other costs. This example also does not tell us whether the $5 the advertiser paid brought enough business.

Step 2 · What if the advertiser is only willing to pay per conversion?

Kevel's official documentation gives another set of figures: 9,000 impressions, 18 billable events, $5 charged per event. The original allows the billable event to be a click or another action; this section chooses the CPA case and interprets the event as a contractually recognized conversion. Source: Kevel, the CPC/CPA worked example under “What Is eCPM?”

QuestionCalculationAnswer
How much does the advertiser pay in total?18 conversions × $5$90
How much is collected per 1,000 impressions on average?$90 ÷ 9,000 × 1,000$10 eCPM

The contract is still $5 per conversion. The $10 eCPM merely converts revenue that has already occurred into “revenue per thousand impressions,” making it easy to compare with the impression purchase price; it does not change the contract to CPM, nor does it guarantee that the next batch of impressions will earn as much.

Step 3 · Connect “collecting per conversion” with “paying per impression”

The following is a combined worked example added by this tutorial, not the same transaction described in either original article: Keep Kevel's 9,000 impressions, 18 conversions, and $5 CPA; further assume this batch of impressions was bought at the $2 CPM from the Epom example.

Whose ledgerHow it's calculatedAmount
Advertiser's ad bill18 conversions × $5 CPAPays $90
Publisher's ad revenue9,000 impressions ÷ 1,000 × $2 CPMReceives $18
Network's procurement spread$90 collected from advertiser − $18 paid to publisherKeeps $72

This is “buy per impression, sell per conversion.” The network commits to paying for impressions, but only receives the advertiser's money when contractual conversions occur. If the impressions bought for the same $18 ultimately produce only 2 conversions, the network collects only $10 and loses $8. The commercial use of conversion prediction is to help the network judge which impressions are worth buying; prediction itself cannot guarantee that conversions happen.

Step 4 · The network made $72—did the advertiser make money?

We can't tell yet. All we know above is that the advertiser spent $90; we still lack how much contribution each incremental order leaves, and how many of the 18 recorded conversions were truly generated by the ad.

Add one explicit teaching assumption: each incremental order leaves $8 of contribution after deducting goods, fulfillment, and other variable costs, before ad spend. Keep the bill and procurement cost above unchanged:

Assumed scenarioAdvertiser's calculationPost-advertising incremental contribution
All 18 are truly incremental18 × $8 − $90+$54
Only 9 are truly incremental9 × $8 − $90−$18

In both scenarios, the network collects $90 for 18 contractual conversions, pays the publisher $18, and keeps $72. Yet the advertiser's economic outcomes are opposite. The number of attributed conversions used for settlement cannot be taken directly as the number of truly incremental conversions. The latter requires additional causal measurement, developed in the next chapter.

At this point the three ledgers are separated: the publisher looks at revenue from selling impressions; the network looks at collections minus procurement cost; the advertiser looks at incremental order contribution minus the ad bill. Only now comes the bidding question: for future impressions not yet bought, how much should the network bid, and how much volume should it buy?

Advanced: bidding calculator and full derivation (optional)

Advanced derivation: the calculator's default values

Let's clarify the source first: these numbers were set by this tutorial and should not be taken as a case from the original paper. The relevant original derivation is Zhang and Wang's “Statistical Arbitrage Mining for Display Advertising,” Section 3.2.1 (hereafter SAM; PDF page 4, printed page 1468): it studies how to bid when competitive prices are uniformly distributed, but does not provide the complete three-party ledger below. Below we build an independent teaching model that can be recomputed line by line.

Reading order: who pays whom → first try a $6 bid → why change to $6.3246 → does the advertiser actually make money. The fixed tables use default parameters and do not change with the calculator's sliders; the interactive calculator comes after the derivation.

1. Sign two contracts first, then talk algorithms

Suppose there is a merchant selling goods, an app that displays ads, and an ad network connecting the two. The “platform” here is this ad network; to see the money flows clearly, other intermediaries and fees are not listed separately for now.

RoleWhat it doesHow money is settled
AdvertiserSells goods and wants ads to bring more ordersPays the platform $30 per order for each contractually recognized order
PublisherOwns ad slots in the app and sells opportunities to display adsAuctions opportunities to the platform and collects media procurement payments
Ad network / platformBuys display opportunities from publishers and delivers ads for advertisersCollects from the advertiser and pays the publisher; it bears the profit or loss on the difference

Contract A: advertiser → platform, billed per order. A “conversion” is the behavior the advertiser cares about; in this example it means only placing an order. “Attribution” is crediting a given order to a given ad touch according to agreed rules. A “billable attributed conversion” is an order that passes contractual checks and can be billed; this text assumes each such order is settled exactly once in full, with no duplicate charges.

This kind of contract is called CPA (cost per action, billing for a specified action): in this example the specified action is placing an order, and the settlement unit price is denoted a = $30 / order. This is an agreed payment price, a different agreement from “the system tries to keep the average acquisition cost at some target.” An order being attributed to an ad does not by itself prove that the order was generated by the ad; Step 5 calculates this.

Contract B: platform → publisher, buying impressions via per-opportunity bidding. An “ad opportunity” is a position and moment available for displaying an ad; a “bid request” is the message by which the publisher invites the platform to bid on this opportunity. A “win” means the platform won this auction; a “successful impression” means the ad actually completed the display event agreed in this example. Loading can still fail after a win, so these two events must be kept separate.

The publisher uses CPM (cost per mille, a price unit per thousand impressions) to express bids. For example, $6 CPM corresponds to $6 ÷ 1,000 = $0.006 per impression. The auction can still sell just one impression opportunity at a time; it does not buy 1,000 at once. The default contract is a first-price auction: after winning, the platform pays its own bid; and this example assumes by default that every win results in a successful impression.

Bid request → platform bids → win → successful impression
→ user may place an order → billable order confirmed per attribution rules
Publisher payment computed per the procurement contract; advertiser bill computed separately per the order contract.

Why does a $6 CPM bid win 30% of opportunities?

There are two separate questions: what price must we beat in this auction? and how often will our chosen bid beat it across many auctions? The first defines the competitive threshold; the second requires a probability model.

1. One auction: compare our bid with the highest competing bid

Assume each opportunity has at least one competing bid. Let Z be the highest competing bid for that opportunity. A floor price is a seller’s minimum accepted price; this model sets no additional floor. It is the competitive threshold. We choose our own bid b. For competing bids of $3, $5, and $9 CPM, the threshold is $9 CPM: our $6 CPM bid loses, while our $10 CPM bid wins. Our bid does not enter the maximum defining Z.

Z = max{competing bids}
Win if b ≥ Z; lose if b < Z

Here max means the largest value in the set. We use the ≥ convention for ties; in the continuous model below an exact tie has probability zero, so that convention does not change the result. In real auctions with discrete price increments, tie rules can matter. The threshold determines whether we win, not necessarily what we pay: in the default first-price contract a winner pays its own bid.

2. Many auctions: model the unknown threshold as a random variable

Different opportunities face different competitors and bids, so Z varies from auction to auction. The platform does not know the next value in advance. For this teaching model, assume Z is uniformly distributed between zero and an upper bound L. This means equal-length price intervals have equal probability. It is an assumption about the highest competing bid, not a claim that every individual competitor bids uniformly.

We write this as Z ~ Uniform(0,L): the symbol ~ means “has the following distribution.” A uniform distribution assigns an interval its length divided by the full range. On a $0–20 CPM range, each $2-wide interval has probability 2/20 = 10%; the intervals $0–2 and $12–14 are equally likely.

3. The win event is exactly the part of the range below our bid

For a fixed bid b, we win precisely when Z falls between 0 and b. That winning interval has length b; the whole interval has length L. Let F(b) denote the cumulative distribution function evaluated at b: the probability that the random threshold is at most that value. Here P(…) means the probability of the event in parentheses.

F(b) = P(Z ≤ b)
= winning interval length / total interval length
= (b − 0) / (L − 0)
= b / L, for 0 ≤ b ≤ L

The denominator is the entire price range, not our bid. Bidding $6 CPM wins against thresholds in $0–6 CPM, whose length is 6; it loses against thresholds in $6–20 CPM, whose length is 14. The probabilities are therefore 6/20 = 30% and 14/20 = 70%.

$0$5$10$15$20 CPM

Win: thresholds $0–6.0 CPM → 6 / 20 = 30%. Lose: thresholds above $6.0 CPM → 70%.

This explorer lets you choose a bid to see its win probability. It is separate from the later calculator, which chooses a bid to maximize platform spread under a budget.

At the boundaries, F(0) = 0 and F(L) = 1. More generally, F(b) = 0 below zero, b/L within the range, and 1 above L. Bidding above L buys no additional win probability in this model.

4. CPM and per-unit prices give the same probability

All prices in a comparison must use the same unit. The bid b, threshold Z, and upper bound L use dollars per billable unit internally. Their CPM quotes are each 1,000 times the corresponding per-unit price. The subscript CPM below labels the quoted price, not a different economic quantity.

bCPM = 1,000b; LCPM = 1,000L
b = $6 / 1,000 = $0.006 per unit
L = $20 / 1,000 = $0.02 per unit
b / L = 0.006 / 0.02
= 6 / 20 = 0.30

The factor of 1,000 cancels in the ratio. Dividing a $6 CPM quote by $0.02 per unit would mix units and give a meaningless answer. A probability is unitless and must lie between 0 and 1.

The billing contract determines what the unit charges for: a win under win-based billing, or a successful display under display-based billing. At the default 100% display-success rate these counts coincide. Changing whether failed displays are charged changes procurement cost; it does not by itself change the win event Z ≤ b.

5. From win probability to an expected number of wins

Let N be the number of opportunities and W the number won. With the same bid and the same marginal threshold distribution for every opportunity, the expected number of wins is the sum of the individual win probabilities. E[W] denotes that average count over repeated runs.

E[W] = N × F(b)
= 1,000,000 × 0.30
= 300,000 wins on average

This expectation does not guarantee exactly 30% wins in any finite run. Independence is not required to add the expectations, although dependence affects how much the realized count fluctuates. With default display success of 100%, those wins also become 300,000 expected impressions.

Optional: the probability-density derivation, and what changes outside this model

The probability density f(z) describes probability per unit of price near the threshold value z. The lowercase z is a possible value of the random variable Z. Uniformity makes the density constant at f(z) = 1/L within the range and zero outside it. The total area is L × (1/L) = 1. Density is not the probability of one exact price; each exact value has probability zero in this continuous distribution.

F(b) = ∫0b f(z) dz
= ∫0b (1/L) dz
= b/L, for 0 ≤ b ≤ L

The integral adds the density over the winning interval. In a nonuniform market, the win probability is still F(b) = P(Z ≤ b), but it need not equal b/L. For example, if 80% of thresholds are below $6 CPM, that bid wins 80%, not 30%. A floor price, discrete bids, different opportunity types, or competitors reacting to our strategy also requires a different model. Even uniformly distributed individual competing bids do not generally produce a uniform maximum.

One more intermediate quantity is needed: the expected revenue to the platform per successful impression, denoted v. It is derived from Contract A and is not another input:

v = q × a
= 0.0005 × $30 = $0.015 / impression

“Expected” refers to the average outcome of repeated delivery under the same conditions. An order can only be a whole number, but the predicted average number of orders can be fractional. $0.015 / impression is equivalent to $15 per thousand impressions, called the platform revenue's eCPM (effective CPM, revenue converted to a per-thousand-impression basis); it merely converts per-order collections into a comparable unit, and the contract is still billed per order.

Finally, fix one simplification: q is the same for all opportunities and does not vary with the competitive threshold or the bid. Otherwise, once a higher bid buys a different set of people, the same conversion probability can no longer be used.

4. Why does the calculator change to $6.3246 CPM?

Only now do we pose the optimization problem: the platform chooses a single uniform bid to maximize its media gross spread, while keeping media procurement cost no more than $2,000. “Optimal” means optimal under this objective; the advertiser's profit has not yet entered the platform's objective function.

Impressions D(b) = N × (b / L)

Raising the bid increases both the win rate and the payment per win, so cost grows with the square of b. Ignoring the budget for now, set the derivative of the gross spread with respect to the bid to zero:

M′(b) = (N / L)(v − 2 b) = 0

b0 denotes the optimal bid when the budget is ignored. The gross spread is a downward-opening quadratic, so this is a maximum; but it requires $2,812.50 of media payments, while the budget is only $2,000. A budget-feasible bid must also satisfy:

C(b) = Nb2 / L ≤ B
b ≤ √(BL / N)
= √(2000 × 0.02 / 1,000,000)
= $0.006324555… / impression

Denote the final bid as b*. It must simultaneously satisfy three conditions—“not yet past the peak of the gross spread,” “within budget,” and “bid not exceeding the competitive threshold's upper bound”—so take the minimum of the three:

b* = min{v / 2, √(BL / N), L}
= min{0.0075, 0.006324555…, 0.02}
= $6.3246 CPM (displayed value)

In the interface, “budget binding” means the budget constraint restricts the choice: the platform would have bid $7.50 but can only bid up to about $6.3246. It does not mean the advertiser also spent only $2,000. The budget is an expected-spend constraint, and also does not guarantee that a randomly realized day never overspends; actual per-impression stop-delivery control is not part of this worked example.

Conversely, raise the budget to $6,000 and the platform still stops at $7.50 CPM, spending only $2,812.50: the objective is to maximize gross spread, and exhausting the budget is not an independent goal.

For the same opportunity, we cannot observe both the shown and not-shown outcomes; this example directly assumes this causal difference is known, whereas in reality it requires evidence such as controlled experiments and cannot be obtained from attribution reports alone.

For “impressions × Δq” to hold, we further assume that opportunities do not affect each other, that there is no cumulative effect from repeated exposure, and that the incremental probability is unrelated to the competitive threshold, the bid, and display failures. In reality, Δq and the attribution probability q are not naturally in a subset relationship either; there is no assumption here that Δq must be less than q.

Extension A: why does the formula change when switching to “ideal second-price”?

An ideal second-price auction means paying the competitive threshold Z upon winning, rather than one's own bid b; floor prices, bid increments, and fees are ignored here. For example, if you bid $6 CPM and the highest competing bid is $4 CPM: first-price pays $6 CPM, and ideal second-price pays $4 CPM. Both win, but the actual payments differ.

With the same bid b held fixed, the win rate is still b / L. But among the opportunities already won, Z is uniformly distributed between 0 and b, so the average payment per win is b / 2. With the default ρ = 1:

Media cost C(b) = N × (b / L) × (b / 2)
= Nb2 / (2 L)
Platform gross spread M(b) = (N / L)(vb − b2 / 2)
M′(b) = (N / L)(v − b)

So, ignoring the budget, the best bid becomes b0 = v. Then apply the media budget and the price range:

b* = min{v, √(2 BL / N), L}
= $8.9443 CPM

With the same $2,000 media budget, the win rate is now about 44.72%, the advertiser bill $6,708.20, the platform gross spread $4,708.20, and the advertiser's incremental contribution profit −$3,130.50. Buying more impressions also means billing for more attributed orders; under the current negative-contribution parameters, the advertiser loses even more.

The comparison holds the competitive threshold distribution and other inputs fixed, not the advertiser bill. In a real market, other buyers may also change their bids after the auction rules change, so this is not a prediction of overall market change.

Extension B: are failed displays billed? First compute a 50% success rate, then look at the full formula

ρ is the previously defined “impression success rate after winning.” Changing it from 100% to 50% means that, on average, only 50 of every 100 wins result in a successful impression. Revenue comes only from orders after successful impressions; procurement cost depends on the publisher contract.

First fix a first-price bid of $6 CPM without re-optimizing. It still wins 300,000 times, but only 150,000 impressions succeed, and platform collections are 150,000 × 0.0005 × $30 = $2,250.

Publisher contractHow cost is calculatedPlatform gross spread
Billed only on successful impression150,000 × $0.006 = $900$2,250 − $900 = $1,350
Billed upon winning300,000 × $0.006 = $1,800$2,250 − $1,800 = $450

“Billed upon winning” makes failed displays incur media cost too, so the platform becomes more cautious when re-optimizing. Both contracts are merely teaching settings for this calculator.

To write all four combinations in a single formula, introduce two notational devices, which are not additional business parameters to estimate:

  • α (alpha), the media billing coefficient: the probability that each win is billed by the publisher. When billed only on successful impression, α = ρ; when billed upon winning, α = 1.
  • k, the auction payment coefficient: 1 for first-price and 2 for ideal second-price, so that the average price per billed event is uniformly written as b / k. It is not a fee rate or take percentage.

Continue to assume that whether a display succeeds is unrelated to the competitive threshold. Then:

Successful impressions D = N × (b / L) × ρ
Media cost C = N × (b / L) × α × (b / k)
Advertiser bill A = Dqa = Dv
Platform gross spread M = (N / L)(ρvb − αb2 / k)

Setting the derivative of M with respect to b to zero gives the budget-unconstrained bid b0 = kρv / (2α); then using C ≤ B to solve for the budget-feasible bid cap yields the calculator's complete formula:

b* = min{L, kρv / (2α), √(kBL / (Nα))}
Advertiser incremental contribution profit = D(Δqg − qa)

In the calculator, ρ ranges over 50%–100%, so α is nonzero. Setting ρ to 50% while keeping the $2,000 budget and first-price auction, after re-optimizing: with billing only on successful impression, the bid is $7.50 CPM and media payment $1,406.25; with billing upon winning, the bid is $3.75 CPM and media payment $703.125. These are a different problem from the comparison table above with the fixed $6 bid.

Source comparison: what comes from the original paper, and what was added by this tutorial?

SAM original paper, Section 3.2.1, Equations 15–20 discusses uniform competitive prices and optimal bidding. In the paper, r (reward per conversion), θ (conversion probability), and l (price upper bound) correspond respectively to a, q, L here. These are the paper's own symbols and need not be carried into the calculator above.

One distinction cannot be omitted: Equation 2 in Section 3 of the paper, in a second-price auction context, uses the bid as an upper bound on procurement cost; this tutorial explicitly adopts a first-price auction by default, where a win pays one's own bid. Extension A separately computes the actual expected payment under ideal second-price. They must not be treated as the same model merely because the expressions look similar.

“Display Advertising with Real-Time Bidding and Behavioural Targeting,” Section 5.4 introduces this type of optimization problem across billing methods. The single advertiser, fixed conversion probability, specific numbers, display-failure contracts, and the true incrementality and advertiser contribution ledger here are all assumptions added by this tutorial to explain the mechanism, not experimental results from the literature.

This model can show how objectives diverge through contracts. It does not simulate differences in value across users, nor the drop in conversion probability after scaling; so it cannot be used directly to judge a real platform's fee rates, profitability, or scaling limits.

First define the event the model is to predict

Let x be the information available at bid time; S is the successful-impression indicator, taking 1 for a successful impression and 0 otherwise. K is the click indicator, taking 1 for a click and 0 otherwise; Y is the indicator of a qualifying target conversion within the agreed observation window, taking 1 if it occurs and 0 otherwise. This example settles on post-click attributed purchases, so a billable event requires K=1 and Y=1. q(x) is the billable conversion probability conditional on a successful impression:

q(x) = P(K=1, Y=1 ∣ x, S=1)
= P(K=1 ∣ x, S=1)
× P(Y=1 ∣ K=1, x, S=1)

This is the chain rule of conditional probability and does not require clicks and conversions to be independent. This example settles only the “click and convert” branch; if the contract also counts view-through conversions, the qualifying branch for K=0 must be added. CTR is the click-through rate per impression, and pCTR is the model's predicted value of that probability. CVR is the conversion rate and pCVR the predicted conversion probability, but the denominator must be declared: P(Y=1 | K=1,x,S=1) is computed per click, q(x) per successful impression. If the model already predicts the conversion probability per impression, you must not multiply by CTR again.

When connecting to the bidding model below, we further assume that, given x and a successful impression, the response probability no longer changes with the bid b or the competitive threshold Z. Opportunities with failed displays produce no conversion revenue in this example; so request-level revenue must also be multiplied by the win rate and the post-win impression success rate, and you cannot treat q(x) as a request-level conversion probability.

For a network that truly bills per billable conversion, v(x)=q(x)×settlement CPA is the expected revenue per impression. In this example it is 0.015 dollars. For the advertiser, predicting order contribution profit should use the expected contribution amount after conversion; predicting incremental value must further answer “what would happen if this ad were not shown.” Response prediction, value prediction, and causal incrementality prediction have different targets.

Model metrics in this chapter: Positives are samples in which the target event occurred; negatives are samples in which it did not. AUC (Area Under the Curve) here refers to a binary ranking metric: the probability that a randomly drawn positive scores higher than a randomly drawn negative, with ties counted as half; it does not directly measure whether probability values are accurate. Probability calibration means that the group of samples predicted at a given probability has a correspondingly matching actual frequency once labels mature.

log loss is used to evaluate probabilistic predictions. For a binary label y (1 if it occurs, 0 otherwise) and model probability p (0 < p < 1), the per-sample loss is −[y ln p + (1−y) ln(1−p)], where ln is the natural logarithm; averaged over a fixed evaluation set, lower is better. The score still depends on the evaluation sample, labels, and weighting definitions, and cannot directly substitute for delivery profit.

What each layer of the model pipeline solves

Candidate retrieval first answers “which ads are worth further computation,” ranking then estimates response and value for a smaller set, and finally valuations, budgets, eligibility, and auction rules are turned into decisions. If retrieval misses high-value ads, the precise models downstream cannot recover them; improving only the ranking AUC may never touch the system's main bottleneck. Meta's publicly described Andromeda (2024) depicts staged retrieval followed by ranking; this is a historical industrial example, not an assertion about all platforms or AXON's current architecture. Meta 2024 Andromeda

Ad features typically come from the advertiser and campaign, creatives, ad slot, user or context, historical interactions, and time. The key is not how long the feature names are, but whether a feature is available at decision time, authorized for use, stable in coverage, and retains the same meaning in training and online. New ads and new creatives lack historical feedback, so transferable content and context information must be used, while leaving a controlled budget for exploration; otherwise historical winners keep accumulating labels and new candidates never get a chance to prove themselves.

For post-click conversion, ESMM (Entire Space Multi-Task Model) jointly trains using the chain relationship among impressions, clicks, and conversions. It offers one design for the problems introduced by training CVR only on clicked samples, but it does not automatically solve cross-policy selection bias, delayed feedback, or causal identification. Original ESMM paper

Model acceptance should attend to ranking, probability calibration, segment stability, and business outcomes all at once. AUC measures one property of ranking; delivery, however, often needs absolute probabilities, because value is multiplied by probability. If all predicted probabilities are uniformly doubled and still do not exceed 1, ranking and AUC are unchanged; if linear value bidding with a fixed coefficient is also used, with no cap or other truncation, bids will double as well. Normal overall calibration does not guarantee that slices such as high-priced publishers, new users, or a particular country are normal. Google's 2013 industrial paper already treated sampling, calibration, and confidence as system problems; it cannot prove that these problems disappear when switching to a larger model. First-price and second-price costs differ

The following is an original simplified decision model, assuming by default that a win always results in a successful impression. Let Z≥0 be the competitive threshold determined by other bids: the price the bidder must meet or beat to win (ties have probability zero in this continuous model), b≥0 one's own bid, and v≥0 the expected monetary value created by winning and being shown. For fixed information x, F(b|x)=P(Z≤b|x) is the conditional cumulative distribution, i.e., the win rate at bid b; f(z|x) is its continuous density, f=∂F/∂z. Below, after fixing x, the conditioning is omitted, written as F(b) and f(b); in integrals, z is a value of the competitive price, dF(z)=f(z)dz.

Bids, values, and payments are all expressed in dollars per won and displayed opportunity (display success is 100% in this baseline), multiplied by 1,000 when sent to a CPM interface. Assume risk neutrality, i.e., decisions based on expected net profit; given x, value no longer depends on Z; a single bid does not change subsequent responses; there are no extra fees, ties, or intertemporal feedback. No budget for now. Let π₁(b), π₂(b) be the unconditional expected net profit per request under first-price and second-price respectively, with both revenue and cost zero on a loss; a prime denotes the derivative with respect to the bid. Expressions containing F/f are used only for interior candidate solutions where f(b)>0, and the allowed bid range, boundaries, and global optimum must still be checked.

In a first-price auction, the winner pays its own bid. The expected net profit per opportunity is:

\pi_1(b)=(v-b)F(b),\qquad \pi_1'(b)=(v-b)f(b)-F(b).

Hence the interior optimal bid satisfies:

b=v-\frac{F(b)}{f(b)}.

This is an implicit equation, not “discounting by a fixed proportion.” So-called bid shading trades off more winning opportunities against higher payments; a lower bid not only saves money but also loses some opportunities.

In an ideal second-price auction, the winner pays the competitive threshold. The expected net profit per opportunity is:

\pi_2(b)=\int_0^b(v-z)\,dF(z),\qquad \pi_2'(b)=(v-b)f(b).

Under the above assumptions, if bidding that price is allowed, b=v is an optimal choice; if there is no competitive-price probability mass near that point, the optimal bid may not be unique. This does not mean all ad systems should bid truthfully. GSP (Generalized Second Price auction) involves multiple ad slots, ranking, and position values; mechanisms with quality scores must also be modeled according to their specific rules. Real transactions also have floor prices, fees, discrete bids, and internal auctions. Google Ad Exchange announced its move to first-price as early as 2019, which is enough to show that older second-price textbooks cannot serve as an operating manual for all current markets. Google first-price migration announcement

The no-budget strategy in the unified worked example

Let the competitive threshold Z be uniformly distributed between 0 and L=0.02 dollars, independent of this group's response probability. For 0≤b≤L, the win rate is F(b)=b/L. This example's network value is v=0.015, the first-price net profit is (v−b)b/L, and the interior optimum is b=v/2=0.0075 dollars, i.e., a bid of 7.5 dollars CPM.

For 1 million requests, the expected number of wins is 375,000; attributed conversions 187.5; the network collects 5,625 dollars from the advertiser; pays the publisher 2,812.50 dollars; and the gross spread between the two is 2,812.50 dollars. The gross spread is before infrastructure, channel fees, operations, bad debt, and cost of capital, and cannot be called final profit directly.

More noteworthy is the advertiser's ledger. Computed on attributed conversions, 187.5×40−5,625=1,875 dollars, which looks profitable. Computed on the per-impression causal increment directly assumed in this example, expected incremental orders are 375,000×0.0002=75, and incremental contribution minus ad spend is 75×40−5,625=−2,625 dollars. An intermediary making money and the customer actually losing money can perfectly well happen at the same time. If this divergence persists, it will eventually affect customer budgets, renewals, and pricing power.

The two kinds of budget objectives must be written out separately

In this section, i indexes bid requests within the planning period; bᵢ is the bid for the i th request, vᵢ is the expected value after winning and being shown, Fᵢ is that request's competitive-threshold cumulative distribution, Cᵢ(bᵢ) is the expected media cost per request including zero spend on losses, and B is the total media budget for the planning period. Summation runs over all requests; max means choosing bids to maximize the objective, and s.t. means the following constraints must be satisfied. Here C(b) is a per-request cost, distinct from the cumulative cost C in the optional calculator; cumulative cost is summed over requests.

Let C(b) be the unconditional expected media cost per request, including zero spend on losses. For first-price it is bF(b), and for ideal second-price it is the integral ∫₀ᵇ z dF(z). If the objective is to maximize total value within the budget, the problem is:

\max_{b_i}\sum_i v_iF_i(b_i) \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_i C_i(b_i)\le B.

After adding the budget constraint to the objective with a multiplier, the per-request term is vF(b)−λC(b), with the request subscript omitted. λ≥0 is the budget multiplier for the total-value objective; the “shadow price” is the increment in the optimal objective from one additional unit of budget, when the corresponding optimality and differentiability conditions hold. In this section both value and cost are in dollars, and the multiplier represents value dollars / budget dollars. For interior candidate solutions with λ>0 and f(b)>0:

\text{Second-price}\quad b=\frac{v}{\lambda}, \qquad \text{First-price}\quad b=\frac{v}{\lambda}-\frac{F(b)}{f(b)}.

If the objective is instead to maximize net profit under the same budget, the problem becomes max Σ[v_iF_i(b_i)−C_i(b_i)]. Its per-request term is vF(b)−(1+μ)C(b), where μ≥0 is the budget multiplier for the net-profit objective; 1 corresponds to the cost itself, and μ to the additional cost of budget scarcity. Under the same interior-solution conditions:

\text{Second-price}\quad b=\frac{v}{1+\mu}, \qquad \text{First-price}\quad b=\frac{v}{1+\mu}-\frac{F(b)}{f(b)}.

This is not a symbolic game. When the budget is slack, the net-profit objective takes μ=0, returning to the no-budget profit optimum; a pure total-value objective with a slack budget has no comparable motive to economize—it buys every positive-value opportunity it can—and there is no legitimate step of “setting λ=0 and still computing with the division formula.” Budget constraints, ROAS constraints, and CPA constraints are also not the same thing; swapping multipliers between different papers often yields strategies that look similar on the surface but have different economic meanings.

The above is an expected-optimization baseline under a known distribution. When the distribution is unknown it must also be learned; when only partial win/loss information is observed, it becomes yet another class of problem. Modern first-price budget research explicitly distinguishes feedback conditions, and its theoretical guarantees cannot be unconditionally transferred to production markets. How a two-thousand-dollar media budget changes the bid

Here we revert to the homogeneous teaching parameters: N=1,000,000 is the number of requests, L=0.02 dollars / impression is the upper bound of the uniform competitive threshold, v=0.015 dollars / successful impression is the network's expected revenue, and B=2,000 dollars is the network media budget. The billable conversion probability per impression is q=0.0005, and the settlement unit price per order is $30; by default a win means a successful impression. The objective is to maximize the expected value of network collections minus media cost.

In this example, the first-price no-budget optimum would spend 2,812.50 dollars, exceeding the 2,000-dollar limit. For one million homogeneous requests, when the budget binds we have Nb²/L=B, hence b=√(BL/N)=0.006324555 dollars. Second-price cost is b²/(2L), and under the same budget the bid is √(2BL/N)=0.008944272 dollars.

You cannot use 2,000 dollars as the denominator of this advertiser's ROAS. When the table switches auction format, it artificially keeps the competitive threshold distribution unchanged, only to compare payment functions; real participants would change their bids, and the table is not a prediction of equilibrium returns under the two mechanisms.

For this homogeneous group, the two kinds of objectives can yield the same bid. More precisely, within the same feasible region, if the optimal solutions of both objectives exhaust the same budget B, then on that budget boundary ΣCᵢ=B the two objective functions differ only by the constant B, so the optimal choice on that boundary is the same, even if opportunities are heterogeneous. No claim is made here that no other tied optimal solutions exist off the budget boundary. Universal equivalence cannot be claimed from this: a net-profit objective may deliberately spend less, while a pure total-value objective would still want to keep buying; and if the feasible region or constraint definitions change, the problem must be re-solved. The budget above is also an expected-spend constraint, while actual requests and payments fluctuate randomly; how to hold a per-path hard cap is a separate responsibility of the system.

Budget pacing is online control, not spreading money evenly

Pacing refers to controlling the rhythm of spend over time. The simplest illustrative update is:

\mu_{t+1}=\max\{0,\mu_t+\eta(c_t-s_t)\}.

Here t indexes control periods; μ_t≥0 is the budget multiplier used in period t, not the bid. The bid is computed from it: for example, b=v/(1+μ_t) under ideal second-price bidding. c_t is actual spend and s_t target spend in that period. The step size η>0, in units of 1/dollar, converts the spend deviation into a multiplier adjustment; max{0,…} keeps the multiplier nonnegative. Spending too fast raises the multiplier and lowers bids; spending too slowly does the reverse. This illustrates the control direction, not a stability guarantee. Delayed billing, traffic cycles, and interacting feedback loops can cause oscillation. Advertising feedback-control research

The target trajectory should also not always spend evenly through the day. If evening opportunities are more valuable, budget should be held back earlier in the day for the evening; if high-value supply is scarce, failing to spend the full budget is not necessarily a malfunction. You need to distinguish “under-delivery due to system failure despite profitable opportunities” from “stopping because no opportunities satisfy the economic constraints.” Using bid multipliers to guarantee full spend can quietly turn value maximization into budget-consumption maximization.

Multi-campaign allocation adds further dimensions on top of these constraints: which advertiser gets a given opportunity, how much quota each campaign has left, whether there are guaranteed-delivery obligations, and whether the platform charges by service fee, impression, or conversion. Statistical arbitrage research links CPA revenue with CPM procurement cost, but still bears user-response, attribution, and payment risk; “arbitrage” does not mean locking in risk-free profit. Why price feedback cannot be used directly as a supervised label

In an ideal second-price setting, let Z be the competitive threshold, b the logged bid, and x the information at decision time; F(z|x) and f(z|x) are respectively the conditional cumulative distribution and density of the threshold. Assume that on a win Z is visible, while on a loss we only know Z>b. Winning-price samples are selected by the policy, and training an ordinary regression directly on winning prices misses the high-price tail. Let ℓ denote the log-likelihood, win, loss the sets of winning and losing samples respectively, and log the natural logarithm; each summand uses that request's own Z, b, x. The censored log-likelihood for conditionally independent samples is:

\ell= \sum_{\mathrm{win}}\log f(Z\mid x) +\sum_{\mathrm{loss}}\log\bigl(1-F(b\mid x)\bigr).

It requires that wins and losses are truly determined by the price condition, and that once the price-related policy history used for bidding is included in the logged x, the bid carries no additional threshold information: this can be written as P(Z≤z | x,b)=F(z|x); if the policy used unlogged, price-related information, this observation model must be extended. Timeouts, creative rejections, budget filtering, and users leaving the page cannot all be lumped into “market price higher than the bid.” The price distribution chapter of the original monograph offers an entry point for understanding the censoring problem. RTB monograph, Chapter 3

In first-price, one's own payment b is not the competitive threshold; on a win it is an upper bound on that threshold. If only win/loss feedback is available, on a win one knows only Z≤b, and on a loss one knows Z>b; you can model the win probability, but you cannot label your own bid directly as the competitors' price. A specific exchange may provide more signals, and the actual interface must be the reference.
