Attribution, experiments, and data

The delivery policy determines what training data looks like

Ad logs are not a passive sample of the world. Every stage of delivery filters what you later get to see. Eligibility filtering (targeting, budget, frequency-cap and content-policy rules) decides which ads may compete for an opportunity. Retrieval (cheap candidate generation; see Chapter 3) decides which candidates get scored. Bidding decides which opportunities are bought. Rendering success and privacy mechanisms decide which outcomes are observed. The labels you train on are therefore conditioned on the logging policy's own choices. A conversion-rate (CVR) model fitted on won impressions estimates P(Y=1 \mid X, \text{shown by the old policy}), not P(Y=1 \mid X) on traffic the old policy never bought. This is selection bias, and no model, however clever, can recover conversion labels for actions that were never taken.

Exploration buys two things: knowledge of regions the current policy avoids, and support overlap, meaning that every action a new policy might choose also had nonzero probability of being taken by the logging policy. It costs real money and user experience. If the logging policy is stochastic and you record the probability of each action it took, you can evaluate a new policy offline with inverse propensity scoring (IPS). Let x_i be the context of logged opportunity i (user, placement, time), a_i the action taken (which ad, what bid), r_i the observed reward, \mu(a \mid x) the logging policy and \pi(a \mid x) the policy to evaluate. The IPS estimate of the new policy's value V(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_x\,\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}[r] is

\hat V_{\text{IPS}}(\pi)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\pi(a_i\mid x_i)}{\mu(a_i\mid x_i)}\,r_i .

It is unbiased under four assumptions. (1) Support: \mu(a\mid x)>0 wherever \pi(a\mid x)>0. (2) The logged \mu(a_i\mid x_i) is the probability actually used, not a reconstruction. (3) The reward is defined and observed the same way under both policies (same conversion event, attribution window and postback mechanism). (4) The new policy does not change the context distribution or the environment; in an auction this fails when the new policy's bids move clearing prices or competitors' behavior. Small logging probabilities produce large weights and high variance; clipping the weight at some M, i.e. using \min\{M,\pi/\mu\}, cuts variance at the cost of bias. Offline replay reweights outcomes you logged; it cannot manufacture counterfactuals you never logged. Swaminathan and Joachims, counterfactual risk minimization

Negative downsampling also shifts absolute probabilities. Fix a feature value, so the label is binary: a positive is a sample where the target event occurred, a negative one where it did not. Let p be the event probability in the population before sampling, s (0<s\le 1) the probability that a negative is retained, and p_s the event probability among retained samples, which is what a model trained on the sampled data without weights learns. If every positive is kept and each negative is kept independently with the same probability s, then p_s = p/(p+s(1-p)), so

p=\frac{s\,p_s}{1-p_s+s\,p_s},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \frac{p}{1-p}=s\cdot\frac{p_s}{1-p_s}.

In logit terms the correction is a constant shift of \ln s. For example, with s=0.1 and p_s=0.5 the true probability is 0.05/0.55=1/11\approx 0.0909, not 50%. Apply the correction once. If training already weighted each retained negative by 1/s, the model is already calibrated to p and correcting again is wrong. If the retention rate depends on features, you need the per-sample retention probability; a single constant no longer works.

Delayed labels and time leakage

Clicks usually precede purchases, and purchases precede long-run contribution profit. An impression that has not converted yet is not a permanent negative, but waiting for every label to mature costs the model freshness. A production pipeline therefore needs four explicit choices: an observation window (how long after the impression or click a conversion still counts), maturity cohorts (groups of events old enough that their labels are, say, 95% final), a delay model (an estimate of P(\text{conversion arrives after } t \mid \text{eventual conversion}) used to correct immature labels), and a backfill policy (how late-arriving labels overwrite earlier negatives). Compare experiment arms only at equal label maturity; an arm launched two days later will look worse simply because its conversions have not arrived. Several papers address this freshness-versus-maturity tension in streaming CVR training. Delayed feedback and elapsed-time sampling

Each training example must contain only information that was available at decision time. Account state after the purchase, conversion counters already incremented by this purchase, and attribution outcomes decided later must not leak into the features. Correct table-update timestamps are not enough for a correct point-in-time join: you must track event time, processing time and the feature-pipeline version separately. A model that wins offline through leakage does not degrade slightly online; it loses the very inputs its predictions depended on.

Attribution allocates credit; experiments identify causation

Attribution is a bookkeeping rule that assigns each conversion to at most one ad touchpoint (a click or an impression), usually because billing or optimization needs a single owner. The common rule is last-touch: credit goes to the last qualifying click within a lookback window before the conversion. A click-through window bounds the gap between click and conversion; a view-through window, usually much shorter, lets an impression with no click claim the conversion when no qualifying click exists. Typical defaults for app installs are 7 days for clicks and 24 hours for views, but contracts and measurement settings vary. For mobile apps the matching is done by an MMP (Mobile Measurement Partner; see Chapter 2): the advertiser's app embeds the MMP's SDK, which reports installs and in-app events; ad networks send the MMP their click and impression claims; the MMP picks the winner under the configured rules and sends postbacks (server-to-server notifications of the attributed event) to the winning network.

Incrementality is a different question: how do business outcomes with the advertising intervention compare with outcomes without it? The two can disagree in both directions. A real conversion can be credited to the wrong touchpoint, and a correctly credited conversion may have happened anyway. Last-touch and view-through rules systematically reward ads placed close to purchases users were already going to make: retargeting existing customers, or spraying cheap impressions to collect view-through credit.

Field evidence shows that rich observational data does not reliably recover the effects that randomized experiments identify. Observational models are not useless, but predictive accuracy does not supply the identifying assumptions a causal claim needs. For example, observational IPS for the effect of exposure D with propensity e(x)=P(D=1\mid X=x) is \hat\tau=\frac1n\sum_i\big[D_iY_i/e(x_i)-(1-D_i)Y_i/(1-e(x_i))\big]. It is unbiased only if, conditional on the logged features, exposure is independent of the potential outcomes (no unmeasured confounding). Ad targeting optimizes exposure toward likely buyers on signals the analyst often cannot see, so that assumption is exactly what fails. Gordon et al., observational methods versus large Facebook experiments

The next example is self-contained; it is not the network campaign from Chapter 1, so that network's media budget does not enter the advertiser's return calculation. Randomly split one eligible population into a treatment group, where the campaign may deliver, and a control group (a holdout), where it may not, with 100,000 people each. Over the same matured window the treatment group makes 1,200 purchases and the control group 1,000. Ad spend is $5,000, all incurred in the treatment group, and the attribution system credits 500 purchases to the campaign. Net revenue per purchase is $50, meaning sales revenue after refunds and other adjustments but before cost of goods, fulfillment and other variable costs. The pre-advertising contribution rate is 40%: the share of net revenue left after those non-advertising variable costs, so each purchase contributes $20 before ad spend. ROAS (return on ad spend) is revenue divided by ad spend; attributed ROAS uses attributed revenue, and iROAS (incremental ROAS) uses the revenue the campaign actually caused. Assume randomization and event definitions are correct, the window has matured, and there is no interference: one group's treatment does not change the other group's purchases.

Quantity Formula Point estimate
Attributed ROAS 500\times\$50\div\$5{,}000 5
Incremental purchases (per 100,000 assigned to treatment) 1{,}200-1{,}000 200
iROAS 200\times\$50\div\$5{,}000 2
Break-even iROAS 1\div 40\% 2.5
Incremental contribution after ad spend 200\times\$50\times 40\%-\$5{,}000 −$1,000

A 5× attributed ROAS can coexist with a loss. Since the campaign caused only 200 extra purchases but was credited with 500, at least 300 of the credited purchases would have happened without it (assuming the ad never suppresses purchases). Break-even needs \$5{,}000/\$20=250 incremental purchases.

These are point estimates from one sample, and the precision depends on the randomization unit and the outcome distribution. As an illustration only: if each user buys at most once and users are independent, the standard error of the difference in purchase counts is \sqrt{1200(1-0.012)+1000(1-0.010)}\approx 46.6. The lift of 200 is about 4.3 standard errors from zero, but its 95% interval is roughly [109, 291] purchases. That maps to iROAS in [1.09, 2.91] and incremental contribution in roughly [-\$2{,}830, +\$830]. So the experiment establishes that the campaign causes purchases, but not that it loses money. Repeat purchases, heavy-tailed order values, or cluster randomization widen these intervals. If the two groups differ in size, compare per-person outcomes first, then state the delivery scale you are extrapolating to.

Assignment to "eligible to see the ad" is not exposure: many treatment users never win an auction slot, never open the app, or scroll past. Use potential-outcome notation. For user i, Z_i\in\{0,1\} is the randomized assignment, D_i(z)\in\{0,1\} is whether the user would actually be exposed under assignment z, and Y_i(z) is the outcome under assignment z. Under the exclusion restriction below, write Y_i(d) for the outcome under exposure d. Because the control group cannot be served the campaign, D_i(0)=0 for everyone (one-sided noncompliance). Let c=P(D=1\mid Z=1) be the exposure rate in the treatment group.

ITT (intent-to-treat effect) \tau_{\text{ITT}}=\mathbb{E}[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)], effect of assignment over everyone randomized \bar Y_{Z=1}-\bar Y_{Z=0} Randomization; no interference between users (SUTVA)

Analyze ITT first. Filtering the treatment group down to exposed users and comparing them with all of control reintroduces exactly the selection that randomization removed. Switching the denominator alone identifies nothing; the TOT rescaling is valid only under the stated assumptions. In the example, if a hypothetical 40% of treatment users were exposed, \tau_{\text{ITT}}=200/100{,}000=0.002 purchases per assigned user and \tau_{\text{TOT}}=0.002/0.4=0.005 per exposed user. Both give the same 200 incremental purchases in total (100{,}000\times0.002=40{,}000\times0.005), so iROAS is unchanged. TOT adds precision and interpretability, not extra profit.

The exclusion restriction needs care in an auction. Withholding the campaign from a control user does not leave the slot empty; the next-best ad wins. The estimand is therefore the effect relative to the ad environment the user would otherwise see, not relative to no ad at all. Ghost ads (Johnson, Lewis and Nubbemeyer, 2017) estimate TOT more precisely. For control users the ad server runs the auction with the campaign included, logs a "ghost impression" whenever the campaign would have won and rendered, then serves the runner-up instead. This observes D_i(1) in both groups, so you can compare exposed treatment users with ghost-exposed control users directly. It requires the ghost logic to replicate the real delivery decision exactly, with identical code paths in both groups. The older placebo design, which shows control users a public-service ad (PSA), fails under optimized delivery: the optimizer delivers the PSA to different users than it would deliver the real ad, so the two exposed groups are not comparable.

Model experiments and channel incrementality experiments are not the same question

A model A/B experiment randomizes traffic between two delivery policies and compares them. A channel incrementality experiment compares advertising with not advertising, with reduced spend, or with different budget levels. The first can show that B beats A; it cannot by itself show that the channel generates positive incremental profit relative to not spending at all. Geo experiments, which randomize geographic regions instead of users, also come in several designs (go dark, heavy-up, budget levels), so choose the question first and the design second. Google GeoX experiment types

Before launch, fix the primary metric, the minimum detectable effect (MDE: the smallest true effect the design detects with a prespecified power at a prespecified false-positive rate), the duration, the label-maturity period, guardrail metrics (metrics that must not degrade, such as latency, user retention or publisher revenue) and the stopping rule. Several mechanisms couple the arms. A shared budget makes them compete for one quota. Bids from one arm move the prices the other arm pays. Cross-device and cross-region behavior leaks users across assignment boundaries. Whether you randomize by user, region or time block, document how each of these interferences is handled. Deciding to stop whenever a peek shows significance inflates the actual false-positive rate unless the test is designed for sequential monitoring.

In the running example the network optimizes its gross spread (collections minus media procurement cost; see Chapter 3), while the advertiser cares about incremental contribution. Report both. Otherwise a policy can raise the network's short-term revenue by chasing conversions that are easy to attribute but not incremental, and leave the resulting customer churn to the sales team.

Privacy and anti-fraud change observability

On iOS, two separate Apple mechanisms matter. ATT (App Tracking Transparency, iOS 14.5, 2021) requires an app to obtain user permission through a system prompt before it tracks the user across other companies' apps and websites or reads the IDFA (Identifier for Advertisers, the per-device ad identifier that previously enabled deterministic user-level attribution). SKAdNetwork (SKAN), and its successor AdAttributionKit (iOS 17.4), are Apple's privacy-preserving attribution APIs: the device itself decides which ad gets credit for an install and sends the ad network a delayed, aggregated postback with no user identifier. ATT and these attribution APIs are not synonyms. Apple's documentation states that using AdAttributionKit alone does not require the ATT prompt, while tracking as Apple defines it, or reading the IDFA, does. Crowd-anonymity thresholds control which postback fields are populated: with too few installs from a campaign, fields such as the fine-grained conversion value (an app-set integer from 0 to 63 encoding post-install activity) or parts of the source identifier come back coarse or empty. Postbacks are cryptographically signed and carry transaction identifiers so receivers can reject forgeries and replays. Apple ad attribution

The system consequences follow directly. A missing user-level postback cannot be labeled "not converted." Aggregated postbacks cannot be split back into individual users without additional assumptions. A signed event proves authenticity, not incrementality. And missingness is not random, because it depends on consent rates and campaign size. A new platform that subsidizes impression purchases has bought delivery opportunities only. It does not automatically obtain training labels that can be joined across customers and used under long-term consent.

Examine fraud layer by layer, because each layer is a separate attack surface. Is the supply identity real? Was the impression actually rendered? Was the click made by a human? Is the conversion valid? Was attribution hijacked, for example by click injection, where malware fires a fake click just before an install to win last-touch credit? Was the purchase later refunded? Supply-chain transparency standards address only the first layer. ads.txt (app-ads.txt for apps) is a file the publisher hosts on its own domain listing which ad systems are authorized to sell its inventory. sellers.json is published by each SSP (supply-side platform; see Chapter 2) or exchange and lists the sellers it pays. The SupplyChain object in an OpenRTB bid request (OpenRTB is the IAB's standard bid-request protocol) lists every hop the request passed through. None of these guarantees traffic quality or economic incrementality. IAB ads.txt, IAB sellers.json and SupplyChain FAQ

The trading analogy applies: positive expected value per trade does not guarantee survival. Correlated fake events, retroactive changes to attribution windows, chargebacks, and a payment-timing mismatch, where the network pays publishers before advertisers pay it, all concentrate risk. A fraud model is one layer. You also need per-source exposure limits, settlement reconciliation, contractual clawback rights, and gradual ramp-up of new sources.
