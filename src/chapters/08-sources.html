Sources, errata, and glossary

An erratum in the RTB monograph’s second-price example

The monograph “Display Advertising with Real-Time Bidding (RTB) and Behavioural Targeting” (arXiv v2: 122 pages, version dated July 15, 2017; the PDF title page says July 18) is a sound end-to-end account of RTB. One worked example in Section 5.3.4, however, gets its budget equation wrong: it counts the win probability twice. This section re-derives the example and quantifies the error. arXiv metadata

Symbols

Notation follows the “Prediction and bidding” chapter. Where the monograph uses a different symbol, it is given in brackets.

  • Z: the competitive threshold, the highest competing bid for one bid request. We win if our bid is at least Z [monograph: market price z].
  • L>0: the upper bound of Z. We assume Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) [monograph: l].
  • b\ge 0: our bid for one request. Z, L and b are all in dollars per impression (multiply by 1,000 for CPM).
  • F(b)=P(Z\le b): the win probability at bid b, and f=F' its density [monograph: w(b) and p_z].
  • \mathbf{1}\{Z\le b\}: the win indicator, 1 on a win and 0 on a loss. E[\cdot] is expectation over Z, and also over r where an integral over r is written.
  • r\in[0,1]: the predicted click-through rate (pCTR) of the request, i.e. the probability that a won impression is clicked. Here r is a probability, not money. (In the SAM paper cited in the “Prediction and bidding” chapter, r is the reward per conversion; that chapter renames it a.)
  • b(r): the bidding function, which maps a request’s pCTR to a bid.
  • N: the number of bid requests the campaign receives over its lifetime [monograph: T]. B: the total media budget in dollars [monograph: B].
  • C(b)=E[Z\,\mathbf{1}\{Z\le b\}]: the expected payment per request, counting zero on a loss, as in the “Prediction and bidding” chapter. c(b) is the monograph’s cost function; its Eq. 5.5 defines it as the expected payment given a win, c(b)=E[Z\mid Z\le b], so C(b)=c(b)\,F(b).
  • \lambda>0: the Lagrange multiplier on the budget [monograph: \lambda]. The objective here counts clicks, so \lambda is in clicks per dollar and 1/\lambda is in dollars per click.

Model and correct solution

Assumptions (the monograph’s special case): an ideal second-price auction, so the winner pays Z, with no floor price, fees or bid increments; every win yields one billable impression; r\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1), independent of Z; and a risk-neutral buyer. For 0\le b\le L:

F(b)=\frac{b}{L},\qquad c(b)=E[Z\mid Z\le b]=\frac{b}{2},\qquad C(b)=E[Z\,\mathbf{1}\{Z\le b\}]=\int_0^b \frac{z}{L}\,dz=\frac{b^2}{2L}.

The objective is to maximize expected clicks subject to expected spend equal to the budget (the monograph’s Eq. 5.11):

\max_{b(\cdot)}\; N\int_0^1 r\,F\bigl(b(r)\bigr)\,dr \quad\text{s.t.}\quad N\int_0^1 C\bigl(b(r)\bigr)\,dr=B.

The Lagrangian separates across r. Maximizing rF(b)-\lambda C(b) pointwise gives the first-order condition (r-\lambda b)\,f(b)=0, so b(r)=r/\lambda as long as r/\lambda\le L. This is the monograph’s Eq. 5.32, and it is the second-price rule b=v/\lambda from the “Prediction and bidding” chapter with value v=r measured in clicks. A linear bid is therefore derived here, not assumed. Expected clicks equal N/(3\lambda L), which rise as \lambda falls, so the budget binds and the equality constraint is justified. Substituting into the constraint:

N\int_0^1 \frac{(r/\lambda)^2}{2L}\,dr=\frac{N}{6\lambda^2L}=B \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{\lambda^*}=\sqrt{\frac{6LB}{N}},\qquad b^*(r)=r\sqrt{\frac{6LB}{N}}.

The closed form holds only when no bid exceeds L, i.e. 1/\lambda^*\le L, which is equivalent to B/N\le L/6. For L/6<B/N<L/2 the optimal bid is \min(r/\lambda,L), and \lambda solves a different budget equation. For B/N\ge L/2 the budget never binds, because bidding L on every request costs E[Z]=L/2 per request.

Where the monograph goes wrong

The monograph’s budget constraint (Eqs. 5.11 and 5.33) is N\int c(b(r))\,F(b(r))\,dr=B. That is correct, because Eq. 5.5 defines c as the conditional payment, so cF=C. But Eq. 5.35 (PDF page 58) evaluates c(b) as \int_0^b z/L\,dz=b^2/(2L), without dividing by F(b). That expression is C(b), not c(b). Eq. 5.36 (page 59) then multiplies it by F(b)=b/L again, so the per-request cost becomes b^3/(2L^2). With b=r/\lambda:

\int_0^1\frac{(r/\lambda)^3}{2L^2}\,dr=\frac{1}{8\lambda^3L^2}=\frac{B}{N} \;\Longrightarrow\; \lambda_{\mathrm{paper}}=\frac12\left(\frac{N}{BL^2}\right)^{1/3},\qquad \frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{paper}}}=2\left(\frac{L^2B}{N}\right)^{1/3}.

These are the monograph’s Eqs. 5.37 and 5.38 (subscript “paper” marks the published value). The linear form b=r/\lambda is unaffected; only the coefficient is wrong. The monograph’s first-price result in the same section, b(r)=r\sqrt{3LB/N} (Eq. 5.29), uses the per-request cost bF(b)=b^2/L correctly, so the slip is confined to the second-price case. Size of the error

At the published coefficient, the true expected spend per request is (1/\lambda_{\mathrm{paper}})^2/(6L). Dividing by the budget per request gives the overspend factor

\frac{(1/\lambda_{\mathrm{paper}})^2/(6L)}{B/N}=\frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{LN}{B}\right)^{1/3}.

The published formula keeps every bid at or below L exactly when B/N\le L/8, and on that whole range the factor is at least \tfrac23\cdot 8^{1/3}=\tfrac43. Wherever the formula applies, it overspends by at least 33%, and the overspend grows as the budget tightens. Boundary effects cannot explain the gap.

Check with the “Prediction and bidding” chapter’s numbers: L=\$0.02 per impression ($20 CPM), N=1{,}000{,}000 requests and B=\$2{,}000, so B/N=\$0.002\le L/8=\$0.0025. The correct coefficient is \sqrt{6\times0.02\times0.002}=\$0.015492 per click: a request with pCTR r gets a bid of r\times\$15.49 CPM, and expected spend is exactly $2,000. The published coefficient is 2(0.02^2\times0.002)^{1/3}=\$0.018566 per click, and its true expected spend is $2,872.58, or 1.436 times the budget. With the unitless values L=1, B/N=0.01, the coefficients are 0.244949 and 0.430887 and the overspend factor is 3.094. The main text derives its budget results independently and does not reuse this example.

Where to read next

For the full RTB picture, read Chapters 2, 3, 5 and 7 of the RTB monograph. For how industrial click prediction defines and calibrates probabilities, read Google’s 2013 click-prediction paper. To build a real integration, work from OpenRTB and your counterparties’ contracts. To judge whether advertising creates value for customers, read the randomized advertising experiments. In every paper, record the explicit assumptions; they matter more than the model names.

Glossary

This is the reader’s canonical glossary, in alphabetical order. Definitions give the sense used in this reader; individual contracts and platforms may define a term differently, and the contract wins.

Term Definition
A/B testA randomized experiment that assigns users (or requests) to treatment or control so that the difference in outcomes estimates a causal effect.
Ad exchangeA marketplace that receives bid requests from sellers, runs the auction among buyers and sets the clearing price.
Ad groupThe level below a campaign that carries finer targeting, bids or creative combinations; the hierarchy differs across platforms.
Ad loadHow many ads are shown per session or per unit of content; raising it trades user retention for short-term revenue.
Ad networkAn intermediary that aggregates publishers’ inventory and sells it to advertisers, often buying in one pricing unit (e.g. CPM) and selling in another (e.g. CPA) and keeping the spread.
Ad opportunityOne chance to show one ad to one user in one slot at one moment; also called an impression opportunity.
Ad requestAn app’s or page’s request for an ad to fill one slot; mediation or an SSP may fan one ad request out into many bid requests.
Ad serverThe system that selects which ad to show for a request, delivers it and logs the result.
AdAttributionKitApple’s privacy-preserving attribution framework (iOS 17.4 and later), successor to SKAdNetwork; it reports conversions in delayed, crowd-anonymized postbacks without user-level identifiers.
ads.txt / app-ads.txtA file the publisher hosts on its own domain listing the ad systems authorized to sell its inventory (app-ads.txt is the app variant).
AdvertiserThe buyer: the business that pays for impressions, clicks or actions in order to reach users.
ATTApp Tracking Transparency: Apple’s iOS permission prompt, required before an app tracks a user across other companies’ apps and websites or reads the IDFA.
AttributionRule-based assignment of credit for a conversion to earlier ad interactions (commonly last-touch); it is an accounting rule, not a causal estimate.
Attribution windowThe maximum delay between an ad interaction and a conversion for the conversion to be credited; click-through and view-through windows are set separately. Also called a lookback window.
AxonAppLovin’s ad prediction and bidding engine.
BidThe price a buyer offers for one opportunity; most interfaces quote it in CPM.
Bid requestThe message, usually in OpenRTB format, by which a seller invites buyers to bid on one ad opportunity before a response deadline.
Bid shadingBidding below one’s value in a first-price auction, trading win probability for a lower payment; at an interior optimum b=v-F(b)/f(b).
Billable eventThe event that triggers payment under a given contract: a win, a rendered impression, a click, an install or a qualifying action.
burlOpenRTB billing notice URL: the exchange calls it when the impression becomes billable under the exchange’s policy.
CalibrationThe property that predicted probabilities match observed frequencies (among requests with pCTR 2%, about 2% are clicked); bidding needs it because bids multiply value by probability.
CampaignAn advertiser’s unit of budget, objective, schedule and targeting.
Censoring (winning prices)Partial observation of the competitive threshold: on a win in a second-price auction it is observed, on a loss we know only that it exceeded our bid, so regression on wins alone is biased.
Clearing priceThe price the auction winner actually pays.
Competitive thresholdThe highest competing bid for an opportunity (or the floor, if higher): the bid needed to win, written Z. The RTB monograph calls it the market price.
ContributionIn this reader, pre-advertising contribution per order: sales revenue minus cost of goods, fulfillment and other variable non-advertising costs.
ConversionThe advertiser’s target action after an ad, such as an install, purchase or registration.
CPACost per action: the price paid per agreed conversion; as a metric, spend divided by conversions.
CPCCost per click: the price paid per click; as a metric, spend divided by clicks.
CPICost per install: the price paid per attributed app install.
CPMCost per mille: the price per 1,000 impressions. A $6 CPM bid is $0.006 for one impression; auctions still sell one impression at a time.
CTR / pCTRClick-through rate: clicks divided by impressions. pCTR is a model’s predicted probability that one impression is clicked.
CVR / pCVRConversion rate; the denominator (per click, per impression or per install) must be stated. pCVR is the model’s predicted value.
Deal typesDirect deal (negotiated by hand, fixed price and volume); programmatic guaranteed (fixed price, reserved volume, automated execution); preferred deal (fixed price, first look, no volume commitment); private auction (invitation-only); open auction (any qualified buyer).
Delayed feedbackConversions arriving hours to days after the impression, so recent training examples carry incomplete labels: a negative may still convert.
Demand sourceAny buyer that can fill an ad request: an ad network, a DSP or a direct campaign.
DSPDemand-side platform: software that bids on impressions across exchanges on behalf of advertisers.
eCPMEffective CPM: revenue or value per 1,000 impressions, whatever the pricing unit (e.g. CPC × CTR × 1,000); it puts bids priced in different units on one scale.
Fill rateThe share of ad requests that receive an ad.
First-price auctionAn auction in which the winner pays its own bid.
Floor priceThe seller’s minimum acceptable price for an opportunity; bids below it lose. Also called a reserve price.
Ghost adsAn experiment design that logs, for control users, the impressions the campaign would have won, so the effect on users who would actually be exposed can be estimated without placebo ads.
Gross spreadA term defined in this reader: an ad network’s collections from advertisers minus its media procurement cost.
Gross vs net revenueRevenue recognition: a platform that controls the service before it reaches the customer (a principal under IFRS 15 and ASC 606) books the full advertiser spend as revenue (gross); an agent books only its fee (net).
GSPGeneralized second-price auction for several ranked slots, as in search ads: each winner pays roughly the minimum bid needed to keep its position. Bidding one’s value is not an equilibrium in general.
Guaranteed deliveryA contract that commits the seller to deliver a fixed volume of qualifying impressions, with a remedy for shortfall.
HoldoutA randomly selected group withheld from ads (or from a campaign or feature) to serve as the control when measuring incrementality.
IABInteractive Advertising Bureau, the industry body whose Tech Lab publishes OpenRTB, ads.txt and sellers.json.
IDFAIdentifier for Advertisers: the per-device iOS advertising identifier, readable only after ATT consent.
ImpressionOne rendered display of an ad. A “successful impression” here means the display event the contract counts actually completed; a won auction can still fail to render.
IncrementalityThe change in an outcome caused by showing ads, measured against a randomized counterfactual; also called lift. Contrast with attribution.
InterstitialA full-screen ad shown at a natural break, such as between game levels.
IPSInverse propensity scoring: an off-policy estimator that reweights each logged reward by \pi(a\mid x)/\mu(a\mid x), the ratio of the new policy’s to the logging policy’s action probability.
iROASIncremental ROAS: revenue caused by the ads (treatment minus control) divided by ad spend.
ITTIntent-to-treat effect: the average effect of being assigned to treatment, whether or not the user was actually exposed.
LTVLifetime value: the value a user generates over a stated horizon. State whether it is revenue or contribution, the horizon, the discounting and whether acquisition cost is already subtracted.
lurlOpenRTB loss notice URL: the exchange calls it when the bid loses, optionally with a loss-reason code.
MAXAppLovin’s mediation platform for app publishers.
MediationSoftware in a publisher’s app that decides which of several demand sources fills each ad request, by a bidding auction, a waterfall or both.
MMPMobile measurement partner (e.g. Adjust, AppsFlyer): a third party whose SDK records installs and in-app events and attributes them to ad networks.
nurlOpenRTB win notice URL: the exchange calls it when the bid wins. On its own it does not mean the ad rendered or is billable.
OpenRTBThe IAB Tech Lab protocol for bid requests, bid responses and notices; this reader cites version 2.6.
PacingOnline control of the spend rate over the budget period, e.g. by adjusting a bid multiplier toward a target spend trajectory; it works alongside, and does not replace, a hard budget cap.
PostbackA server-to-server message that reports a conversion, and possibly its value, to the ad network or MMP.
ProgrammaticBuying and selling ad opportunities through automated systems, one opportunity at a time.
PSAPublic-service ad: a placebo ad shown to control users in older experiment designs; biased when an optimizer chooses who sees ads.
PublisherThe owner of the app or website whose ad slots are sold.
Retrieval and rankingThe stages of ad selection: retrieval picks a small candidate set worth scoring; ranking then estimates response probabilities and value for each candidate.
Rewarded videoA video ad the user opts into in exchange for an in-app reward.
ROASReturn on ad spend: attributed revenue divided by ad spend. It ignores cost of goods and does not measure incrementality.
RPMRevenue per 1,000 ad requests (also called request eCPM); if each filled request yields one impression, RPM = fill rate × impression eCPM.
RTBReal-time bidding: an auction run separately for each ad opportunity while the user’s page or app waits, typically within about 100 ms.
SDKSoftware development kit: a library embedded in an app, e.g. by an ad network, mediation platform or MMP.
Second-price auctionAn auction in which the winner pays the highest competing bid, or the floor if higher. “Ideal second-price” in this reader ignores floors, bid increments and fees.
sellers.json / SupplyChain objectsellers.json is a file each SSP or exchange publishes listing the sellers it pays; the OpenRTB SupplyChain object lists every intermediary a bid request passed through.
SettlementThe final, contractually checked computation of who owes whom.
SKAdNetworkApple’s earlier privacy-preserving install-attribution API (SKAN), succeeded by AdAttributionKit.
SSPSupply-side platform: software that sells a publisher’s inventory to many buyers and exchanges and manages floor prices.
Statistical arbitrageHere, buying impressions on CPM while selling outcomes on CPA: profitable in expectation, but exposed to conversion, attribution and payment risk.
Take rateThe platform’s net revenue divided by the gross advertiser spend that flows through it.
tCPATarget CPA: the average cost per action an advertiser asks the bidder to aim for; a bidding goal, not a billing price.
TOT / CACETreatment effect on the treated / complier average causal effect: the effect on users actually exposed; with one-sided noncompliance, the ITT divided by the exposure rate in the treatment group.
tROASTarget ROAS: the return on ad spend an advertiser asks the bidder to aim for; a bidding goal, not a billing contract.
View-through conversionA conversion credited to an impression that was not clicked; a click-through conversion follows a click.
WaterfallA mediation method that calls demand sources one at a time in a configured priority order until one fills the request.
Win rateThe probability of winning at bid b, F(b)=P(Z\le b); as a metric, wins divided by bids.

Review record

November 11, 2026 (Chinese edition). An independent Claude Opus 5.5 review covered all eight chapters, formulas, tables, exercises and calculator code. It found no arithmetic or derivation errors but flagged gaps: incremental-ROAS labelling, the successful-impression condition, symbol scope, and definitions used before they were given. The editor revised accordingly and, from the source monograph, corrected one misreading in the review: there r is the predicted click-through rate, not a monetary value. Derivations were also checked by numerical integration, grid search over the budget-feasible region, and recomputation of every worked example.

November 12, 2026 (English edition, first pass). Corrections to translation at inline boundaries, the pacing multiplier, the derivative variable, Axon data qualifiers, chapter references and billing units.

November 12, 2026 (English edition, close-reading revision). Each chapter was re-derived and rewritten by a separate reviewer, then cross-checked by the editor.

  • Math. No numerical errors were found in the original worked examples. Fixed: the validity range of the corrected budget-bid coefficient (B/N\le L/6, not a condition on T); inconsistent rounding between text and calculator; one confidence interval stated two ways (chapters 4 and 7 now use the same binomial standard error). Added: KKT derivation of the calculator bid with its shadow prices, second-order and boundary checks, pacing as projected dual subgradient descent, the first- and second-price censored likelihoods, the sampling correction in odds form, and an uncertainty analysis showing that the chapter 4 experiment establishes a positive lift but not the sign of the profit.
  • Definitions. Every symbol, acronym and ad-domain term is now defined at or before first use in each chapter, or linked to where it is defined. Parameters that were defined only inside collapsed sections are restated where used. The glossary is the canonical reference.
  • Facts (chapter 6). Claims were spot-checked against AppLovin's 2025 10-K and its MAX and Axon documentation: net (agent) revenue recognition with dynamically set prices, the MAX fee on bidding-network revenue, win/loss notices sent to every bidder, and the apps-business sale. Every claim is labelled public fact, inference or unknown.
  • Notation and style. US dollars throughout; one symbol set across chapters 3, 7 and 8 (L, N, B, F); all mathematics typeset with KaTeX.

The review covers the text and code under their stated assumptions. It does not establish that those assumptions hold in any real market. Source page and equation numbers for the RTB monograph were not re-verified in this pass.
