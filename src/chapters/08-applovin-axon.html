The gist. AppLovin sits on both sides of one market. MAX runs the auction inside publishers' apps; AppLovin Ads, powered by the Axon model, is one of the bidders in that auction, and it earns nearly all of the company's revenue. Running the auction shows AppLovin every bid, but seeing data is not permission to train on it, and the company says Axon does not use rivals' bids. A better model makes money by moving spend toward impressions that convert, even when the number of wins stays fixed in the uniform-threshold example. So a real moat shows up in advertisers' incremental returns on comparable supply, not in win rates, publisher prices or revenue growth.

What the products are

AppLovin sells three things that are easy to blur: a prediction engine, the ad product it powers, and the auction where that product bids. Publishers here are app developers; ad networks reach their inventory through an SDK (a library compiled into the app). Mediation software in the app picks the demand source for each ad request, either by calling networks in a fixed order (a waterfall) or by real-time bids (in-app bidding). A DSP (demand-side platform) bids for advertisers. MAX is AppLovin's mediation product, which the company calls its in-app bidding solution. Axon is AppLovin's prediction and bidding engine (the filings say "recommendation engine"). The advertiser product it powers is Axon Ads Manager in the 2025 Form 10-K and AppLovin Ads in the Q3 2026 Form 10-Q; this chapter says AppLovin Ads.

Public fact. AppLovin describes Axon as the AI advertising engine that powers AppLovin Ads. AppLovin bids on each impression according to the value Axon assigns to it for a given advertiser, aiming at that advertiser's return-on-ad-spend (ROAS) goal. The 2025 10-K and the Q3 2026 10-Q both say this product generates substantially all of AppLovin's revenue; the 10-K adds that revenue from other services "was not material". Axon official description, AppLovin 2025 10-K, AppLovin Q3 2026 10-Q

Public fact. MAX's partner documentation describes a "unified first-price auction" in which the highest bidder wins, and its OpenRTB specification says bidders are expected "to pay the CPM amount they bid in full". DSPs bid through AppLovin Exchange over OpenRTB 2.5; ad networks bid through tokens from their own SDKs. Non-bidding networks enter at a CPM set by the publisher or by auto-CPM from their recent reported CPMs. The documented sequence for one ad request: MAX auction flow, AppLovin OpenRTB specification, bid responses and notices, auto-CPM

  1. Request
    Ad request. The app asks for an ad. MAX collects bid tokens from the network SDKs it initialized at launch and sends the request to the MAX ad server.
  2. ≤ tmax
    Bids. The auction server sends bid requests to SDK bidders and to DSPs in AppLovin Exchange. Only bids returned within the timeout tmax count.
  3. Resolve
    Unified auction. Bids are merged with the publisher's waterfall networks at their CPM prices; the highest CPM wins. Invalid bids (below the floor, missing or zero) get a loss notice now.
  4. Load
    Win and loss notices. The winner's ad is loaded. If the load fails, the winner gets a loss notice. Other bidders get their loss notice after a winning ad loads.
  5. Render
    Billing. When the ad renders (video starts, or a graphic is displayed), MAX calls the winner's billing URL, the signal on which DSPs should count impressions and spend.

Public fact. The notices carry prices. ${AUCTION_PRICE}, the final price, is substituted into both the win URL and the loss URL, "so every bidder (win or loss) sees the final paid price". The winner also receives ${AUCTION_MINIMUM_BID_TO_WIN}, defined as the second-highest bid. A loss notice carries an OpenRTB loss-reason code. These are MAX's semantics; other exchanges define their notices differently (Chapter 7). MAX notice macros

One MAX auction: who earns what

Given, for one ad request (prices in dollars CPM unless marked per impression): publisher floor $3.00; waterfall network W entered at $5.50. Bids: AppLovin Ads $7.50, SDK bidder X $6.80, DSP Y $6.00, DSP Z $2.00. AppLovin Ads bids for an advertiser who pays a=\$30 per conversion, with conversion probability q=0.0005 per impression, so value v=qa=\$0.015 per impression; $7.50 CPM is an illustrative bid, chosen using the half-value rule from the Chapter 4 appendix. Let \varphi=5\% be the MAX fee on winning auction spend; MAX's real fee rate is not disclosed. For this stylized CPA contract, assume the fee is charged to a third-party bidder on top of the publisher payment.

  1. Filter. Z's $2.00 is below the $3.00 floor, so Z gets a loss notice during the auction.
  2. Rank by CPM: $7.50 (AppLovin Ads) > $6.80 (X) > $6.00 (Y) > $5.50 (W). AppLovin Ads wins and, under first price, pays its bid: b=\$7.50\ \text{CPM}=\$7.50/1000=\$0.0075\ \text{per impression}.
  3. Notices. The winner learns the second-highest bid, $6.80, so it paid $7.50 − $6.80 = $0.70 CPM more than needed to win. X and Y each receive AUCTION_PRICE = $7.50 in their loss notices: the price they had to beat.
  4. Render and bill. The billing URL fires at render; the publisher is credited $0.0075 for this impression.
  5. AppLovin's revenue when its own bidder wins: the advertiser charge minus the publisher payment, per impression and in expectation over the conversion: v-b=\$0.015-\$0.0075=\$0.0075.
  6. Counterfactual: X wins at the same $7.50. AppLovin earns only the MAX fee: \varphi\,b=0.05\times\$0.0075=\$0.000375\ \text{per impression}.

Result: the publisher earns $7.50 per 1,000 such impressions whoever wins; AppLovin expects $7.50 per 1,000 when its own bidder wins and $0.375 when a third party wins at the same price, a factor of 20 under the assumed prices and fee.

Check: (v-b)/(\varphi b)=0.0075/0.000375=20. The winning price ($7.50) is at least the second bid ($6.80), as a highest-bid-wins auction requires; among comparable eligible real-time bids, a reported winning price below a losing bidder’s own valid bid would warrant reconciliation against publisher settlement records. The notice itself is generated by the auctioneer.

Under these inputs the auctioneer earns more when its own bidder wins; at a fixed publisher payment b that holds only if v-b>\varphi b. The example shows a possible incentive, not auction behavior. A ROAS goal and a budget are constraints; Axon turns them into one bid per impression

An AppLovin advertiser never types a bid. Its two main settings are the two constraints of its problem: spend at most the budget each day, and earn at least the ROAS goal in revenue per dollar spent. Axon then bids on every impression so as to buy as much predicted revenue as those two constraints allow. The per-impression work sits in Axon's predictions; the two settings only fix how predicted revenue is turned into a price, and how much of it to buy.

Public fact. AppLovin's campaign API takes the following:

  • A goal type: purchase-revenue ROAS, ad-revenue ROAS or blended ROAS, as well as cost-per-install and cost-per-event goals.
  • A goal value, set once for all countries or per country.
  • A ROAS day target, day 7 or day 28: the revenue window counted from install.
  • A required daily budget, set once for all countries or per country.

The goal type is fixed when the campaign is created; the goal value and the budget can be changed later. Two bidding strategies are listed:

  • target_goal_with_cpi_billing: AppLovin “aims to control the cost per result around your target goal” and charges per install.
  • auto_bidding_with_cpm_billing: it “aims to fully use your budget while maximizing results at the lowest possible cost per result” and charges per impression.

AppLovin campaign management API. The documentation does not publish the bidding algorithm. What follows is the standard model of bidding under a value goal and a budget (Aggarwal, Badanidiyuru and Mehta, 2019), not AppLovin's disclosed method.

Model. For each impression i, Axon predicts its value, the expected day-7 revenue it will produce:

v_i=P(\text{install}\mid i)\times\mathbb E[\text{D7 revenue}\mid\text{install},\,i].

With ROAS goal R (0.4 for 40%) and a pacing factor \lambda between 0 and 1, it bids

b_i=\lambda\,\frac{v_i}{R}.
  • The goal sets the exchange rate. Paying v_i/R for v_i of expected revenue earns exactly R, so v_i/R is the most the impression is worth at the goal. MAX's auction is first-price (the winner pays its bid; see above), so bidding v_i/R everywhere yields a campaign ROAS of exactly R in expectation, provided the predictions are calibrated.
  • The budget sets \lambda. If bidding v_i/R would spend more than the budget, the pacing controller lowers \lambda until expected spend equals the budget, and ROAS comes out above the goal. If it would not, \lambda=1: the goal binds and the campaign spends less than its budget.
  • The predictions pick the impressions. One multiplier scales every bid, so a user predicted to bring twice the revenue gets twice the bid. This is why two settings suffice; chapter 4 shows why one multiplier is the right answer for a budget.
Worked example

Which constraint binds

Given, for one country and one day: N=1{,}000{,}000 alike opportunities, each with a 0.5% install probability and $2 expected D7 revenue per install, so v=0.005\times\$2=\$0.01 per impression ($10 CPM). The highest competing bid is Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,\$0.05), as in chapter 4's model with a $50 CPM ceiling. ROAS goal 40%.

  1. Bid at \lambda=1: \$0.01/0.4=\$0.025 per impression, $25 CPM. It wins with probability 0.025/0.05=0.5: 500,000 impressions.
  2. Spend 500{,}000\times\$0.025=\$12{,}500; D7 revenue 500{,}000\times\$0.01=\$5{,}000; ROAS 5{,}000/12{,}500=40\%; 2,500 installs at $5 each.
  3. Budget $20,000: it does not bind. The campaign spends $12,500 and leaves $7,500 unspent.
  4. Budget $5,000: it binds. Expected spend is Nb^2/L, so the controller lowers the bid to b=\sqrt{5{,}000\times0.05/10^6}=\$0.0158\ (\$15.81\text{ CPM}), which is \lambda=15.81/25=0.632. It wins 316,228 impressions for $5,000 and earns $3,162 of D7 revenue, a ROAS of 63.2%, with 1,581 installs at $3.16.

Result: with a slack budget the goal binds, ROAS lands on 40% and $7,500 goes unspent; with a $5,000 budget the budget binds, bids fall to 63% of the goal-implied bid, and ROAS rises to 63%.

Check: when every impression has value v and costs its bid b, ROAS is v/b: 0.01/0.025=40\% and 0.01/0.0158=63.2\% ✓. Spend 316{,}228\times\$0.0158114=\$5{,}000 ✓.

Reading the table as an advertiser:

  • Spend below budget means the goal is the lever. Raising the budget changes nothing. Lowering the goal buys volume at a worse rate.
  • The marginal return is far below the average. Moving from a 40% to a 30% goal adds $7,500 of spend and $1,325 of D7 revenue, a marginal ROAS of 17.7% even though the campaign reports 31.6% (chapter 3 explains why).
  • Spend at budget with ROAS above the goal means the budget is the lever. Raising the budget buys volume until ROAS falls to the goal.
  • Set the goal from lifetime economics. The goal counts only seven (or 28) days of revenue, so derive it from the long-run return you need. If D7 revenue is typically a quarter of first-year revenue and you need a first-year ROAS of 120%, set the D7 goal at 0.25\times120\%=30\%. Revenue is not profit: break-even on contribution needs a return of 1/m, where m is the contribution margin (chapter 3).
  • The ROAS is attributed, not caused. It counts revenue the attribution rules credit to AppLovin; only a holdout test shows how much the ads caused (chapter 6).
  • Billing decides who bears which error. With CPI billing the advertiser pays per install, so AppLovin bears errors in the predicted install rate, as the platform bears click-rate errors under CPC (chapter 3). Errors in predicted revenue per install stay with the advertiser and show up as missed ROAS. With CPM billing the advertiser bears both.
Optional: why one multiplier is optimal under both constraints

Take chapter 4's first-price model with impressions of different values: the highest competing bid Z_i\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L), so a bid b_i\le L wins with probability b_i/L and pays b_i. The advertiser maximizes expected revenue \sum_i (b_i/L)\,v_i subject to a budget, \sum_i (b_i/L)\,b_i\le B, and a ROAS goal, \sum_i (b_i/L)\,v_i\ge R\sum_i (b_i/L)\,b_i. Revenue is the objective because the advertiser has already expressed what profit it needs through R.

  1. Attach a multiplier \gamma\ge0 to the ROAS constraint and \mu\ge0 to the budget. The problem separates into one term per impression: \max_{b_i}\ \frac{b_i}{L}\Big[(1+\gamma)\,v_i-(\gamma R+\mu)\,b_i\Big].
  2. The bracket times b_i is a downward parabola, maximized at b_i=\frac{1+\gamma}{2(\gamma R+\mu)}\,v_i=\kappa\,v_i. The factor \kappa is the same for every impression: one multiplier.
  3. With bids \kappa v_i, every won impression pays \kappa per dollar of value, so campaign ROAS is 1/\kappa. The goal requires \kappa\le1/R, and the budget requires \kappa\le\kappa_B, the multiplier at which expected spend equals B.
  4. Revenue rises with \kappa, so the best choice is \kappa=\min\{1/R,\ \kappa_B\}, which is the main text's \lambda/R with \lambda=\min\{1,\ R\,\kappa_B\}.

Check: in the worked example 1/R=2.5. A $5,000 budget allows a bid of $0.0158, so \kappa_B=0.0158/0.01=1.58; then \kappa=1.58 and \lambda=0.4\times1.58=0.632 ✓. Two caveats: the bid is exactly proportional to value only because competition is uniform, and real first-price bidders shade by the shape of the competing-bid distribution; in a second-price auction one multiplier is exactly optimal without that assumption (Aggarwal, Badanidiyuru and Mehta, 2019).

Axon can predict revenue, not just clicks, because revenue labels come back to it

A model can predict any outcome it receives labels for. Clicks are simply the label an ad network observes by itself; for app campaigns, installs and revenue come back too, because the advertiser's measurement partner sends them. The value v_i in the bid above is therefore a prediction like any other, only with rarer, later and noisier labels.

Public fact. A campaign in AppLovin's API names its measurement partner (the tracking_method field, for example Adjust or AppsFlyer). Such a partner (an MMP; chapter 6) sends the ad network a postback for each install it credits to that network and for the later in-app events the advertiser chooses to forward, typically including purchases with their value. Inference. If the advertiser's own app also shows ads through MAX, AppLovin sees the ad revenue each of its users generates directly.

Each install then becomes one training example, labelled with the revenue its user generates in the 7 days after install. The label is final only a week later, which is the delayed-label problem of chapter 5. Because most users never pay and a few pay a lot, the value is usually modelled in two steps:

v_i=P(\text{install}\mid i)\times P(\text{pays within 7 days}\mid\text{install},\,i)\times\mathbb E[\text{amount}\mid\text{payer},\,i].

With a 0.5% install rate, 4% of installs paying within the week and $50 per payer, v=0.005\times0.04\times\$50=\$0.01 per impression, the value used in the example above.

At bid time no install has happened yet, so the model can use only what exists before the impression. AppLovin's published list of Axon's inputs (below) maps onto three kinds of signal:

  • Device and network: device model and price tier, operating system, country, carrier or connection type. Spending differs sharply between a new flagship phone and a budget Android.
  • Context: the app showing the ad, the placement (rewarded or interstitial) and the time of day.
  • Engagement history: where a device identifier is available, how the device responded to past ads, such as which apps it installed through AppLovin and what those installs later earned according to postbacks from other advertisers' campaigns. This lets one game's labels inform the value of a user to another game. Whether every postback may be pooled across advertisers is a question of contracts and permissions, taken up in the next section.

Settings select and price predictions: what each model predicts and which loss trains it

In the standard design, changing a ROAS goal retrains nothing. Outcome models predict what an impression will bring (an install, a purchase, revenue), shared across campaigns through app and campaign features; the goal type chooses which predictions are combined, and the goal value puts a price on them. The advertiser input that reaches the models in this design is what comes back after the install, because that is where the labels come from: the events and purchases its measurement partner forwards and, if the app shows ads through MAX, the ad revenue AppLovin sees there (AppLovin says MAX signals help optimize ad-revenue campaigns, without specifying the labels). Even in this design the goal reaches the training data indirectly: it changes which impressions are won, and only shown impressions get outcomes.

Public fact. AppLovin's API makes the goal type and the ROAS day target (DAY7 or DAY28) creation-only settings, while goal values and budgets, overall or per country, can be updated later. AppLovin publishes Axon's input signals and says it learns continually, but not its model architecture, labels, losses, or how it uses the goal internally. One set of predictions, six settings

The log loss for label y\in\{0,1\} and prediction p is -[y\ln p+(1-y)\ln(1-p)]; chapter 5 shows that its expectation is minimized at the true probability. The revenue model is where the choices matter. With amount a and prediction m, four common options:

  • Squared error in dollars, (a-m)^2. Minimized at the conditional mean (when the amount has finite variance), which is the right target, but squaring large dollar residuals lets a few big spenders dominate the fit and makes it noisy.
  • Squared error on the logarithm, (\ln a-\ln m)^2. Stable, but minimized at \ln m=\mathbb E[\ln a], so exponentiating the output gives the geometric mean, which is below the arithmetic mean whenever amounts vary among users with the same features. Used as is, it underbids at fixed λ. A binding budget's controller can absorb a bias common to every segment that shares one λ by raising it, if λ has room: from λ = 0.8, a ratio near 1.9 as in the example below would need λ ≈ 1.5, above its cap of 1, so bids stay low and the campaign underspends. Relative bids stay distorted wherever the ratio of arithmetic to geometric mean differs within that group. Under a log-normal the ratio is e^{\sigma^2/2}, so when amounts are log-normal given the features and the spread is known, multiplying the true geometric mean by it gives the arithmetic mean exactly; a fitted log-mean still carries its estimation error.
  • Log-normal likelihood. The model outputs a location \mu and a spread \sigma and minimizes \ln\sigma+(\ln a-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2) (plus terms not depending on them); the bid then uses the log-normal's mean e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}. Correct if amounts really are log-normal given the features.
  • Tweedie or gamma loss with a log link. For Tweedie power \rho between 1 and 2 the loss is -a\,m^{1-\rho}/(1-\rho)+m^{2-\rho}/(2-\rho), whose derivative with respect to m, m^{-\rho}(m-a), is zero in expectation at m=\mathbb E[a]. With \rho between 1 and 2 it allows exact zeros, so it can model revenue per install directly. The gamma loss, \rho=2, is a/m+\ln m for positive amounts and is also minimized at the mean. Both assume noise grows with the mean, which suits revenue; that assumption affects how efficiently the data are used, not which value the loss targets. scikit-learn: Tweedie deviance

The zero-inflated log-normal loss combines the purchase and amount models in one network. It outputs a probability p of any revenue and log-normal parameters \mu,\sigma; an install's loss is the log loss on whether it earned anything plus, for those that did, the log-normal loss on the amount, and the prediction is p\,e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}. For purchase revenue the positive outcome is paying; for ad or blended revenue it is earning any revenue, which includes users who never buy. For Tile Quest's purchase revenue, 3% paying and a $20 payer mean give $0.60 per install. Wang, Liu and Miao, 2019

Worked example

The same five payers, three answers

Given: in one segment, five payers spend $5, $5, $5, $15 and $70 in their first 7 days. The segment has an install probability of 0.4% and a 3% payer rate, and the goal is 20% D7 ROAS. Each loss is fitted with constant parameters within the segment.

  1. Squared error in dollars, or a Tweedie or gamma loss: the best constant is the sample mean, (5+5+5+15+70)/5=\$20.
  2. Squared error on the logarithm: the best constant is the geometric mean, e^{(3\ln5+\ln15+\ln70)/5}\approx\$10.56.
  3. Log-normal likelihood: \hat\mu\approx2.357, \hat\sigma\approx1.037, so the fitted mean is e^{\hat\mu+\hat\sigma^2/2}\approx\$18.08.
  4. Bids at 20%, 0.004\times0.03\times\text{amount}/0.20: $12.00, $6.34 and $10.85 CPM.

Result: squared error on the logarithm, exponentiated naively, cuts the bid by 47% here, while fitting its own loss perfectly well; a loss that targets the mean, or a fitted distribution read through its mean, gets close to the $20 sample mean. What the bid needs is the expected spend of the next comparable payer; five observations cannot say whether $20 or $18.08 predicts it better, but both are far above $10.56.

Check: the Tweedie or gamma constant solves \sum_j (m-a_j)\,m^{-\rho}=0, so m is the sample mean ✓. The log-normal's $18.08 differs from $20 because its mean is computed from two fitted parameters rather than averaged; that happens even with genuinely log-normal draws, and with many payers the two converge if the log-normal is right. In production, the dollar-scale check is the same either way: compare summed predictions with realized revenue, in dollars, by segment.

Getting the loss right is necessary, not sufficient. Five more things decide whether the predictions can be bid on:

  • Outcomes exist only for shown ads. The label is revenue attributed to a rendered impression or its click. Losing an auction yields no rendered impression and so no outcome, so neither ordinary training nor ESMM-style training can fill in inventory the old policy never served; that needs exploration or the randomized live check of the life-of-a-customer section.
  • Features are logged at bid time. Each training row joins the features as they were when the bid was made with the postbacks that arrive later, through the impression or click identifier. That join works only for linkable traffic; SKAN-only traffic is measured by cohort, and a missing postback must stay distinct from an observed zero. A feature built from information that was not available at bid time leaks the answer.
  • Labels wait for their windows. A row is final only after its window and the reporting delay. Counted from the impression, each install's revenue window ends 7 or 28 days after its own install, so a whole impression cohort closes only after the latest install the attribution rules can still credit (the view-through lookback runs from the impression, the click-through lookback from the click) plus that window: an install credited six days after the impression can still earn D7 revenue on day 13. The cutoff applies to whole cohorts: keeping a young cohort's early positives while dropping its unresolved rows inflates the rates, and keeping its unresolved rows as negatives deflates them, unless a delayed-feedback model corrects for it (chapter 5).
  • Sampling is undone exactly once. Install models are usually trained on downsampled negatives. Either weight the kept negatives by the inverse of their sampling rate during training, or train unweighted and apply the odds correction to the predictions (chapter 5); doing both, or neither, biases every bid.
  • The product is calibrated, not just its parts. The value multiplies two or three predictions, so their errors multiply too, and heads that are each calibrated on average can still give a biased product when their errors move together across impressions. The final check compares summed bid-time predictions with matured outcomes in the goal's own unit (revenue in dollars for ROAS, credited events for CPE, each credited payer once for this example's CPP) on held-out shown, linkable traffic: by score band, by segment, and especially on inventory a changed goal newly reaches, using impressions from exploration or the new policy's live arm. SKAN-only impressions can still be scored and the score logged, but their postbacks cannot be joined back to the individual score, and Apple includes a conversion value only when its privacy threshold allows: a postback without one still counts the install but leaves its revenue unknown. Count each install once, from the winning network's first postback; Apple also sends value-less postbacks to networks that did not win. So they are checked at the level of reported cohorts.

The life of a customer: from the first campaign to a steady ROAS campaign

Whether a new app starts on an install goal or directly on a ROAS goal, the platform faces the same problem: its estimate of the app's install rate starts updating within hours, as install postbacks arrive, but it needs weeks of matured purchases to learn the app's revenue per install. The objective decides what the campaign buys while it learns; the billing choice, separately, decides who bears errors in the predicted install rate. This section follows one fictional app from integration to a mature ROAS campaign: which systems run, which models are trained, and what data each stage needs.

Public fact. AppLovin's published material:

  • Today a new app may launch on ROAS. AppLovin's current first-campaign guide offers blended, purchase or ad-revenue ROAS with a day-7 or day-28 window, or cost per purchase (CPP) when “every purchaser has the same value, such as subscriptions”. It does not list an install goal as a first step. It advises launching “both a Day 7 and a Day 28 campaign” when payback takes longer than 7 days, running “each platform” (iOS, Android) “as its own campaign”, and setting goals from “what you're already achieving on other advertising channels”.   • Day-7 goals learn slowly. A 2024 post says day-7 campaigns “require more time to collect mature data and begin the optimization process”, suit apps with a 6–18 month payback, and that some apps “achieve ~10% IAP ROAS” on them. AppLovin, IAP ROAS campaigns (2024, archived)

So starting on installs is a choice, not AppLovin's current rule. The 2022 product required historical cohorts of installs and purchasers but did not say they had to come from an earlier install campaign; other networks set their own eligibility rules. Everything below the public facts is a model of how such a system typically works, not AppLovin's disclosed design, and every number is illustrative.

The running app. Tile Quest, a fictional puzzle game on Android that earns money from in-app purchases. On the install-first path its first campaign has a goal of $4 per install (CPI), is billed a fixed $4 per credited install, and spends its full $10,000 daily budget, so it buys 2,500 installs a day. Of those installs, 3% make a purchase within 7 days, spending $20 on average, so D7 revenue per install is 0.03\times\$20=\$0.60 and the install campaign's D7 ROAS is \$0.60/\$4=15\%.

A D7 ROAS goal far below 100% is normal, because it counts only the first week of a payback that takes months. Suppose Tile Quest's first-week revenue is typically 14% of its first-year revenue, the 30% app-store fee is its only variable cost, and every attributed dollar is incremental. Then one point of D7 ROAS is worth 0.70/0.14=5 points of first-year return after the fee, and:

  • the install campaign's 15% D7 ROAS means 15\%/0.14\approx107\% of spend back in first-year revenue, or 75% after the fee: it loses 25 cents of every dollar in year one;
  • break-even is a D7 ROAS of 0.14/0.70=20\%, which is the goal both paths use below. A 20% goal is the advertiser asking for its money back within a year, not accepting a loss.
  1. Day −7 to 0
    Integration. The app ships the MMP's SDK; install, open, progress and purchase events are mapped and forwarded to AppLovin; creatives are uploaded; tracking is tested on a real device.
  2. Days 1–7
    First campaign, cold start. Either path: the app's install-rate estimate starts updating within hours. Install-first buys installs at $4; ROAS-from-launch already bids on revenue, using revenue estimates borrowed from similar apps.
  3. Days 8–30
    Revenue labels mature. Purchase postbacks turn into D7 labels at least seven days behind the installs, and the app's own revenue model takes over from the pooled one.
  4. From about day 30
    Bidding on the app's own revenue. Install-first: a new ROAS campaign starts, a policy change that buys a different mix of users. ROAS-from-launch: the same campaign continues, its revenue estimates now mostly the app's own.
  5. Month 2 onward
    Steady state. Per-country goals, creative refresh, budget changes judged at the margin, holdout tests and drift checks.

Integration decides what can ever be learned

The platform can only optimize toward events it receives, so integration is the first and least reversible decision. An event that is never forwarded can never become a label. A verified purchase without its value still says that the user paid, but its amount is unknown, not zero: the user is a confirmed positive for the purchase-rate model, while their 7-day total stays incomplete, and out of the amount model, until the missing amount is recovered.

  1. A Tile Quest video renders in another game and the user clicks it. An auction AppLovin lost produces no impression and no training row.
  2. The user installs and first opens the app two days later. The MMP credits the last eligible click within its lookback window, AppLovin's, and sends AppLovin an install postback.
  3. The label builder joins the postback to the logged click and impression. Purchases in the 7 days after install attach to that install.
  4. If a known tracking outage hit some hours, the affected impressions are held back or backfilled, not written as non-installs.

Revenue for that traffic is a per-campaign cohort estimate, built on a separate path. Days 1–7: the install-rate estimate starts updating within hours

A brand-new app has no history, so the first predictions come from a shared install-rate model plus a prior for this app; installs are frequent enough that the app's own data take over once the first day's installs have been reported.

  • Model. One install-rate model across all advertisers, with features of the device, the context and (where permitted) the device's ad history, crossed with features of the app (genre, store category) and of each creative. A new app borrows the behaviour of similar apps until its own installs arrive.
  • Value and price. Under CPI billing AppLovin collects $4 per credited install, so at a 0.4% install rate an impression is worth 0.004\times\$4=\$0.016 to AppLovin in expected receipts, $16 CPM. That is the most AppLovin should bid into MAX's first-price auction; it bids less, scaled by pacing and shaded. What it pays publishers is its cost, and receipts minus that cost are its spread (chapter 4's appendix). The advertiser simply pays $4 per install: $10,000 buys 2,500 installs, which at 0.4% come from about 625,000 impressions.
  • Data volume. About 100 installs are eventually credited to each hour's impressions (2,500 ÷ 24). Once a full day's installs have been reported, which takes longer than a day because of reporting delays, the app-level install rate has a relative standard error of about 2%: \sqrt{(1-0.004)/2{,}500}=2.0\%. A thin creative or placement still leans on the shared model for days.
  • Exploration. New creatives and thinly observed segments get bids drawn from their uncertainty rather than from the point estimate, so the system keeps buying some impressions it is unsure about. Some of these bids exceed expected receipts: a draw of 0.6% instead of the 0.4% mean values an impression at $24 CPM rather than $16. Accepting that expected loss on a small share of traffic is the price of information; capping exploratory bids at $16 would never reach the inventory where the estimate might be too low. Without this, a creative that looks bad after 3,000 impressions may receive too few further impressions to correct the estimate, whatever its true rate.
  • Risk. With CPI billing a wrong install-rate prediction costs AppLovin, the party that holds the data and can correct it fastest (chapter 3). The advertiser bears only the revenue per install, which is why its D7 ROAS sits at whatever the installs happen to be worth, 15% here.
Worked example

A prior that gives way to data

Given: similar puzzle games install at 0.3% per impression. The system treats that as a prior worth 20,000 impressions of evidence: 60 installs in 20,000 impressions. Tile Quest's first 50,000 impressions produce 200 installs, a raw rate of 0.4%; treat these impressions as matured, so every outcome is known. A live estimate from the first hours would need a delayed-feedback correction and would be approximate.

  1. Pool the prior with the observed data: \frac{60+200}{20{,}000+50{,}000}=\frac{260}{70{,}000}=0.371\%.
  2. Once the first day's impressions have matured (625,000 impressions, 2,500 installs): \frac{60+2{,}500}{20{,}000+625{,}000}=\frac{2{,}560}{645{,}000}=0.397\%, with the prior now 3.1% of the evidence.

Result: after 50,000 impressions the estimate has moved from 0.30% to 0.37%; once the first day's impressions have matured it is 0.397%, essentially the app's own rate.

Check: these are the means of a Beta(60, 19,940) prior updated with the observed installs and non-installs ✓. The prior shrinks noisy early estimates toward similar apps. That matters because the system then selects: if it favoured whichever creative or segment had the highest raw rate after a few hundred impressions, it would systematically pick lucky ones, the winner's curse.

Days 8–30: revenue labels mature, and the app's revenue model takes over

Every credited install becomes a training example for revenue, but its label is complete only once the 7-day window has closed and late postbacks have arrived, and it is positive for only 3% of installs, so the app's own revenue estimate needs weeks; until then the system can start from similar apps.

  • Labels. Each install's label is its total purchase revenue in the 7 days after install, repeat purchases included. The window closes on day 8 for day-1 installs, but postbacks can arrive late and revise a cohort, so the label builder tracks event time and receipt time separately. With prompt reporting, by the end of day 30 the system has 23 days of matured cohorts: about 57,500 installs and 1,725 payers.
  • Models. A purchase model, P(\text{pays within 7 days}\mid\text{install},\,i), trained on installs; and an amount model for a payer's 7-day total, trained on payers. A heavy-tailed amount is often modelled as log-normal, but the bid needs its arithmetic mean, e^{m+\sigma^2/2} for log-scale mean m and spread \sigma. Exponentiating a predicted mean log gives the geometric mean, which at \sigma=1.5 is e^{1.125}=3.08 times too low, so calibration is checked in dollars, not in logs.
  • Segments need pooling. Revenue per install is a rare event times a heavy-tailed amount. With the spread assumed in the optional box below, Tile Quest's app-wide average D7 revenue per install reaches a 10% relative standard error after about 31,500 matured installs, 13 days of installs plus the 7-day wait; by day 30 its 57,500 matured installs give 7.4%. A 95% interval of ±10% would need about 121,000. Each country and device segment, though, has only a fraction of those installs, so segment estimates borrow strength from the app-wide estimate and, at launch, from similar apps, with weights that depend on how much the populations really differ.
  • Intermediate goals. The API also offers cost-per-event (CPE) and cost-per-purchaser (CPP) goals. Each day's install cohort supplies 625 users who reach level 10 within 3 days (25% of 2,500) but only 75 who pay within 7 (3%), so a CPE goal on that event has a much denser label, at least on the traffic the install campaign bought; it helps only if reaching level 10 predicts later spending. A 7-day CPP goal has the same 3% positive rate as the purchase model, and the same timing: a purchase confirms a positive on receipt, while a non-payer is confirmed only after the window closes. It removes the amount prediction: a CPP goal of $133.33 gives the same average affordable spend per impression as the $4 CPI goal (0.004\times0.03\times\$133.33=\$0.016), and for the same install rate doubles it for a user twice as likely to pay. AppLovin's guide calls CPP cost per purchase and its API cost per purchaser; the difference matters when users buy repeatedly. This example counts each paying user once within 7 days, both its own assumptions: if every payer bought twice, $133.33 per purchaser would be $66.67 per purchase. AppLovin's 2024 post cites a game that combined cost-per-engagement campaigns with IAP ROAS.
Worked example

ROAS from launch: a pooled revenue estimate gives way to the app's own

Given: on the ROAS-from-launch path there is no install campaign, and the goal is the break-even 20% D7 ROAS. Similar puzzle games earn $0.80 of D7 revenue per install, and the system treats that genre estimate as worth 10,000 matured installs of evidence. Tile Quest's users actually earn $0.60, as on the install path, so the genre estimate is a third too optimistic. Assume the install-rate predictions are accurate on the traffic won, the bid passes through to the advertiser unshaded in a first-price auction, λ = 1, and the revenue estimate is refreshed from matured cohorts at the end of a day. The campaign is billed per impression, so it pays for impressions that bring no install too; averaged over its won traffic, its expected effective CPI, spend divided by expected installs, is predicted revenue per install divided by the goal. The install counts below are those observed on this path; daily spend may vary as the price changes.

  1. Days 1–9, bidding on the genre estimate: the expected effective CPI is \$0.80/0.20=\$4.00, as much as the install campaign pays, and an install brings \$0.60 on average. D7 ROAS is \$0.60/\$4.00=15\%; first-year receipts after the fee are 75 cents per dollar of spend.
  2. The day-1 cohort completes its seven days at the end of day 8 and the day-2 cohort at the end of day 9. Suppose the first refresh comes then, with 5,000 matured installs averaging $0.60: \frac{10{,}000\times\$0.80+5{,}000\times\$0.60}{15{,}000}\approx\$0.733. On traffic bought from day 10, the expected effective CPI is \$0.733/0.20\approx\$3.67 and D7 ROAS about 16.4% (82 cents per dollar in year one after the fee).
  3. Suppose that at the end of day 30 the matured cohorts of days 1–23 hold 57,500 installs, again averaging $0.60: \frac{10{,}000\times\$0.80+57{,}500\times\$0.60}{67{,}500}\approx\$0.630, so traffic bought from day 31 costs about $3.15 per install and returns an expected D7 ROAS of about 19.1% (95 cents of first-year receipts after the fee per dollar).

Result: expected D7 ROAS equals the goal times true over predicted revenue, so while the campaign leans on a too-optimistic genre estimate it overpays for installs, and its expected ROAS climbs toward the 20% goal from below: 15%, then about 16.4%, then about 19.1%. These are expectations for traffic bought at each price, confirmed only when that traffic's installs, some arriving days after the impression, have had their seven days and their purchase postbacks. Here impressions bought in the first nine days have the same expected D7 ROAS of 15% as the install-first path, because the genre estimate happens to imply the same $4 per install.

Check: with accurate predictions, true over predicted is 1 and expected D7 ROAS is exactly 20%, though a finite cohort lands above or below it. Expected effective CPI × D7 ROAS = $4.00 × 15% ≈ $3.67 × 16.4% ≈ $3.15 × 19.1% ≈ $0.60 ✓ (the percentages use unrounded prices). With equal per-install variance, the weights are proportional to sample sizes. A too-pessimistic estimate fails differently: at $0.40 the campaign would pay $2 per install and return 30%, above its goal, but its lower bids lose every auction whose competing bid falls between them and the correct ones. It gives up installs the advertiser would have wanted, not money. Pacing, a shifting traffic mix or sampling error would move the realized figures.

On the install-first path the switch is a policy change and opens a second learning phase; a ROAS-from-launch campaign meets the same issues more gradually as its traffic mix shifts:

  • The model may have to extrapolate. The install campaign bid $12 CPM for users like A. When the competing bid is $10 both campaigns win A; when it is $20 only the ROAS campaign does. If auctions like the second are common, the install campaign's logs hold few labels exactly where the ROAS campaign now spends. This is the policy bias of chapter 5; where the old campaign had no chance of winning, reweighting its logs cannot recover the missing outcomes.
  • An offline check is necessary but not sufficient. On held-out matured installs, rank by predicted D7 revenue and compare predicted with realized dollars in ten groups; separately, rank by predicted purchase probability and compare observed payer rates. Neither shows calibration on inventory the new campaign starts winning. A live check does: randomly send a small fraction of eligible requests to the new policy at its intended bids (throttling it through a low λ would push its bids back down), log assignment, bid, creative and render, and on that arm compare summed bid-time revenue predictions with matured revenue over all rendered impressions, zeros included, and that revenue with the arm's spend. Make the comparison separately for traffic the old campaign also won and traffic only the new bids reach, where the logs tell them apart: errors in the two can cancel in the total.
  • Control runs on forecasts. Budget pacing uses recorded spend (plus pending install charges under CPI billing), but the ROAS check cannot wait seven days, so it steers on forecast D7 revenue: the model's predictions, updated by early purchases and the app's usual revenue curve, with matured cohorts confirming a week or more later. If predictions on won traffic run uniformly 25% high, the dashboard forecasts 20% and the matured cohorts show 20\%/1.25=16\%.
  • Volume may fall. Bids rise for users like A and fall for users like B; whether total installs and spend fall depends on how much of each kind of traffic there is and what competitors bid. AppLovin warns that unrealistic goals “may prevent the campaign from spending or scaling effectively”.

The 20% goal is only as good as its three assumptions, and each can move it. If a holdout test estimates that only 80% of attributed revenue is incremental, break-even rises to 0.14/(0.70\times0.80)=25\%. If users keep paying after the first year and the advertiser judges payback over a longer horizon, it falls. And the 14% can drift upward for ROAS-bought users, who are chosen for spending early, which makes the true break-even higher than it looks. Setting the goal well above what earlier campaigns achieved, 15% here, risks a campaign that cannot spend. A common compromise is to launch at, say, 17.5% and raise the goal toward the 20% break-even as matured cohorts confirm the predictions. Under the baseline assumptions, hitting 17.5% returns 87.5 cents of first-year receipts after the fee per dollar, so the 12.5-cent shortfall is a deliberate price paid for volume and learning, not a mistake.

Optional: how many installs a revenue estimate needs

Model one install's D7 revenue as X=BY: B is 1 with probability p (the install pays) and Y is the payer's 7-day total, independent of B, with mean \mu and coefficient of variation c (standard deviation divided by mean). Installs are independent and identically distributed.

  1. Moments: \mathbb E X=p\mu and \mathbb E X^2=p\,\mathbb E Y^2=p\mu^2(1+c^2), so \operatorname{Var}X=p\mu^2(1+c^2)-p^2\mu^2.
  2. The average of n installs has relative standard error \frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}X/n}}{p\mu}=\sqrt{\frac{(1+c^2)/p-1}{n}}.
  3. Take p=0.03 and a log-normal amount with log-scale spread \sigma=1.5, for which 1+c^2=e^{\sigma^2}=e^{2.25}=9.488. Then (1+c^2)/p-1=9.488/0.03-1=315.3.
  4. A 10% relative standard error needs n=315.3/0.01\approx31{,}500 installs: 12.6 days at 2,500 a day, plus 7 days for the labels to mature. One week of installs (17,500) gives \sqrt{315.3/17{,}500}=13.4\%. A 95% interval of ±10% needs a standard error of 10\%/1.96, so n=315.3\times1.96^2/0.01\approx121{,}000.

Check: with c=0 (every payer spends the same) the formula reduces to the binomial \sqrt{(1-p)/(np)} ✓. Relative to that, the heavy tail multiplies the required sample by (1+c^2-p)/(1-p)=(9.488-0.03)/0.97=9.75. A segment needs the same number of installs for the same precision, which is why segment-level revenue estimates borrow strength from the app-wide estimate and, early on, from similar apps. The formula gives only the sampling error of the app's own mean; how much weight similar apps deserve also depends on how different their revenue really is, which the formula cannot tell. It sets no switch date either.

How a prediction advantage becomes retained profit

Better information can never hurt a single bidder in a fixed market; it pays by changing which impressions the bidder buys and at what price, and how much of the gain the bidder keeps depends on the market. Let \Pi(\mathcal I) be the set of feasible policies (same objective, actions and budget constraints) that can be computed from information \mathcal I. Then

\mathcal I_{\text{old}}\subseteq\mathcal I_{\text{new}}\;\Rightarrow\;\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{old}})\subseteq\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{new}})\;\Rightarrow\;\max_{\pi\in\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{new}})}\mathbb E[u(\pi)]\ \ge\ \max_{\pi\in\Pi(\mathcal I_{\text{old}})}\mathbb E[u(\pi)].

The proof: the better-informed bidder can ignore the extra information and replay the old policy. The premises are that competitors and the clearing-price distribution stay fixed, the information is free, available at decision time and refines the old, and the policy optimizes on accurate values. A higher offline AUC (ranking quality on held-out logs) establishes none of these.

In the uniform-threshold model, let \hat v_{\rm old} and \hat v_{\rm new} be calibrated estimates of the impression’s dollar value. Assume the same mean prediction and that both models’ bids lie in the linear part of the win curve. The change in expected profit is N[\operatorname{Var}(\hat v_{\rm new})-\operatorname{Var}(\hat v_{\rm old})]/(4L), with the same expected win count. In the example below, profit rises from $2,812.50 to $3,825 at 375,000 expected wins. In a separate example with fixed true value, multiplying the forecast by a factor r changes profit by a factor 1-(r-1)^2 and spend by a factor r^2 while bids stay within the threshold range. That example holds the forecast error fixed. Chapter 5 shows how selecting the highest of several noisy scores can also lead to overprediction.

For the refinement formula, let s=\hat v be a calibrated value estimate, so the conditional expected true value at score s is s. The threshold is independent of the value and score. At bid s/2, conditional expected profit is (s-s/2)(s/(2L))=s^2/(4L). Across N opportunities, expected profit is N[\operatorname{Var}(s)+(\mathbb E[s])^2]/(4L). The mean term cancels when old and new predictions have the same mean. Expected wins are N\mathbb E[s]/(2L), so they stay fixed because the win probability is linear over this range.

A sharper model: same win count, more profit

Given: N=1{,}000{,}000 requests; highest competing bid Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) with L=\$0.02 per impression, unaffected by our bids; a=\$30 per conversion; first price; no budget cap. Half the requests have q_H=0.0008, half q_L=0.0002 (mean 0.0005). The old model cannot tell them apart and predicts 0.0005 everywhere; the new model sees the segment. Both bid b=\hat v/2, the uniform-threshold optimum (Chapter 4 appendix), where \hat v=\hat q\,a is the model's value estimate per impression.

  1. Values per impression: v_H=0.0008\times\$30=\$0.024, v_L=0.0002\times\$30=\$0.006; pooled \bar v=\$0.015.
  2. Old model: bid $0.0075 ($7.50 CPM) everywhere; win rate F=0.0075/0.02=37.5\%; 375,000 wins, 187,500 per segment. Spend 375{,}000\times\$0.0075=\$2{,}812.50. Conversions 187{,}500\times0.0008+187{,}500\times0.0002=150+37.5=187.5, worth 187.5\times\$30=\$5{,}625. Profit $2,812.50.
  3. New model, high segment: bid v_H/2=\$0.012 ($12 CPM), win rate 60%, 300,000 wins, spend $3,600, 240 conversions worth $7,200.
  4. New model, low segment: bid v_L/2=\$0.003 ($3 CPM), win rate 15%, 75,000 wins, spend $225, 15 conversions worth $450.
  5. New totals: 375,000 wins, spend $3,825, 255 conversions worth $7,650, profit $3,825.
  6. In general, at b=v/2 profit per request is (v-b)\,b/L=v^2/(4L), so the gain from refinement is N\,\frac{\mathbb E[v^2]-\bar v^2}{4L}=N\,\frac{\operatorname{Var}(v)}{4L}=10^6\times\frac{0.009^2}{0.08}=\$1{,}012.50.

Result: win count unchanged at 375,000; profit +36% ($2,812.50 → $3,825); this bidder’s spend also +36%; its average payment $7.50 → $10.20 CPM.

Check: $3,825 − $2,812.50 = $1,012.50, matching step 6. Cost per conversion is b/q=a/2=\$15 in every segment under both models: $2,812.50 / 187.5 = $3,825 / 255 = $15.

Win rate did not move, so it cannot be the metric that detects a better model. The gain came from moving wins out of the low segment into the high one.

A miscalibrated model: more volume, less margin

Given: the same market with a single segment, true q=0.0005, so v=\$0.015. The model predicts \hat q=r\,q with r=1.2: every probability is 20% too high. Multiplying all scores by a constant leaves ranking and AUC unchanged.

  1. Bid: b=r\,v/2=1.2\times\$0.015/2=\$0.009 ($9 CPM).
  2. Win rate: F=0.009/0.02=45\%, so 450,000 wins.

Total publisher revenue. Assume every request clears, and when our bidder loses, the rival pays its own bid Z. With a fixed bid b and Z uniform from 0 to L, the publisher earns b with probability b/L. The rival wins otherwise. Averaging both payments gives b\frac bL+\int_b^L\frac zL\,dz=\frac{b^2}{L}+\frac{L^2-b^2}{2L}=\frac L2+\frac{b^2}{2L}. The first term of the left-hand side is our bidder’s spend per request. The integral accounts for the rival’s spend on the requests our bidder loses. Across a million requests, baseline publisher revenue is \$10{,}000+\$2{,}812.50/2=\$11{,}406.25. Inference. How much of a model's gain AppLovin keeps is shaped by market structure as well as model performance. Bidding more for better opportunities raises publisher revenue; competitors and publishers' floor prices adjust; advertisers push for better pricing. The split depends on whether the information is exclusive and stable, whether customers have alternatives, and how the platform charges.

Evidence that separates a moat from good-looking metrics

A moat exists if, on the same supply surface (the inventory one can bid on), with comparable data permissions and budgets, new advertisers and extra budget keep producing incremental contribution for advertisers. Compare incremental contribution per dollar with competing bidders on matched inventory, and check whether that advantage persists. Correct for survivorship bias: a showcase of surviving high-return customers proves nothing about the rest.

Three explanations for growth need different evidence. A better model shows up as higher performance on matched supply at the same budget; privileged data, in which signals each bidder was entitled to use; self-preferencing, in loss notices that contradict the documented auction rules (any bidder can check those) and, for an own bidder that might see rivals' bids before bidding, in an audit of bid-time data access, which loss notices cannot reveal. An advertiser holdout measures one campaign's causal return; separating causes needs a comparison that varies the proposed mechanism.

Three metrics look good without proving any of this. Attributed revenue is what the attribution rule credits to the ad; incremental revenue would not have happened without it (Chapter 6). D7 ROAS is attributed revenue in the seven days after acquisition divided by acquisition spend (defined in Chapter 3).

  1. Higher pCTR with flat ROAS. If predicted click-through rate (pCTR) and the clearing CPM both rise while D7 ROAS stays flat, the model may simply be buying more clickable traffic at a higher price. Decompose the change into price and traffic mix first.
  2. Higher publisher eCPM. Publisher eCPM (revenue per thousand impressions) can rise while advertisers gain nothing: in the miscalibration example under the same market assumptions, aggregate publisher revenue rises about 5.4% while the bidder’s profit falls.
  3. Subsidized growth. Inference (generic, not a claim about AppLovin's practices). Credits or discounts buy room for trial and error, not unbiased labels. Labels from subsidized customers, cheap traffic or retargeting (ads to users who already engaged) may not transfer once full pricing applies. Check retention after subsidies end and calibration by segment.

Takeaways

  • Axon is the model, AppLovin Ads the product that earns nearly all revenue, MAX the first-price auction where it bids. Every bidder whose bid was evaluated receives the final price in its notice (whether rejected bids, e.g. below the floor, also receive it is not documented). Revenue is reported net, so advertiser spend and take rate are not disclosed.
  • The auctioneer earns far more when its own bidder wins than from its fee on a rival's win. Neutrality is the claim to test. Loss-notice prices can flag inconsistencies among eligible real-time bids for reconciliation, but not an own bidder that sees rivals' bids before bidding and bids just above them; that needs an audit of bid-time data access.
  • In the standard design, goal values and budgets retrain nothing: the goal type picks which predictions are combined, and at fixed λ a cost goal multiplies them while a ROAS goal divides them. The labels come from what the measurement partner forwards, plus, possibly, ad revenue that AppLovin sees through MAX, and the goal shapes training data only through which impressions are won. Probabilities are trained with log loss; revenue needs a loss whose minimizer is the mean in dollars, or a fitted distribution read through its mean, since squared error on the logarithm, exponentiated naively, underbids at fixed pacing.
  • With uniform thresholds and interior bids, refinement can raise profit at a constant expected win count; miscalibration moves spend to first order and profit only to second order. Neither win rate nor publisher eCPM identifies a better model.
