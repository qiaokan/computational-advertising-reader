Comprehensive exercises and answers

Ten questions, one hundred points in total

Shared parameters for Questions 2–4: N=1,000,000 opportunities; a win means a successful impression; billable conversion probability per impression q=0.0005, $30 charged per order, network value v=q×30=0.015 dollars / impression; pre-advertising contribution per order $40; the true difference in conversion probability per successful impression relative to not showing for the same opportunity is Δq=0.0002. Z is the competitive threshold, and Z~Uniform(0,L) denotes that it is uniformly distributed between 0 and L=0.02 dollars / impression; b is the bid, and F(b)=b/L is the win rate (0≤b≤L). B denotes the media budget; unless a question imposes other restrictions, the optimization objective is always network collections minus media cost. Multiply amounts by 1,000 when converting to CPM; the $2,000 media budget is added only in Question 4.

Each question is worth 10 points. Spend 30 minutes answering independently first, then 20 minutes checking. Calculation questions are judged not only on the numbers but also on the target party, units, and assumptions. Scoring at least 80 points with no conceptual errors on Questions 2, 6, and 8 indicates that you have built a foundational framework usable for discussion and review; this is only this tutorial's acceptance criterion, not an industry certification.

Question 1 Product dimensions. An ad is shown in a game's rewarded video, bought via real-time auction; the publisher charges per impression, and the advertiser sets a target CPA of 30 dollars. Can you assert that the advertiser pays only after a conversion? Which two kinds of key contract information are missing?
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 1. No. Target CPA is an optimization constraint. The two missing kinds of information are: first, whether the advertiser side is actually billed per impression, click, or conversion, and at what price; second, the event definition of a billable conversion, the attribution window, deduplication, and refund and chargeback rules. Publisher-side billing and both parties' budgets must also be confirmed separately and cannot be inferred from the target CPA. 5 points for correctly making the distinction, 5 points for the two clearly identified kinds of missing information.

Question 2 Three values. Using the unified worked example summarized in the shared parameters above (first introduced in “A fictional campaign used throughout” in the “A one-day learning roadmap” chapter), compute the network's collection value, the advertiser's attributed contribution value, and the incremental contribution value, each expressed as eCPM. Why can't the network automatically replace its own receivables estimate with the advertiser's incremental contribution value?
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 2. The network is 15 dollars eCPM, the advertiser's attributed contribution is 20 dollars eCPM, and the incremental contribution is 8 dollars eCPM. The network's receivables are determined by billable attributed events and the contract; the advertiser's incremental contribution determines its true economic value and long-term willingness to pay, and cannot directly rewrite the contractual collection probability. 2 points for each of the three numbers, 4 points for the distinction in objectives.

Question 3 First-price bidding. With v=0.015, Z~Uniform(0,0.02) and no budget constraint, derive the optimal bid, win rate, media spend for one million requests, and network collections. Then identify one condition under which “the bid is always half the valuation” fails.
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 3. Maximize (0.015−b)b/0.02; setting the derivative to zero gives b=0.0075, i.e., 7.5 CPM; the win rate is 37.5%, media spend 2,812.50 dollars, and network collections 5,625 dollars. A non-uniform competitive threshold, a budget, floor prices, extra fees, or value correlated with the competitive threshold can all break this special case; even if the distribution is still uniform, if the lower bound is not zero, or v/2>L must be capped at L, you can no longer directly use v/2. Derivation 4 points, numbers 4 points, boundaries 2 points.

Question 4 Budget definitions. The network media budget is changed to 2,000 dollars; find the first-price optimal bid and the advertiser bill. If the advertiser says its own hard budget is also 2,000 dollars, is the original solution still executable? Optional bonus: if only the advertiser's expected bill is limited to 2,000 dollars while the original media budget is kept, find this example's homogeneous bidding solution; does this solution guarantee a hard cap on the actual bill?
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 4. b=√(2000×0.02/1000000)=0.006324555, i.e., 6.324555 CPM; expected attributed conversions are 158.113883, and the advertiser bill is 4,743.41649 dollars. If the advertiser has a separate 2,000-dollar cap, the original solution does not satisfy that constraint; in-flight and future settlements must be controlled separately on the CPA receivables side, not merely by limiting media spend. Calculation 6 points, budget owner 4 points.

Question 4 bonus: the expected bill is NvF(b)=Nvb/L; restricting it to no more than 2,000 gives b≤0.002666667 dollars, i.e., 2.666667 CPM. In this feasible range, where net profit in this example still rises with the bid, the optimum takes this upper bound; expected conversions are about 66.666667, media spend about 355.56 dollars, and network gross spread about 1,644.44 dollars. This result satisfies only the expected constraint and cannot guarantee that the actual CPA bill does not exceed 2,000; a hard cap still requires handling in-flight actions and contractual billing obligations. This bonus is not scored separately.

Question 5 Calibration and sampling. All positives are retained, and negatives are independently and randomly retained with a feature-independent probability of 10%; the probability trained without weight correction is 0.5—what is the true probability? If a new model's AUC rises but its predicted probabilities are doubled across the board, can it go live directly?
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 5. p=0.1×0.5/(1−0.5+0.1×0.5)=1/11≈0.090909. You cannot launch based on an AUC increase alone; you must check whether sampling has been corrected, segment calibration, monetary loss, label maturity, and constrained online results. Numerical value 4 points, calibration and decision logic 6 points.

Question 6 Incremental profit. Two groups of one hundred thousand people each have 1,200 and 1,000 purchases respectively; attributed purchases are 500, ad spend is 5,000 dollars, sales revenue per order after refunds and other adjustments is 50 dollars (before goods, fulfillment, and other costs), and the pre-advertising contribution rate is 40%. Compute attributed ROAS, incremental ROAS, and incremental contribution profit; explain why you cannot just compare actually exposed users with control.
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 6. Attributed ROAS is 5, incremental ROAS is 2, and incremental contribution profit is −1,000 dollars. These are point estimates and still require appropriate uncertainty analysis. Actual exposure is a selection occurring after random assignment, and filtering on it destroys the original comparability; first analyze the ITT by random eligibility assignment. Numbers 6 points, causal and statistical boundaries 4 points.

Question 7 Price feedback. In first-price, after bidding 8 dollars CPM you win and pay 8 dollars CPM. Can you use 8 as a supervised label for the competitive threshold? What bias arises in second-price if only winning prices are used for training?
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 7. No; the first-price payment is one's own bid, so you only know the competitive threshold is not above that bid, unless the interface provides a more precise signal. In second-price, looking only at winning prices omits competitive thresholds above one's own bid, causing a truncation/censoring selection problem; loss information and a correct observation model are needed. 5 points per part.

Question 8 Ledger states. A bid uses the post-display billing contract of Case A in “How delivery probability changes the bid” in the “Real-time advertising systems” chapter, and stipulates burl as the billing-confirmation signal. After the bid is sent, a win notice is received, but no billable notice yet; then a duplicate win notice arrives. Can the system confirm media spend? Can it release the entire reservation? How should it handle the billing notice later arriving in duplicate?
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 8. Under this question's contract, a win by itself cannot confirm billability, and a duplicate win cannot be counted twice; undischarged external obligations make releasing the entire reservation unsafe. The first valid billing signal converts the actual amount from reservation to spend and releases the difference from the reservation cap; duplicate signals no longer change the ledger, and late or missing events are left for reconciliation. Only when an authoritative conclusion confirms that the remaining payment obligation has been discharged may the corresponding reservation be released. State distinctions 4 points, obligation management 3 points, idempotency and reconciliation 3 points.

Question 9 Offline versus online. A new model has better offline log loss, but online network gross spread falls. Give four distinguishable diagnostic paths, and for two of them identify the logs or metrics needed.
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 9. You can check overbidding caused by probability miscalibration, changes in price or traffic mix, label delay or leakage, mismatch between feature and model versions, increased timeouts, changes in fees or attribution definitions, and budget-control oscillation. For example, check calibration with predicted/matured outcomes sliced by model and publisher; check overbidding and traffic selection with bid, win rate, clearing price, traffic composition, and cost logs. Four distinct mechanisms 6 points in total, two pieces of evidence that can actually localize the problem 4 points in total.

Question 10 Moat evidence. Someone claims: “MAX sees all bids and Adjust has all attribution data, so Axon can necessarily train on all data, and a technical replicator need only subsidize data purchases to catch up.” Identify at least four missing links in this argument, and give one validation metric better than total conversion volume.
Reference answers and scoring points

Question 10. Data being seen by a system does not mean it is permitted for training; being permitted does not mean it actually enters the pipeline; owning a measurement company does not mean owning customer data; more samples do not mean unbiased and transferable; the same model does not mean the same supply and execution conditions; short-term response prediction does not mean incremental profit; subsidies cannot prove retention after full pricing begins. Usable metrics include customer retention after subsidies are removed, matured incremental contribution under the same budget constraints, or the marginal return curve of additional budget. Four gaps 8 points, a suitable metric 2 points.
