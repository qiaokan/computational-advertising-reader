The gist. The ten questions follow the reader's main line: what one impression is worth and to whom, who pays when a predicted rate is wrong, how to bid under a budget, why the auction's winners are overpredicted, how training logs mislead a model, how to tell credited sales from caused ones, how a ledger records money exactly once, and what evidence would show a durable data advantage. Each question uses new numbers or a new angle, so it tests whether you can apply a mechanism, not whether you remember a worked example.

Ten questions, 100 points

CPA-contract parameters for Questions 1, 5 and 8. The running campaign from “A fictional campaign used throughout” (Start here), worked through in chapter 4's optional network-spread appendix. Question 3 specifies the advertiser's direct-buying contract separately; Questions 2, 4, 6 and 7 state their own numbers.

  • Opportunities. N=1{,}000{,}000 statistically identical bid opportunities (bid requests from publishers). Every auction win yields exactly one successful impression; display failures are ignored here (Real-time advertising systems relaxes this).
  • Contracts. The ad network sells to the advertiser on CPA terms: it charges a=\$30 per billable attributed conversion, a conversion that the agreed attribution rules credit to the ad and that the contract makes payable. It buys impressions from publishers in first-price auctions: the winner pays its own bid, per impression.
  • Conversion probability. Each impression produces a billable attributed conversion with probability q=0.0005, the same for every opportunity and independent of the bid and of competition. The network's expected collections per impression are v=qa=\$0.015.
  • Advertiser economics. Pre-advertising contribution per order is g=\$40: sales revenue minus goods, fulfillment and other per-order variable costs, before ad spend and fixed costs. The true causal lift is \Delta q=0.0002: for the same opportunity, showing the ad raises the probability of an order by 0.0002 compared with not showing it. Here \Delta q<q is a property of the given numbers, not a general law; attribution can both credit orders the ad did not cause and miss orders it did.
  • Competition. The competitive threshold Z is the highest competing bid for an opportunity, in dollars per impression, with Z\sim\text{Uniform}(0,L), L=\$0.02, independently across opportunities. A bid b wins when b\ge Z, so the win rate is F(b)=b/L for 0\le b\le L.
  • Objective. In the network-spread appendix, the network picks one uniform bid b to maximize expected media gross spread: expected collections from the advertiser minus expected payments to publishers. Its budget caps payments to publishers, not the advertiser's spend.
  • Units. “Spend”, “bill” and “collections” mean expected values. CPM is a price per thousand impressions; eCPM (effective CPM) expresses any expected amount per impression as an amount per thousand impressions. Dollars per impression × 1,000 = CPM.
Question 1: Three values. Using the shared parameters above, compute three values per impression, each as an eCPM (dollars per thousand impressions): (a) billed, the network's expected collections; (b) credited, the advertiser's attributed conversions valued at g each; (c) caused, the orders the ad causes valued at g each. Why can't the network simply replace its own receivables estimate with the caused value?
Reference answers and scoring points
Worked solution

Billed, credited and caused, per thousand impressions

Given: q=0.0005 billable attributed conversions per impression, a=\$30 per billable conversion, g=\$40 contribution per order, \Delta q=0.0002 caused orders per impression.

  1. (a) Billed: each impression bills a with probability q. \begin{aligned}1000\,q\,a &= 1000\times 0.0005\times 30\\ &= 1000\times\$0.015 = \$15\ \text{eCPM}\end{aligned}
  2. (b) Credited: the same attributed conversions, valued at g. \begin{aligned}1000\,q\,g &= 1000\times 0.0005\times 40\\ &= 1000\times\$0.020 = \$20\ \text{eCPM}\end{aligned}
  3. (c) Caused: only orders the ad causes count. \begin{aligned}1000\,\Delta q\,g &= 1000\times 0.0002\times 40\\ &= 1000\times\$0.008 = \$8\ \text{eCPM}\end{aligned}

Result: (a) $15 billed, (b) $20 credited, (c) $8 caused, per 1,000 impressions.

Check: (b)/(a) = g/a = 40/30 = 4/3 ✓, and (c)/(b) = \Delta q/q = 0.4 ✓. The attribution report shows the advertiser gaining $20 − $15 = $5 per thousand impressions; it actually loses $15 − $8 = $7. Equivalently its cost per caused order is qa/\Delta q = 0.015/0.0002 = \$75 against g=\$40, matching chapter 4's appendix.

Why not substitute (c) for (a). A full-marks answer makes these points:

  • Receivables are set by the contract. The network collects $30 for every billable attributed conversion, whether or not the ad caused it. Its expected revenue per impression is qa; bids priced off \Delta q\,g would misprice every auction (here they would value an impression at $8 instead of $15 eCPM).
  • The caused value is the advertiser's objective. It measures what the ads are really worth to the advertiser and so its long-run willingness to keep paying.
  • The gap is a separate risk. A $7 loss per thousand impressions is a churn risk. Track it as its own metric or constraint (an incremental-CPA or iROAS guardrail), not by rewriting the receivables forecast.

Scoring: 2 points for each number; 4 points for distinguishing the two objectives.

Question 2: Four points and who bears the risk. A game studio buys rewarded-video impressions (ads a player opts into for an in-game reward) on an oCPM product: it sets a target of $30 per purchase, is billed per 1,000 impressions, and judges the campaign on D7 ROAS (chapter 3). The platform forecasts pCTR = 0.02 clicks per impression and pCVR = 0.04 purchases per click. Every bid is set from this forecast, and, as in chapter 3's worked example, each product's expected bill at the forecast equals its eCPM bid. (a) Name the four points. Can the studio conclude that it pays only when a purchase happens? (b) The true CTR turns out to be 0.01; CVR is as forecast. Compute the cost per purchase under oCPM, and under CPC and CPA products bid from the same forecast. Who absorbs the error in each case? (c) Repeat with the true CVR at 0.02 and CTR as forecast. (d) Which risk does no pricing product move off the studio?
Reference answers and scoring points

(a) The four points (chapter 2): ranked at the impression (the auction compares eCPMs); billed at the impression; bid at the purchase ($30); judged on D7 ROAS, attributed revenue in the first 168 hours after install divided by spend. No, the studio does not pay only per purchase: the $30 is a bidding target, not a billing price, and the invoice counts impressions. To settle what is owed it would need the contract's billing basis and price, and, for any per-conversion product, the definition of a billable conversion (event, attribution window, deduplication, refunds).

Worked solution

One forecast error, three products

Given: pCTR = 0.02, pCVR = 0.04, $30 per purchase; expected bill at the forecast equals the eCPM bid.

  1. Bids. oCPM: 1000\times0.02\times0.04\times\$30=\$24 per 1,000 impressions. CPC: 0.04\times\$30=\$1.20 per click, ranked at 1000\times0.02\times\$1.20=\$24. CPA: $30 per purchase, ranked at 1000\times0.02\times0.04\times\$30=\$24.
  2. (b) True CTR 0.01: per 1,000 impressions, 1000\times0.01=10 clicks and 10\times0.04=0.4 purchases. Bills: oCPM $24; CPC 10\times\$1.20=\$12; CPA 0.4\times\$30=\$12. Cost per purchase: oCPM \$24/0.4=\$60; CPC and CPA \$12/0.4=\$30.
  3. (c) True CVR 0.02: 1000\times0.02=20 clicks and 20\times0.02=0.4 purchases. Bills: oCPM $24; CPC 20\times\$1.20=\$24; CPA 0.4\times\$30=\$12. Cost per purchase: oCPM and CPC $60; CPA $30.

Result: the CTR error costs the oCPM studio $60 per purchase but is absorbed by the platform under CPC and CPA; the CVR error costs the studio $60 under oCPM and CPC and is absorbed by the platform only under CPA.

Check against the rules of chapter 3: the segment between the ranked point and the billed point is the seller's; the segment between the billed point and the bid point is the payer's. CTR lies between impression and click: rank→bill under CPC and CPA (platform), bill→bid under oCPM (studio). CVR lies between click and purchase: rank→bill only under CPA, bill→bid under oCPM and CPC. Where the platform absorbs the error it collects $12 per 1,000 impressions instead of the $24 it ranked on.

(d) Bid→judged stays with the studio under every product: whether purchases turn into D7 revenue, and the gap between purchases credited by attribution and purchases the ad caused (chapter 1).

Scoring: (a) 3 points (four points 2, target ≠ billing 1); (b) 3 points; (c) 2 points; (d) 2 points.

Question 3: Advertiser bidding under a budget. Use the direct contract of chapter 4, not the CPA contract of Question 1: the advertiser buys impressions itself, pays exactly the auction cost with no fees, and every win displays. Let N=10^6, Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,L) with L=\$0.02 per impression, and advertiser incremental contribution w=\Delta q\,g=0.0002\times\$40=\$0.008 per impression, independent of competition and bid. The advertiser picks one bid b to maximize incremental contribution after ad spend. (a) First price, no budget: find the bid, expected impressions, ad spend and objective value, and say why this is not the $7.50 CPM network-spread bid. (b) Add an expected-spend cap of $2,000, then of $500. At $500, what is the budget multiplier \mu_A and what does it mean? (c) Ideal second price with the $2,000 cap. (d) Do these solutions guarantee a realized hard spending limit?
Reference answers and scoring points

(a) First-price cost per win is b and the win probability is F(b)=b/L, so the objective is

J(b)=\frac NL(wb-b^2)=\frac NL\left[\left(\frac w2\right)^2-\left(b-\frac w2\right)^2\right].

The square is never negative, so the maximum is at b=w/2=\$0.004 per impression, $4 CPM, within [0,L]. Expected impressions are 10^6(0.004/0.02)=200{,}000; spend is 200{,}000\times0.004=\$800; incremental contribution is 200{,}000\times0.008=\$1{,}600 before spend and $800 after. The appendix's $7.50 CPM bid uses the network's receipts v=qa=\$0.015 under CPA billing: it maximizes a different party's ledger, and substituting v for w changes the economic question.

(b) Spend is Nb^2/L, so the cap allows bids up to b_B=\sqrt{BL/N}, and the best feasible bid is b_1^*=\min\{w/2,\sqrt{BL/N},L\}.

  1. At $2,000, b_B=\sqrt{2000\times0.02/10^6}=\$0.0063246, above w/2: the budget is slack. The answer is unchanged ($4 CPM, $800 after spend), and $1,200 stays unspent; the bidder does not force spend.
  2. At $500, b_1^*=\sqrt{500\times0.02/10^6}=\$0.00316227766 per impression ($3.1623 CPM): the budget binds. Expected impressions are 158,113.88; contribution before spend is 158{,}113.88\times0.008=\$1{,}264.91; after the $500 spend the objective is $764.91, below the unconstrained $800 as it must be.
  3. The multiplier makes the advertiser bid as if each impression were worth w/(1+\mu_A): \mu_A=w/(2b_B)-1=0.008/(2\times0.0031623)-1\approx0.2649. It is the budget's marginal value: one more budget dollar adds about $0.26 of contribution after spend.

(c) In second price the winner pays Z, so b_2^*=\min\{w,\sqrt{2BL/N},L\}. At $2,000 the cap \sqrt{8\times10^{-5}}=\$0.0089443 is above w, so the advertiser bids $8 CPM, wins 400,000 impressions in expectation and spends 10^6(0.008)^2/(2\times0.02)=\$1{,}600. Incremental contribution is $3,200 before spend and $1,600 after; the budget is slack.

(d) No. All amounts are expected values computed from unrounded inputs. A realized hard cap needs reservation and settlement controls (chapter 7).

Scoring: (a) objective, bid and ledger 3 points; (b) slack and binding cases and \mu_A 3 points; (c) 2 points; (d) 2 points.

Question 4: The winner's curse. Two ads compete for one slot. Both have the same true conversion probability, q=0.0005 per impression, and both advertisers bid $30 per conversion on an oCPM product: the platform bids its predicted probability × $30 per impression, and the winner pays that bid (first price). The predictions are unbiased but noisy: each ad's prediction is q(1+0.2\,\varepsilon), with an independent standard normal \varepsilon per ad. The higher prediction wins (chapter 5). (a) Is the model biased? (b) Compute the winning ad's expected prediction relative to q. Hint: \max(x,y)=\tfrac{x+y}{2}+\tfrac{|x-y|}{2}. (c) What does the winning advertiser pay per conversion on average? (d) Why does an offline calibration check, on logs an older model selected or on randomized traffic, not reveal this, and what are two fixes? (e) Without calculating, what changes with ten competing ads?
Reference answers and scoring points

(a) No. Each prediction has mean q, so averaged over all scored ads the model is calibrated. The bias comes from which prediction gets acted on: the auction selects the larger one.

Worked solution

Expected maximum of two noisy predictions

Given: \varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2 independent standard normal; the winner's prediction is q(1+0.2M) with M=\max(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2).

  1. Split the maximum: M=\tfrac{\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2}{2}+\tfrac{|\varepsilon_1-\varepsilon_2|}{2}. The first term has mean 0.
  2. \varepsilon_1-\varepsilon_2 is normal with mean 0 and variance 2. For X\sim N(0,\sigma^2), by symmetry and the substitution u=x^2/(2\sigma^2): \begin{aligned}\mathbb E|X|&=2\int_0^\infty x\,\frac{e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dx\\ &=\frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_0^\infty e^{-u}\,du=\sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}.\end{aligned} With \sigma=\sqrt2, \mathbb E|\varepsilon_1-\varepsilon_2|=2/\sqrt\pi.
  3. So \mathbb E[M]=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{2}{\sqrt\pi}=1/\sqrt\pi\approx0.5642, and the winner's expected prediction is q\,(1+0.2\times0.5642)=1.1128\,q.
  4. (c) Payment: the winner pays $30 × its prediction per impression, on average \$30\times1.1128\times0.0005=\$0.016693 ($16.69 CPM), against a true value of \$30\times0.0005=\$0.015. Its true conversion probability is still q (both ads are equally good), so its cost per conversion is 0.016693/0.0005=\$33.39.

Result: the winning prediction averages 11.3% above the truth, and the advertiser pays about $33.39 per conversion against its $30 target.

Check: numerical integration of \int m\cdot2\varphi(m)\Phi(m)\,dm (the density of the larger of two standard normals is 2\varphi\Phi) gives 0.5642, matching 1/\sqrt\pi; and \$33.39/\$30=1.1128, the same 11.3%.

(d) In those logs the new model's errors did not choose which impressions were bought, so its over- and underpredictions cancel; online, the auction keeps the overpredictions, so the bias shows up only on traffic the new model itself wins. Fixes: measure calibration on impressions the current model won; shrink predictions toward a prior, more strongly where they are noisier (new ads, little data).

(e) The bias grows with the number of competitors: the expected maximum of ten standard normals is about 1.5388, so the winner is overpredicted by about 31% (chapter 5). Under oCPM the advertiser absorbs the overrun; under a cost guarantee or CPA billing the platform does (chapter 3).

Scoring: (a) 1 point; (b) 4 points; (c) 2 points; (d) 2 points; (e) 1 point.

Question 5: Calibration and sampling. A conversion model is trained on downsampled data: every positive example is kept, and each negative is kept independently with probability 10%, regardless of its features (Prediction and the data it learns from). Training applies no weight correction, and the model is calibrated on the sampled distribution. For some feature vector it outputs 0.5. What is the true conversion probability? Separately: a new model has a higher AUC than the current one, but its predicted probabilities are about twice the observed conversion rates across the board. Can it go live as is?
Reference answers and scoring points
Worked solution

Undoing negative downsampling

Given: negative retention rate s=0.1, positives all kept, sampled-data prediction p_s=0.5 (calibrated on the sampled data). Want: true probability p for the same feature vector.

  1. For a fixed feature vector, sampling keeps a fraction 1 of positives and s of negatives, so it multiplies the odds by 1/s: \frac{p_s}{1-p_s}=\frac{p}{(1-p)\,s}.
  2. True odds: s\cdot\frac{p_s}{1-p_s}=0.1\times\frac{0.5}{0.5}=0.1.
  3. Back to a probability: p=\frac{0.1}{1+0.1}=\frac{1}{11}\approx 0.0909.

Result: p = 1/11 ≈ 0.0909 (9.09%).

Check: the closed form p=\frac{s\,p_s}{1-p_s+s\,p_s}=\frac{0.05}{0.5+0.05}=0.0909 ✓; at s=1 it returns p=p_s, as it must. Using the raw 0.5 would overstate value 5.5-fold here, and about 1/s-fold when p is small.

The higher-AUC model cannot go live as is. A full-marks answer contains:

  • AUC is blind to calibration. It depends only on ranking and is unchanged by any strictly increasing transformation of the scores.
  • Bids use absolute probabilities. The network's expected receipts per impression are v=30q, so doubling q doubles the value the bidder thinks it buys. In the shared example without a budget, the first-price bid rises from v/2 = $7.50 CPM to (2v)/2=\$0.015 per impression = $15 CPM, where the true spread (0.015-b)\,b/L is zero: the whole $2,812.50 of spread per million requests (chapter 4's appendix) is lost.
  • Launch checklist. Confirm the sampling correction was applied exactly once; recalibrate on matured labels; check calibration by segment (a uniform factor of 2 rarely holds everywhere); evaluate monetary loss, not ranking metrics; then run a limited online experiment.

Second-order point: under a binding budget a pacing multiplier would absorb a perfectly uniform factor for one campaign (the appendix's $6.3246 CPM network bid is set by its binding $2,000 media budget, not by v), but across campaigns and segments with different errors it misallocates spend. Scoring: number 4 points; calibration and launch logic 6 points.

Question 6: Price feedback. In a first-price auction you bid $8 CPM, win, and pay $8 CPM. Can you use $8 as a supervised label for that auction's competitive threshold Z (Learning competing prices from censored auction feedback)? In a second-price auction, where the winner pays Z, what bias results if you fit a model of Z only on the prices you paid in auctions you won, and how do you fix it?
Reference answers and scoring points

First price: no. You pay your own bid, so the price carries no information about Z beyond the win itself. Full-marks points:

  • A win says only Z\le 8 (CPM): a censored observation bounding Z from above, not a label. A loss says only Z>b.
  • Win/loss records are still informative: their likelihood pins down the win probability at the bids actually placed, so learning the whole curve needs varied bids (or a parametric form).
  • You learn more only if the exchange returns an extra signal, such as the minimum bid that would have won.

Second price: downward bias. Full-marks points:

  • A win reveals Z exactly, but only when Z\le b; training on wins alone drops every auction with Z>b, truncating the sample from above.
  • The fitted Z is biased low: it underestimates the threshold and overestimates the win rate at every bid. The bias is worst on contested, high-value inventory, where you lose most often.
  • Fix: keep losses as censored observations at b and maximize the censored likelihood, in which each win contributes the density f(Z) and each loss 1-F(b).
Illustration

Wins-only versus censored estimate

Given (for illustration, not stated in the question): Z\sim\text{Uniform}(0,\$20\ \text{CPM}), the shared example's L, and you always bid $8 CPM in a second-price auction.

  1. Win rate: F(8)=8/20=40\%. The other 60% of auctions produce no price.
  2. Prices seen on wins: Z\mid Z\le 8\sim\text{Uniform}(0,8), mean $4 CPM. A model fitted to them puts all its mass below $8 and predicts a 100% win rate at an $8 bid; the truth is 40%.
  3. Censored estimate: for a uniform threshold the censored likelihood is maximized at \hat L=b/\text{win rate}=\$8/0.40=\$20 CPM, so the estimated mean is \hat L/2=\$10 CPM, the truth.

Result: wins only: mean Z of $4 CPM; censored likelihood: $10 CPM, the true value.

Check: in general the wins-only mean is b/2, which reaches the true L/2 only at b=L, when you win everything and nothing is censored ✓. The censored estimate uses only the win and loss counts, as chapter 5 derives.

Scoring: 5 points per part.

Question 7: Incremental profit. An eligible population is randomly split into a treatment group (the campaign may deliver) and a holdout control group (it may not), 100,000 users each (chapter 6). Over the same matured window, treatment has 1,350 purchases and control 1,000. The attribution system credits 700 of the treatment purchases to the ad. Ad spend is $5,000. Net revenue per purchase is $50 (after refunds, before goods, fulfillment and other costs), and the pre-advertising contribution rate is 40% of net revenue. Assume correct randomization and event definitions, no interference between the groups, and that each user buys at most once, independently. Compute attributed ROAS (attributed revenue ÷ ad spend), incremental ROAS (iROAS: incremental revenue ÷ ad spend), incremental contribution profit (incremental contribution − ad spend) and a 95% interval for that profit. Is the campaign profitable? Then explain why you cannot just compare the treatment users who actually saw the ad with the control group.
Reference answers and scoring points
Worked solution

Attributed versus incremental returns

Given: 100,000 users per group; purchases 1,350 (treatment) and 1,000 (control); 700 attributed purchases; ad spend $5,000; net revenue $50 per purchase; contribution rate 40%.

  1. Incremental purchases (equal groups, so compare counts directly): 1{,}350-1{,}000=350.
  2. Attributed ROAS: \frac{700\times\$50}{\$5{,}000}=\frac{\$35{,}000}{\$5{,}000}=7.0
  3. iROAS: \frac{350\times\$50}{\$5{,}000}=\frac{\$17{,}500}{\$5{,}000}=3.5
  4. Incremental contribution profit: contribution per purchase \$50\times0.4=\$20, so \begin{aligned}&350\times\$20-\$5{,}000\\ &=\$7{,}000-\$5{,}000\\ &=+\$2{,}000.\end{aligned}
  5. Break-even: iROAS 1/0.4=2.5, i.e. \$5{,}000/\$20=250 incremental purchases.
  6. Standard error of the lift: each group's count is binomial with variance np(1-p), and independent variances add. \begin{aligned}\text{SE}^2&=1350(1-0.0135)\\ &\quad+1000(1-0.010)\\ &=1331.775+990=2321.775,\\ \text{SE}&=\sqrt{2321.775}\approx 48.18.\end{aligned}
  7. 95% interval for the lift: 350\pm1.96\times48.18=350\pm94.4, i.e. [255.6,\ 444.4] purchases. Profit is \$20\times\text{lift}-\$5{,}000, so it lies in [+\$111,\ +\$3{,}889].

Result: attributed ROAS 7.0, iROAS 3.5, incremental contribution profit +$2,000, 95% interval about +$111 to +$3,889. The lower end of the lift interval, 255.6, clears the break-even 250, so the campaign is profitable at the 95% level, though only just.

Check: attributed ROAS ÷ iROAS = 7.0/3.5 = 2 = 700/350 ✓: attribution credits twice the measured lift, so at least 350 credited purchases would have happened anyway (if the ad never suppresses a purchase). Profit also equals \$5{,}000\times(0.4\times3.5-1)=\$2{,}000 ✓.

Why not compare exposed treatment users with control. Full-marks points (chapter 6):

  • Exposure is decided after randomization, by targeting, auction wins and how active each user is, all of which correlate with the propensity to buy.
  • Exposed treatment users and the whole control group are therefore not comparable: the comparison reintroduces selection bias, typically inflating the effect.
  • Analyze the ITT (intent to treat: the effect by original random assignment) first.
  • For the effect per exposed user, divide the ITT by the treatment group's exposure rate (the Wald ratio, valid if control users cannot be exposed and assignment acts only through exposure), or use ghost ads that record which control users would have been exposed.

Scoring: numbers and interval 6 points; causal and statistical caveats 4 points.

Question 9: Ledger states. A bid is placed under display-based billing, where only a confirmed display incurs a charge (Real-time advertising systems): the publisher is paid only after a successful impression, and the contract names the OpenRTB billing notice burl as the signal that confirms billing. Before the bid was sent, the budget system reserved the bid's maximum possible cost. After the bid, a win notice (nurl) arrives but no billing notice; then a duplicate of the same win notice arrives. Can the system book this as confirmed media spend? Can it release the whole reservation? How should it handle the billing notice when it later arrives twice?
Reference answers and scoring points
  • Confirm spend? No. Under this contract a win creates no payment obligation; only a successful impression does, and burl is what confirms it. The duplicate nurl is deduplicated on the bid's business key (exchange, request, impression and bid IDs, plus the event type) and changes nothing.
  • Release the whole reservation? No. The impression may still render and burl may arrive late, so the external obligation is still open. Releasing the reservation would let the same budget be spent twice. Release it only on authoritative evidence that the obligation is gone: a confirmed loss or cancellation, a reconciliation result, or a binding contractual cutoff. A local timeout is not such evidence.
  • Billing notice twice. The first valid burl moves the actual billed amount from reserved to confirmed spend and releases the difference between the reservation cap and that amount. The duplicate is an idempotent no-op on the ledger, logged for reconciliation. Late, missing or mismatched notices go to a pending-reconciliation state and are settled against the exchange's settlement reports. An amount above the cap means the cost-cap premise has broken: send it to exception handling.

Question 10: Evidence for a moat. MAX is AppLovin's mediation product, which runs publishers' auctions across demand sources; Adjust is an MMP (mobile measurement partner) that AppLovin owns, which attributes conversions on behalf of advertisers; Axon is its prediction and bidding engine (AppLovin and Axon). Someone claims: “MAX sees all bids and Adjust has all the attribution data, so Axon necessarily trains on all of it, and a competitor that can replicate the technology only needs to subsidize data purchases to catch up.” Identify at least four missing links in this argument, and propose one validation metric better than total conversion volume.
Reference answers and scoring points

Missing links (any four, each stated as the specific step that fails):

  1. Seeing is not permission. MAX receives bids to run auctions; contracts and privacy rules govern any other use. MAX also sees only the auctions it mediates, not all bids.
  2. Permission is not use. Data allowed for training does not necessarily enter the training pipeline. AppLovin's own description says Axon does not ingest other bidders' bid information.
  3. Processing is not ownership. Adjust processes attribution data on advertisers' behalf; the 10-K says Adjust's data is not shared with AppLovin or used to optimize its recommendation engine unless a customer directs it.
  4. More data is not unbiased or transferable data. Samples collected under one delivery policy and supply mix are selection-biased for other policies.
  5. Same model, different access. Replicating the technology does not replicate supply access and execution conditions.
  6. Prediction is not incrementality. Predicting short-term responses well is not delivering incremental profit to advertisers.
  7. Subsidies prove nothing about retention. They cannot show that advertisers stay once full pricing starts; and some of the data is not for sale at any price.

Better metrics (any one, with why it beats volume): advertiser retention and spend after subsidies end; matured incremental contribution from holdout tests under the same budget constraint; or the marginal-return curve of additional budget. Each measures value delivered to the advertiser rather than activity. Scoring: four gaps 8 points; a suitable metric 2 points.
